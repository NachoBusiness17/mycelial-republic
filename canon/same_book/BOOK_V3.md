The frost held the stones of the upper courtyard until the middle of the second month. Miriam sat at the oak table beside the hearth, sharpening her slate pencil with a flat hone, when three new couriers from the southern precinct rode through the iron gate. They wore heavy wool coats stiffened with salt, and each carried an iron lever wrapped in burlap. They had come to regulate the mountain mill-race, claiming the winter ice had warped the town sluice.

"The head of water belongs to the valley," the first courier said, setting his iron bar upon the table. "We have come to ease the bronze gate so the wheel may turn on a half-draft."

Miriam looked from the iron bar to the hearth. "The gate has no half-draft," she said. "The brook is either barred or it runs. If you bring a lever to coax the bronze between two notches, you will bend the hinge and drown the lower storehouse."

She rose and led the three men out through the postern gate to the stone race where the torrent fell from the cliff. The bronze sluice hung between two uprights of granite, held by a counter-weight of lead that rested upon an iron trigger. Below the gate, the channel was bone dry; above it, the mountain water pressed eight cubits deep against the plate.

The second courier took his bar and pressed against the bronze lip, trying to lift the plate by the breadth of a thumb. The metal did not groan, nor did a drop of foam escape between the granite grooves. The counter-weight held the plate fast as an anvil.

"The stone will not budge for half-hearted pressure," Miriam told them, pointing to the notched balance beam above their heads. "The maker set the counter-stone so that no gentle push can crack the seal. If you apply twenty pounds, nothing moves. If you apply forty pounds, nothing moves. But when the balance weight touches fifty stones, the trigger drops, the lead falls, and the gate opens with the full weight of the river. The house does not dither with trickles. It acts with all its strength, or it stays shut."

The couriers lowered their bars, seeing that the bronze knew only two states: the absolute dam, and the open torrent.

They turned back toward the lower cellars, crossing through the gallery of bells beneath the eastern terrace. Here, thirty-six copper bells hung from long fir joists, balanced so delicately that a heavy step upon the floor would set the tongues to chattering. The couriers walked abreast, their boots striking the flagstones together with a heavy, marching crunch. At the third pace, the joists began to groan; the copper bells clashed wildly, and the floor vibrated with a rising shudder that shook mortar dust from the ceiling.

Miriam halted at the threshold and held up her hand. "If you walk with the same foot at the same second," she said, "the house will shake itself to pieces beneath you. The resonance multiplies your weight until the timber splits."

"How can three men pass a narrow gallery without stepping upon the stone?" the third courier asked, resting his back against the shaking wall.

"Listen to the echo in the stone," Miriam answered. "Do not strike the floor together. The first man takes the down-beat when the water drips. The second man takes the half-beat when the draft turns. The third man takes the quarter-beat when the bell hums. Interleave your strides so that no two heels touch the rock in the same instant. When your steps divide the circle, the floor will not feel your passage."

The three men listened to the drip of the dark water and separated their stride, one following the other across the four intervals of the measure. The bells ceased their shivering. The joists settled back into the stone sockets, and the floor bore their crossing without a murmur.

At the far end of the terrace lay the eastern timber wing, built forty years before to store hemp and cordage. During the rainy autumn, a leak in the roof valley had admitted the black damp, and a white fungus had spread across the center joist, eating the heartwood into sponge. A local builder stood in the doorway with an iron pot of pitch, preparing to paint the rotten beam black so the timber would appear sound.

"The magistrate must see a whole house," the builder muttered, dipping his brush into the pitch. "If he sees the rot, he will order the entire hall pulled down."

Miriam took the brush from his hand and tossed it into the gravel. "You paint a corpse to look like a sleeper," she said. "The black pitch hides the decay from the eye, but the spore continues eating beneath the coat. When the winter snow falls on the roof, the hidden rot will collapse the gallery and drag the main stone wall into the chasm."

"Then the entire house is doomed," the builder cried. "If we cannot hide the rot, we must burn the whole wing to the soil."

"We do not burn the house to kill a rotted joist," Miriam replied.

She sent for the two-man crosscut saw and two iron wedges. Stepping onto the sound masonry of the central arch, she directed the cut through the mortise ten cubits back from the fungus, severing the rotted gallery cleanly from the stone house. With a final blow of the sledge against the wedge, the dead timber parted from the wall and tumbled into the gorge below, shattering upon the boulders. The granite foundation remained unyielding, the clean face of the stone exposed to the wind and the sun.

"The house is smaller by three bays," Miriam said, inspecting the cut stone, "but what remains is living rock. We do not rewrite the ledger to pretend the timber never failed, and we do not poison the foundation to spare a broken beam. Cut the rot free at the joint, and the mountain carries the rest."

As they returned to the warm room, the first courier opened his slate and took up his pencil. "We have recorded the sluice, the bells, and the fallen wing," he said smoothly. "Now tell us what time the great clock struck when the water rose, and where the village blacksmith left the iron hoops for the sluice gate."

Miriam did not reach for her ledger. She looked the courier in the eye and spoke plainly:

"The house has never kept a clock upon its towers; the sun upon the stone lintel is our only dial, and no pendulum has ever swung within these walls. Furthermore, there is no blacksmith in this valley, and there has never been a forge upon this land; the iron was carted from the foundry across the lake sixty years ago. The house answers for every stone it laid and every drop it held, but it will not answer questions built upon ghosts and empty air."

The courier looked at his clean slate, then erased the lines he had begun to draw. He closed his pouch, bowed to the woman with the seal, and followed his companions out into the quiet cold.
