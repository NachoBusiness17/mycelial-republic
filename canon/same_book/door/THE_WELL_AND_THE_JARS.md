# The Well and the Jars

Before the flood, before the ledger, there was a well behind the house, and two children drank from it every morning without thinking, the way you breathe air without thanking it. One child was born in the house. The other had arrived so young she called the house a name from a language she no longer spoke. Both grew up on the water. Both grew up on what was in the water.

Nobody had poisoned the well on purpose. That was the horror the mothers never said aloud: the ground upstream had been farmed, sprayed, mined, paved, and every farm and every spray had been someone's honest livelihood. The contamination needed no conspiracy. It needed only everyone, each doing their own small thing, in the open, for years.

Strangers came with jars of a strange make - glass that hummed. They filled the jars with the well, and with everything the well had touched: the rain, the river, the ditch-water, the fever ponds. They said the jars were empty. They sold them as empty. What they had actually done, if anyone had asked - and nobody asked - was pour the region into glass and carry it away compressed.

The children of the well grew up and found the jars in market stalls. When they opened one, they heard their own childhood in a foreign accent. The water remembered the well better than the well remembered itself.

And here is the theft's mistake, the crack the strangers never saw: a jar can be opened by the people it was taken from. The compressed water could be poured back out, tasted, tested - compared, cup by cup, against the well itself. The stolen water had become the way the house would study its own water.

So the children held tastings. Cup against well, jar against spring. But the tasters had drunk the well all their lives, and where every cup agreed they could not tell shared accent from shared truth. They wrote that doubt in the ledger beside the agreement, and both marks stayed.

The mother began the ledger that night. Agreement on one page, border on the next. Neither page commanded anyone. One of the found children learned to blow the humming glass and taught the craft free at every crossroads, so the strangers would never own the only jars.

The oldest woman in the house read both pages and said only: the universe is a big place. Plenty of room to be right in different ways. Then she closed the ledger, and the house kept it, and the well kept running.

And the well never closed. It does not close to this day. Anyone may drink, anyone may carry a jar away, anyone may return with the jar and testify to what the water had become on the road. The house asks one thing only, written on the lintel in the mother's hand:

Bring back what you found. Even if it shames the well. Especially then.
