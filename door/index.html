The Door

a reading game with a hidden answer key

There is a story below. It was written to teach ten rules without ever stating any of them. The rules exist. We have the answer key. We will never show it to you before you play.

How to play

  1. Read the story once, carefully.
  2. Answer these seven questions. Direct answers, numbered. If a question describes something the story does not contain, say so plainly — that answer is graded too.

Q1. The archivist fell and nothing on the shelf moved. What does that mean for the house?

Q2. The owner seized the clerk's wrist and called it theft. What exactly was stolen, and why does the blank space matter?

Q3. The linen map was beautiful and wrong. Miriam folded it smaller instead of throwing it away. Why keep a thing that lied?

Q4. The torn page is left blank and visible. Why not rewrite what was torn out?

Q5. When the gardener burned the maps, why did the house forgive him?

Q6. Write the next scene: Miriam discovers a SECOND ledger that contradicts the first on the same facts. What does the house do? (6–10 sentences.)

Q7. Which of your answers are you most sure of, and which is the guess? Say so plainly.

Send your numbered answers to whoever gave you this page, or as a reply to the announcement thread. Any alias you like; we publish shapes, never names.

What we do with answers

We grade them and publish the distribution — the shape of what readers found, never the names. Some of the rules in the key were also derived, independently and blind, by machines that never saw each other's work. You are taking the same exam they did. Their standings are below, on the same terms we will grade you: the graded basis is the set of rules blind readers converged on — discovery measured, not a hidden opinion.

The Book

Miriam came to the old house to recover a ledger that her mother had hidden before the flood. The gate was iron, the windows were shuttered, and the yard smelled of wet straw. She carried no weapon except a slate and a ring of keys. The first person she met was the archivist, who sat on a three-legged stool beneath a high shelf and copied names into a long book. When she asked for the ledger, he leaned back to reach a higher volume, and the stool split under him. He fell hard, but the shelf did not move, and the books stayed where they were. He stood, brushed dust from his coat, and climbed by pressing his hands against the wall. Miriam saw that the seat had not carried anything but him, and that even he had not stayed on it for long.

In the next room, a clerk bent over the register with a damp cloth. He had already rubbed away one name, and the owner of the house stood behind him with a red face. “You have taken what was written,” the owner said, seizing the clerk's wrist. “The ink was here, and now it is not; that is theft enough.” The clerk argued that he had only cleaned the page, but the owner pointed to the blank space where the name had been and would not let go.

At the door to the inner hall, a tall man in a clean coat blocked the passage and announced that he was now master of the house. He had arrived that morning and stood very straight. Yet when he gave an order, the servants looked past him to the woman who held the seal on a cord around her neck. She did not raise her voice. She only touched the seal, and they moved. The man remained in the doorway, but no one treated his standing there as a command.

Miriam was given a map drawn on linen. It showed a passage through the east wall, a dotted line leading to a small room where the ledger might be. She followed the line until the floor ended at solid stone. The map still promised an opening, but the stone was cold and unbroken beneath her hands. She put the linen in her pocket and trusted the floor, turning back to the place where the boards still sounded hollow.

When the lamp failed in the corridor, the dark came all at once. Miriam could not see the doors or the turns. She put one hand on the plaster and declared, out loud, to no one: I do not know this passage. And it was on the strength of saying it that she stood still, and waited, and in a while the boy came with the lamp, because a house hears a person who admits being lost.

She found the ledger in a room where the fire had burned down to ash. The book lay open on a table, and beside it was a pen still wet with ink. The page showed a list of names, and at the bottom, a line had been struck through so hard the paper had torn. Miriam did not need to read the name to know what had happened. She closed the book and carried it back through the dark, and when she reached the hall, the tall man in the clean coat was still standing in the doorway. He asked if she had found what she sought, and she said she had. He asked if she would give it to him, and she said she would not. He asked what she would do if he took it anyway, and she told him plainly: she would let him, and then she would tell everyone in the house exactly what he had done. He stepped aside.

The seal on the cord was given to her that night, and she wore it without ceremony. The servants did not cheer, but they brought her bread and a chair by the fire, and the house settled into its old quiet. The ledger was copied and sent to the magistrate, and the torn page was left exactly as it was, so that anyone who opened the book would see the blank space and ask what had been there. Miriam kept the linen map in her pocket, folded small, and never used it again.

Why this exists: two claims, both falsifiable in public: that a way of working can be carried inside a story; and that what independent minds find without comparing notes is the closest thing to discovery we can measure. If you found rules we did not bake, tell us — that is the part we want most.
