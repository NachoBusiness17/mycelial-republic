Introduction

When courts construe the meaning of constitutional provisions, they typically employ several approaches to constitutional interpretation (also referred to as constitutional construction). While questions related to the interpretation of specific constitutional provisions are best answered through a close reading of the text of the Constitution itself and the historical context in which those provisions were adopted, the Supreme Court has not adopted a single fixed mode of constitutional interpretation. Instead, the Court has, over time, employed several modes of constitutional interpretation, including but not limited to (1) textualism; (2) original meaning or original intent (also referred to as originalism); (3) adherence to judicial precedent (also referred to as stare decisis); (4) pragmatism; (5) moral reasoning; (6) national identity; (7) structuralism; and (8) historical practices of the political branches.

This report describes each of these modes of constitutional interpretation and discusses the principal arguments made by supporters and critics of each mode. Where possible, the report identifies Supreme Court cases that exemplify the use of each mode. As will be seen, judicial employment of these modes of constitutional interpretation does not occur in isolation; a single opinion may employ several different modes of constitutional interpretation when analyzing a particular constitutional question. For example, a judicial opinion construing a particular constitutional provision might examine the provision's text, consider the historical practices of the political branches, and apply precedent from prior Supreme Court cases addressing the provision. The report also describes several of the principal debates surrounding the various modes of constitutional interpretation, including debates between proponents of originalism and proponents of a "living Constitution."

Textualism

Textualism, frequently referred to as "plain meaning," is the view that courts should construe constitutional provisions based on the meaning of the words used in the Constitution at the time of adoption (or, for some textualists, the current plain meaning of the words). Under this approach, if the language of a constitutional provision is precise and clear, courts must apply the provision according to its terms, and judicial inquiry into the Constitution's meaning ends. Judges and scholars who support textualism argue that courts should not consult extrinsic sources of constitutional meaning, such as the intent of the Constitution's drafters, unless the constitutional text is ambiguous or otherwise unclear.

Supporters of textualism advance several arguments for their approach. First, textualists contend that judicial interpretation of the Constitution should be grounded in the actual words that were ratified, rather than unexpressed understandings or intentions of the document's framers. Second, textualism arguably constrains judicial discretion because it ties judicial decision-making to authoritative constitutional texts. Third, textualism promotes the rule of law values of predictability and stability because courts applying textualism reach results based on clear, discernible standards.

Critics of textualism, however, argue that the approach is often indeterminate because many words in the Constitution are imprecise and subject to multiple plausible interpretations. For instance, the Eighth Amendment's proscription of "cruel and unusual punishment," the Due Process Clauses' guarantee of "liberty," and the First Amendment's protections of "freedom of speech" are commonly cited provisions whose terms arguably do not have a single, readily discernible meaning. Critics also contend that textualism may be under-inclusive because a strict adherence to text might ignore the Constitution's broader purposes and principles.

Original Meaning

The original meaning mode of constitutional interpretation (also referred to as "originalism") holds that courts should construe constitutional provisions in accordance with the meaning that the provisions had at the time they were adopted. Originalists typically argue that the Constitution's meaning was fixed at the time each provision was ratified, and that judicial inquiry should center on the meaning of the words to those who ratified the provision at issue. Some originalists, however, focus instead on the original intent of the Constitution's drafters, rather than the original understanding of the ratifiers.

Advocates of originalism argue that the approach constrains judges from injecting their own subjective policy preferences into constitutional analysis. Because the Constitution's meaning is fixed at the time of adoption, originalists contend that judicial decisions construing the Constitution are legitimate only if they are grounded in the document's original meaning, which can be objectively determined through historical analysis. Originalists also argue that the approach promotes the rule of law values of predictability and stability because judges are bound by fixed meanings rather than evolving social values.

Critics of originalism make several objections. Some argue that the historical record needed to determine original meaning is often incomplete or ambiguous, giving judges considerable room to reach their preferred results while claiming to be faithful to the original meaning. Others object that originalism can produce unacceptable results in modern circumstances that the Constitution's framers could not have anticipated. Finally, critics note that originalism's legitimacy depends on the contested premise that the Constitution's meaning was fixed at the time of adoption, rather than evolving over time.

Theories of Constitutional Interpretation and Judicial Review: The Debate Between Originalism and Living Constitutionalism

One of the principal debates over modes of constitutional interpretation is between proponents of originalism and proponents of the so-called "living Constitution." Living constitutionalism is less a single mode of interpretation than a theory of constitutional change, under which the Constitution's meaning evolves over time through judicial interpretation in response to changing social conditions and values. Proponents of living constitutionalism contend that a static Constitution, interpreted according to its original meaning, is incapable of addressing modern problems that the Constitution's framers could not have foreseen. Advocates also argue that judicial updates to the Constitution's meaning are consistent with the Constitution's broad principles, such as liberty and equality, and preserve the document's legitimacy in changing times.

Critics of living constitutionalism, including most originalists, argue that the approach gives judges unbounded discretion to reshape the Constitution according to their personal policy preferences, undermining democratic self-governance and the rule of law. Originalists contend that the proper method of constitutional change is the amendment process outlined in Article V of the Constitution, not judicial innovation.

The debate between originalism and living constitutionalism has been a central feature of constitutional theory for decades, and the Supreme Court's jurisprudence contains elements of both approaches. Justice Antonin Scalia, a prominent proponent of originalism, famously debated the approach with Judge Richard A. Posner and others who favor a more adaptive mode of constitutional interpretation. (For a discussion of the Amendment procedures contained in Article V, see the "amendment" provisions of the Constitution, which allow changes to be proposed by two-thirds of each house of Congress or by a national convention, and ratified by three-fourths of the states.)

Judicial Precedent

The judicial precedent mode of interpretation (also referred to as adherence to stare decisis) requires courts to follow prior judicial decisions construing constitutional provisions. Under this approach, the Supreme Court's prior interpretations of the Constitution bind lower courts and, absent special justifications for overruling prior precedent, bind the Court itself.

Supporters of adherence to judicial precedent argue that stare decisis promotes stability, predictability, and even-handedness in the law, and conserves judicial resources by resolving questions that have already been decided. Critics argue that rigid adherence to precedent can perpetuate erroneous constitutional interpretations and that the Constitution itself—not the Court's prior constructions—is the supreme law of the land.

Pragmatism

The pragmatist mode of constitutional interpretation contends that courts should construe constitutional provisions in light of the practical consequences of alternative interpretations. Pragmatist judges weigh the real-world effects of their rulings and consider whether a particular interpretation advances or undermines contemporary notions of sound public policy.

Supporters of pragmatism argue that the approach allows the Constitution to remain relevant and responsive to contemporary circumstances. Critics contend that pragmatism is indeterminate and allows judges to decide cases based on their own policy views rather than the Constitution's commands.

Moral Reasoning

Under the moral reasoning mode of interpretation, courts construe constitutional provisions in light of fundamental moral principles, such as fairness, justice, and human dignity. The approach is associated with the view that the Constitution embodies general moral principles that courts must apply to particular circumstances.

Supporters argue that moral reasoning is necessary because the Constitution's provisions are written in broad, principled terms. Critics respond that moral reasoning licenses judges to enforce their own moral views rather than the law, citing the natural-law reasoning of cases such as Lochner v. New York as a cautionary example.

National Identity

The national identity mode of constitutional interpretation holds that courts should interpret the Constitution in light of the character and identity of the American nation as a whole. Under this approach, constitutional provisions may be construed to preserve the values and commitments that define the national community, such as equality and democratic self-government.

Structuralism

Structuralism refers to the interpretation of the Constitution based on the relationships among the various structures that the Constitution establishes—the separation of powers among the three branches of the federal government, and the division of authority between the federal government and the states. Under this approach, particular constitutional provisions are construed in light of the overall constitutional structure, including principles such as checks and balances and federalism that may not be stated in any single provision but arise from the Constitution's architecture.

Historical Practices

The historical practices mode of constitutional interpretation gives weight to the longstanding historical practices of the political branches—the practices of Congress and the President—as evidence of constitutional meaning. Under this approach, a longstanding and unchallenged practice of the political branches may inform a court's understanding of the constitutional allocation of powers, particularly in the area of separation of powers and foreign affairs.
