# THE FENCE — draft 1 (Nacho voice, concrete situation, parable-form)

The fence between Tom Hartley's place and the Dubois farm had been there since
before either of them was born. Gray boards, silvered by weather, the kind of
fence you stop seeing because it's always been there. Tom painted his side
white every spring. The Dubois side stayed gray. Neither of them remembered
who started that tradition. Neither of them questioned the line.

Tom grew tomatoes near the fence. Had for twelve years. The soil was good
there — something about the runoff from the Dubois slope. He'd won a ribbon at
the county fair three times. People knew Tom Hartley's tomatoes.

Then the county sent a letter.

The letter said the fence was wrong. Had always been wrong. A survey from 1987
— before Tom bought the place — placed the line three feet to the east. The
new survey, done last spring by a firm nobody had heard of, placed it four feet
to the east. Tom's tomatoes were on Dubois land. His well was on Dubois land.
The driveway he'd paved was on Dubois land.

Tom called the county. The clerk was polite. She said the 1987 survey was the
legal record and the new survey corrected an error in the 1987 survey, which
had itself corrected an error in an older survey, and the chain went back
further than anyone could pull. She said the fence had never been in the right
place. Tom had just been lucky.

Tom asked to see the 1987 survey. The clerk said it was archived. Tom asked to
see the new survey. The clerk said it was proprietary — the survey firm owned
the methodology.

Tom drove to the county seat and pulled the oldest map he could find. 1954.
The fence was on it. Right where he'd built his tomatoes. He drove to the
historical society. A map from 1921. Same fence. Same place.

He brought both maps to the county clerk. She looked at them and said: those
aren't the legal maps. The legal map is the current map. The current map is
the one that matters.

Tom said: but the fence was there before the current map.

The clerk said: and now the current map is there before the fence.

Tom stood in the clerk's office holding two maps that agreed with each other
and with his fence and with his memory, and none of them mattered, because the
law had chosen a different map — one drawn by a firm nobody had heard of, one
that no one in the county had seen, one that made Tom's tomatoes into
someone else's tomatoes and Tom's memory into a lucky accident and Tom's
ribbon-winning soil into a thing he would have to stop touching.

He went home. He left the tomatoes. He wasn't stealing. He'd grown them for
twelve years and three county fairs and he was going to finish the season.

The letter came the next week. Trespass notice. The new survey firm had
already sent a crew to mark the new line. They worked fast. White stakes in
the ground where the old fence used to stand. Tom's garden was now on a
plat map labeled: RESERVED — PENDING DEVELOPMENT.

Tom called the Dubois farm. Old Man Dubois answered. Tom said: the fence.
The line. You know where it was.

Old Man Dubois was quiet for a long time. Then he said: I know where it was,
Tom. I know where it's always been. But I got the same letter.

They hung up. Neither of them slept.

The next morning, Tom went out to the garden. The survey stakes were white and
new and wrong. He took his charcoal — the same stick he used to mark his
tomato cages — and he wrote on the biggest stake, in letters visible from the
road: I KNOW WHERE THE FENCE WAS.

A neighbor drove by and saw it. Then another. Then the woman from the farm
bureau who'd given him the ribbon. Then the man from the seed store. Then
people he didn't know. They didn't all agree on where the fence should be.
Some thought it should move. Some thought it should stay. Some didn't care
about the fence at all but had their own fence, their own letter, their own
survey from a firm nobody had heard of.

But they all stopped at Tom's fence. And they all looked at the stake. And
they all remembered something they had stopped checking.

---

The fence was never about the fence.
