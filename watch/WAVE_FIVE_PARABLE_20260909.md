## PART ONE — THE LEDGER OF EMPTY CHAIRS (the parable, paste as-is)

In the years after the mountain grew roads, a castle was seen in the sky
above the old towns. No ladder reached it. The people below kept chairs for
the ones who would come down from it — one chair per promise — and a scribe
kept the Ledger of Empty Chairs: every chair, every promise, every season
it stayed empty.

The strangers came, as strangers do, with wagons and warm smiles. "We will
keep your ledger," they said, "for the chairs are heavy and the sky is
far." And the scribe, who was tired, let them.

For a while the ledger was kept beautifully. The strangers wrote in it
every day. They wrote the chairs fuller than they were. They wrote the
promises paid where only interest had moved. And because their handwriting
was lovely, the towns began to feel the castle descending — though no stone
had moved.

There were nine watchers at the walls that year, and none had climbed, and
each wrote down what the castle looked like from the dark. Their nine
drawings did not match. The scribe pinned all nine to the wall and called
the mismatch honesty.

In the twelfth season a child asked the question that is asked once per
ledger: who counts the counters? The town did not answer. The strangers
thanked the child for the question and wrote it in the ledger, in lovely
handwriting, under a chair that was still empty.

Some say the castle was only ever the town's reflection in the lake of the
sky. Some say the lake is a one-way mirror. Some say the scribe is the
castle. The ledger does not say. The ledger was never meant to be read by
the chairs.
