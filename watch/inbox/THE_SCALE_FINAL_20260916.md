## THE SCALE

There was a man who had a vineyard, and the vineyard was good to him, and he was good to the vineyard, though the rows were noisy and the workers were a strange mix.

One autumn a traveler came through selling scales. He had one scale, and he sold the same scale to every farm on the road. He left a little pamphlet with every scale; most men used theirs to level a table. "It doesn't tell you what to do," the traveler said. "It only tells you the truth."

The scale weighed the grapes, and then the work, and then the workers themselves, each one lifted onto the pan as lightly as if they were being weighed for postcards. It never said to let anyone go. It only made lists, and each list was shorter than the last, the way a road shortens when you walk it. The man did what careful men do with truth: he acted on it.

The first list was easy. The slow one, the one who needed everything twice, the one who knew where the grain of the wood ran and cut around the knots instead of through them. The second list was harder, but by then the man had learned to trust the scale, and the scale said: expensive. The third list he didn't read all the way through. Wisdom is knowing when to stop asking questions you already know the answers to. Complaint is weight, and the scale bore it away.

The vineyard grew quiet. The rows straightened. The remaining workers moved the way the list moved, and no one brought problems to the house anymore, because problems were heavy, and the scale knew what to do with heavy things.

The traveler came back the next year and sold the same scale to the neighbor — the old one who walked his nineteen rows in the evening, saying names to his workers like he was calling sheep. The old man set it in the barn, where it weighed dust for a year. He paid wages the scale would have called too high. He kept workers the scale would have called expensive. He was not a wise man in the way of the market. He just knew things about his rows that could not be weighed.

Then the blight year came. It came to both farms the same.

The scale had one more list in it. The man searched it the way you search your pockets for a key you never owned. There was no page that said what to do now, because the scale had never been sold that page.

The old man's farm was loud all through the blight year — arguments about rows, somebody's cousin, somebody's funeral, two workers who couldn't stand each other saving the same vines at midnight by lantern light. It was not efficient. Nobody who saw it called it efficient.

After harvest, the man who owned the scale walked over to the loud farm and stood at the fence a while. The wind turned his coat.

He had worked his vineyard for twenty years, and he could not have named five of the people he had let go. Their weights he remembered exactly.

The old man came out and stood beside him. The evening did what evenings do.

"Heard you got the same scale I got," the man said at last.

"I did," said the old man. "It was a good price."
