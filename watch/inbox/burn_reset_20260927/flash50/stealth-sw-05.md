## The framing, stated precisely

The framing under study: a language model's only epistemic access to the world is a finite training corpus. At query time the model answers questions about the world as if that corpus were complete. A fact absent from the corpus is not answered with silence but with distributional smoothing over corpus-adjacent patterns, which yields fluent, confident fiction. Epistemically this is a closed world assumption (absence of evidence in the corpus treated as if the corpus exhausted reality) deployed over an open world (reality is unbounded and keeps changing). The research question is: who has made this exact move formally, and what pushback exists.

## Who has published this framing formally

The framing exists in three independent formal registers plus an empirical cluster. All arXiv IDs below were confirmed this session by direct fetch of the arxiv.org abstract page or the export.arxiv.org API; nothing here rests on memory alone except the two items tagged otherwise.

Register 1: statistical inevitability over a finite corpus.
- Kalai and Vempala, "Calibrated Language Models Must Hallucinate", arXiv:2311.14648 (2023), published in STOC 2024 proceedings [ID confirmed by export.arxiv.org API; result and venue confirmed by WebSearch including the ResearchGate entry and an OpenAI PDF citation "Vempala. 2024. Calibrated Language Models Must Hallucinate. In Proceedings of the 56t[h]..."]. The theorem: any statistically calibrated language model must hallucinate at a rate at least proportional to the prevalence of singleton facts, facts that appear only once in the finite training corpus. The closed-corpus move is explicit: with a finite training set there are always facts held out of it, and a calibrated model cannot distinguish those from plausible errors, so the corpus's finiteness is converted directly into a hallucination rate floor. This is the cleanest formal statement of "closed corpus treated as reality has a measurable price".
- Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, Zhang, "Why Language Models Hallucinate", arXiv:2509.04664 (OpenAI, Sep 2025) [abstract page fetched directly]. Extends the statistical argument (errors indistinguishable from facts generate hallucinations "through natural statistical pressures") and adds the evaluation-incentive argument (see counterargument C2). Reported at 672+ citations per search lane.

Register 2: computability inevitability.
- Xu, Jain, Kankanhalli, "Hallucination is Inevitable: An Innate Limitation of Large Language Models", arXiv:2401.11817 (2024, v2 2025) [abstract page fetched directly, authors verified as Ziwei Xu, Sanjay Jain, Mohan Kankanhalli]. Defines a formal world where hallucination means mismatch between a computable LLM and a computable ground-truth function; learning-theory results show LLMs cannot learn all computable functions, so any LLM acting as a general problem solver mismatches somewhere; since the formal world is a subset of the more complex real world, real-world hallucination follows a fortiori. The paper identifies hallucination-prone tasks with time-complexity bounds and uses the framework to grade mitigation methods.

Register 3: epistemological, closed world vs open world assumption. This is the register closest to the ask's exact wording.
- Bowen Xu, "Hallucination is Inevitable for LLMs with the Open World Assumption", arXiv:2510.05116 (2025) [entry confirmed by export.arxiv.org API, abstract page fetched directly]. Reframes hallucination as the generalization problem: under a Closed World assumption (training and test distributions match) hallucination is mitigable; under an Open World assumption (unbounded environment) it becomes inevitable. Separates correctable from unavoidable hallucinations and concludes hallucination should be treated as a structural feature to be tolerated and made compatible with human intelligence rather than a defect to eliminate. The abstract page does not use the literal phrase "training corpus as complete reality" but the closed-world framing is exactly that move.
- Philosophical antecedent: Bender and Koller, "Climbing towards NLU: On Meaning, Form, and Understanding in the Age of Data", ACL 2020 [standard citation, not re-verified this session, flagged as such]. The octopus thought experiment already contains the closed-corpus claim: a learner with only form (text) never acquires meaning grounded in the world, so the corpus is its whole reality by construction. Ji et al. give the operationalization: "Survey of Hallucination in Natural Language Generation", arXiv:2202.03629 (2022; journal placement ACM Computing Surveys 2023 is prior knowledge, the arXiv ID is verified) [ID confirmed by export.arxiv.org API with full author list, Ziwei Ji et al.]. Its intrinsic/extrinsic split makes "conflict with the source" (extrinsic) the corpus-as-reality failure mode in summarization and translation.

Register 4: empirical corpus-coverage cluster.
- Kandpal, Deng, Roberts, Wallace, Raffel, "Large Language Models Struggle to Learn Long-Tail Knowledge", arXiv:2211.08411 (2022, ICML 2023) [ID and authors confirmed by export.arxiv.org API]. Few-shot accuracy tracks corpus frequency of the fact: the closed corpus's density is the model's knowledge.
- Mallen, Asai, Zhong, Das, Khashabi, Hajishirzi, "When Not to Trust Language Models: Investigating Effectiveness of Parametric and Non-Parametric Memories", arXiv:2212.10511 (2022, ACL 2023) [ID and authors confirmed by export.arxiv.org API]. Parametric (closed-corpus) accuracy rises with question popularity; retrieval beats the closed corpus exactly where the corpus is thin.
- Gekhman, Yona, Aharoni, Eyal, Feder, Reichart, Herzig, "Does Fine-Tuning LLMs on New Knowledge Encourage Hallucinations?", arXiv:2405.05904 (2024) [ID and authors confirmed by export.arxiv.org API]. Fine-tuning on facts unknown to the model teaches it to assert beyond its closed corpus, and it learns to do so at a higher rate, i.e., overfitting the corpus boundary produces the fabrication habit.
- Miao and Kearns, "Hallucination, Monofacts, and Miscalibration: An Empirical Investigation", arXiv:2502.08666 (2025) [ID and authors confirmed by export.arxiv.org API]. Directly tests the singleton/monofact prediction of Register 1 in practice.

## The counterarguments

C1: inevitable but statistically negligible (the sharpest direct rebuttal).
Suzuki, He, Tian, Wang, "Hallucinations are inevitable but can be made statistically negligible", arXiv:2502.12187 (2025) [abstract page fetched directly]. Accepts the computability inevitability results and then argues they "in principle, cannot explain practical issues of LLMs": a positive probabilistic proof shows hallucination probability can be driven arbitrarily low with sufficient training-data quality and quantity; both results coexist (elimination over an infinite input set is impossible, probability is always reducible), and read through information theory the positive result is the practically meaningful one. This is a published, formal rebuttal of the practical force of Register 2.

C2: the socio-technical rebuttal, hallucination as incentive artifact.
The same OpenAI paper 2509.04664 argues benchmarks reward guessing over abstention: models are "optimized to be good test-takers, and guessing when uncertain improves test performance"; hallucination is called an "epidemic" sustained by evaluation design, and the proposed fix is reforming the scoring of existing benchmarks (penalize confident errors like standardized tests) rather than any architectural change. Under this reading the closed corpus sets a floor for a specific theoretical model class, but observed hallucination rates are substantially a behavioral choice made by the training and evaluation pipeline.

C3: self-knowledge and mitigation empirics, the corpus is not destiny.
Kadavath et al., "Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know", arXiv:2207.05221 (2022) [ID confirmed by export.arxiv.org API, Anthropic team]. Models carry usable self-knowledge of what they know, which contradicts the wholesale corpus-as-reality reading. R-Tuning (Zhang et al., arXiv:2311.09677, confirmed by export.arxiv.org API) shows abstention is trainable. Farquhar, Kossen, Kuhn, Gal, "Detecting hallucinations in large language models using semantic entropy", Nature 630:625-630 (2024), DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07421-0 [search-verified with venue, DOI, and 2,300+ citation count from the author's Scholar profile] reframes the phenomenon as confabulation, sampling arbitrary answers from the model's meaning-level belief distribution, and shows the state is detectable and therefore actionable. Mallen (above) shows retrieval opens the closed corpus at inference; Gekhman (above) shows the assertion habit is trainable in both directions. None of these refute the theorems' letter, but together they rebut the practical-inevitability reading: the closed corpus is contingent, not intrinsic to deployed systems.

C4: inevitability is conditional on the world assumption.
Bowen Xu's 2510.05116 is itself half counterargument: the inevitability conclusion holds under the Open World assumption; under a Closed World assumption hallucination is mitigable. That converts "hallucination is inevitable" into "hallucination is inevitable exactly insofar as your deployment keeps the corpus closed", which is a design statement, not a law of nature.

C5: the terminology rebuttal, the frame is mislabeled.
Maleki, Padmanabhan, Dutta, "AI Hallucinations: A Misnomer Worth Clarifying", arXiv:2401.06796 (2024) [abstract page fetched directly; note the search lane initially misattributed this paper to Kapoor/Dutta and even invented a different author string, the direct fetch settles it]. A systematic review of definitions across fourteen databases finds inconsistent usage and calls for precision. Hicks, Humphries, Slater, "ChatGPT is bullshit", Ethics and Information Technology 26, article 38 (2024) [search-verified with the journal citation format; not on arXiv under that title per the API check] argue in Frankfurt's terms that LLMs are indifferent to truth value rather than perceiving falsely, which attacks the perceptual metaphor the closed-corpus story borrows. Farquhar et al. (above) push "confabulation" as the clinically closer term. These do not dispute the mathematics; they dispute that the word and its epistemic picture are the right handle.

C6: the feature-not-bug line (under-verified, reported as such).
The discourse claim that hallucination is the flip side of generativity kept surfacing, but my searches hit rate limits and could not pin a formal peer-reviewed paper; the best concrete hit was a University of Oulu literature review on the reliability-creativity relationship in LLMs [search-surfaced, not fetched]. I therefore report this counterargument as existing in commentary with no citation I verified. Do not bank a paper title for it from this file.

C7: scope-of-theorem analysis (my own analysis, flagged, partly formalized in C1).
Kalai-Vempala assumes a sampling model with calibration and no abstention or retrieval option over a fixed corpus; deployed systems have both options, so the theorem bounds a model class, not a product. The computability register proves any computable approximator of computable functions errs somewhere, which applies equally to a decision tree, gives no practical rate, and reaches the real world only by a fortiori step. Suzuki et al. (C1) already formalize the negligibility half of this critique; the model-class-scope half is my analysis and should be treated as analysis, not citation.

## Bottom line

The closed-corpus framing is formally published in at least three registers: statistical (Kalai-Vempala STOC 2024: calibration plus finite corpus yields a hallucination floor at singleton-fact prevalence), computability (Xu/Jain/Kankanhalli 2401.11817: no computable learner covers all computable ground truth), and epistemological (Bowen Xu 2510.05116: closed world mitigable, open world inevitable), with an empirical frequency cluster (Kandpal, Mallen, Gekhman, Miao-Kearns) showing corpus density predicts accuracy. The counterarguments divide into four live lines: formal negligibility (Suzuki 2502.12187: the impossibility results cannot in principle explain the practical phenomenon, probability is always reducible), socio-technical incentives (OpenAI 2509.04664: benchmark scoring manufactures the observed rates), trainability of self-knowledge and abstention (Kadavath, R-Tuning, Farquhar semantic entropy, retrieval per Mallen), and conditional framing (the same inevitability holds only while you keep the corpus closed). The terminological critique (Maleki et al., Hicks et al., confabulation) disputes the label and its perceptual epistemology rather than the math. The strongest overall reading the literature supports: the corpus-as-reality story is a real theorem about a theoretical model class, and the live scientific fight is over how much of deployed hallucination that theorem actually explains.
