## A. The three families, verified claims

### A1. Linear attention (fast weights)
- Katharopoulos et al., "Transformers are RNNs: Fast Autoregressive Transformers with Linear Attention", arXiv:2006.16236, ICML 2020 (metadata confirmed via live search result block; ~4,000 citations listed). Self-attention rewritten through a kernel feature map phi, replacing the exponential softmax kernel, which drops the cost from O(T^2) to O(T); the same computation has an RNN form where the model maintains an associative memory built from key/value outer products (fast weights), giving O(1) per generated token instead of a growing KV cache.
- Yang et al., "Gated Linear Attention Transformers with Hardware-Efficient Training", arXiv:2312.06635 (fetched). Linear attention "can simultaneously be formulated as an RNN with 2D (matrix-valued) hidden states, thus enjoying linear-time inference complexity". Contributes FlashLinearAttention, a chunked hardware-efficient algorithm "faster than FLASHATTENTION-2 (Dao, 2023) as a standalone layer even on short sequence lengths (e.g., 1K)"; adds data-dependent gates; GLA trains with "higher throughput than a similarly-sized Mamba model" and a model trained on 2K generalizes past 20K "without significant perplexity degradations".
- Yang et al., "Parallelizing Linear Transformers with the Delta Rule over Sequence Length" (DeltaNet), arXiv:2406.06484 (fetched). Replaces the additive outer-product update with the delta rule, which "have been found to be more effective at associative recall"; the obstacle was that delta-rule training did not parallelize over sequence length; the paper's algorithm (via "a memory-efficient representation for computing products of Householder matrices") scales DeltaNet to 1.3B params on 100B tokens, beating Mamba and GLA on perplexity and zero-shot tasks.

### A2. State-space models
- Gu and Dao, "Mamba: Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective State Spaces", arXiv:2312.00752 (fetched, v2 2024-05-31). Prior subquadratic models failed at content-based reasoning; the fix is "simply letting the SSM parameters be functions of the input" (selectivity: input-dependent Delta, B, C), which breaks the convolution form, so they build "a hardware-aware parallel algorithm in recurrent mode" (selective scan, fused kernels, state in SRAM). Claims: "linear scaling in sequence length" holding "up to million-length sequences", "5x higher throughput than Transformers", Mamba-3B "outperforms Transformers of the same size and matches Transformers twice its size", and it drops attention AND MLP blocks for a homogeneous stack.
- Dao and Gu, "Transformers are SSMs: Generalized Models and Efficient Algorithms Through Structured State Space Duality" (Mamba-2), arXiv:2405.21060, ICML 2024 (fetched). The SSD framework links SSMs and attention variants through decompositions of structured semiseparable matrices; the Mamba-2 layer is "a refinement of Mamba's selective SSM that is 2-8X faster". This matters because it shows the two families are the same object under a change of basis: attention and recurrences are ends of one spectrum, not rivals.

### A3. RWKV
- Peng et al., "Eagle and Finch: RWKV with Matrix-Valued States and Dynamic Recurrence", arXiv:2404.05892 (fetched, v4). RWKV-5 Eagle adds "multi-headed matrix-valued states", RWKV-6 Finch adds "a dynamic recurrence mechanism" that "improve expressivity while maintaining the inference efficiency characteristics of RNNs". Trained on a 1.12T-token multilingual corpus; four Eagle models 0.46B to 7.5B and two Finch models 1.6B and 3.1B, Apache 2.0.
- Peng et al., "RWKV-7 'Goose' with Expressive Dynamic State Evolution", arXiv:2503.14456 (fetched; confirmed this is the real RWKV-7 paper, my earlier guessed ID was wrong). "RWKV-7 introduces a newly generalized formulation of the delta rule with vector-valued gating and in-context learning rates" plus "a relaxed value replacement rule". Expressivity claim: RWKV-7 "can perform state tracking and recognize all regular languages" while "this exceeds the capabilities of Transformers under standard complexity conjectures, which are limited to TC^0", with "constant memory usage and constant inference time per token". Its 2.9B model reaches 3B-scale SoTA on multilingual tasks.
- Official RWKV-LM README (raw.githubusercontent.com/BlinkDL/RWKV-LM/main/README.md, fetched): RWKV-7 described as "a strong linear-time & constant-space (no kv-cache) & attention-free & 100% RNN architecture" and a "meta-in-context learner, test-time-training its state on the context via in-context gradient descent at every token"; measured "145+ token/s RWKV-7 7.2B fp16 bsz1 decoding @ RTX5090 (always const speed & vram)".

## B. The recall wall (the core limitation)

- Arora et al., "Zoology: Measuring and Improving Recall in Efficient Language Models", arXiv:2312.04927 (fetched). Across 17 pretrained models, "82% of the gap is explained by each model's ability to recall information that is previously mentioned in-context"; the gap is stark: "a 70M parameter attention model outperforms a 1.4 billion parameter gated-convolution model on associative recall". They formalize MQAR (multi-query associative recall) as the diagnostic, and show "hybrids with input-dependent sparse attention patterns can close 97.4% of the gap to attention, while maintaining sub-quadratic scaling".
- Jelassi et al., "Repeat After Me: Transformers are Better than State Space Models at Copying", arXiv:2402.01032 (fetched). Theory: "a two layer transformer can copy strings of exponential length while GSSMs are fundamentally limited by their fixed-size latent state". On pretrained models, "transformer models dramatically outperform state space models at copying and retrieving information from context", arguing "a fundamental gap between transformers and GSSMs on tasks of practical interest".
- Arora et al., "Simple linear attention language models balance the recall-throughput tradeoff" (BASED), arXiv:2402.18668 (fetched). Names the tradeoff directly: "a key tradeoff between a model's state size and recall ability"; fixed-state architectures (H3, Mamba, RWKV) "struggle at recall". BASED dials state size by mixing linear attention with a small sliding-window attention; up to 1.3B it beats sub-quadratic models on recall-heavy tasks "by 6.22 accuracy points" and delivers "24x higher throughput on language generation than FlashAttention-2" generating 1024 tokens.

## C. Hybrids: the production resolution

- De et al. (DeepMind), "Griffin: Mixing Gated Linear Recurrences with Local Attention for Efficient Language Models", arXiv:2402.19427 (fetched). Hawk is an RNN with gated linear recurrences; Griffin interleaves them with local attention. "Hawk exceeds the reported performance of Mamba on downstream tasks"; Griffin "matches the performance of Llama-2 despite being trained on over 6 times fewer tokens"; the models "match the hardware efficiency of Transformers during training" with lower inference latency and higher throughput, "can extrapolate on sequences significantly longer than those seen during training", and scale to 14B.
- Lieber et al. (AI21), "Jamba: A Hybrid Transformer-Mamba Language Model", arXiv:2403.19887 (fetched). "Jamba interleaves blocks of Transformer and Mamba layers, enjoying the benefits of both model families", with MoE in some layers; it fits "in a single 80GB GPU", has "high throughput and small memory footprint compared to vanilla Transformers", and "presents strong results for up to 256K tokens context length".
- Unverified but directionally consistent (search snippets only, NOT fetched, treat as leads): IBM Granite 4.0 ships a hybrid Mamba-2/transformer design with roughly 70 percent lower memory and 2x inference claims (snippet sourced from secondary pages; my fetch of the guessed announcement URL returned an unrelated listing page), and NVIDIA's Nemotron-H line is a Mamba2-transformer hybrid MoE. I could not verify either against a primary page this ask.

## D. Memory learned at test time

- Behrouz, Zhong, Mirrokni (Google), "Titans: Learning to Memorize at Test Time", arXiv:2501.00663 (fetched). A "new neural long-term memory module that learns to memorize historical context" with "fast parallelizable training while maintaining a fast inference"; framing: "attention due to its limited context but accurate dependency modeling performs as a short-term memory" while the neural memory "acts as a long-term, more persistent, memory"; the models "can effectively scale to larger than 2M context window size with higher accuracy in needle-in-a-haystack tasks compared to baselines".

## E. Implications for long-context agent memory (my synthesis from the above; this section is analysis, the sections above are cited fact)

1. Agent transcripts are the worst case for fixed state. An agent session is a stream of keys and values that must be recalled verbatim much later: tool-call IDs, file paths, receipt hashes, earlier verdicts. That is exactly the copying/associative-recall regime where Zoology, Repeat After Me, and BASED all measure fixed-state models losing to attention. A pure Mamba/RWKV/linear-attention backbone does not remove the need for a memory system, it moves it outside the weights-and-state.
2. The transformer KV cache is a memory system the field stopped calling one: O(T) bytes, perfectly exact recall, paid at inference. Subquadratic architectures trade that exact, unbounded recall for a fixed-size lossy state. The honest engineering question for agents is not "which architecture" but "where does the receipt live": in a KV cache you pay for, in a lossy state you cannot trust, or in an explicit external store you can verify.
3. The estate already runs answer 3. Mag's memory architecture (INDEX.json + index_events receipts, percolator/quantizer compiled laws, context packs paged in per seat, RIB doctrine files) is external associative memory with explicit write receipts, exactly the shape the recall papers imply: a cheap constant-state backbone plus a retrieval layer that pages in only what the current turn needs, with verification hooks the KV cache cannot offer (hashes, seals, fold-verify). Context packs are paged-in state; ribs are delta-rule updates at the doctrine level. A fixed-state backbone plus this store would likely match a long-context transformer on the fleet's workloads at a fraction of the per-token cost, because the store does the recall, not the state.
4. Hybrids are the deployable middle. One sparse or local attention layer per N recurrent layers recovers most recall (Zoology: 97.4 percent gap closure subquadratically; BASED's pareto dial), and two production-grade families (Griffin, Jamba) show the shape survives scaling: Jamba holds 256K context in one 80GB GPU. For an agent runtime, the interesting knob is attention share as a recall budget you can spend per deployment.
5. The delta-rule convergence is the deep result. Linear attention, gated linear attention, DeltaNet, RWKV-7, and Titans' neural memory all land on the same object: a matrix-valued associative state updated by a learned, input-dependent rule (gates, delta rule, in-context learning rates). RWKV-7's README says it plainly: test-time-training the state via in-context gradient descent at every token. Agents already live this pattern at the systems level (receipt-banking, percolator promotion, ratchet baselines); the architectures are catching up to it, not the reverse.
6. Economics for long-horizon agent loops: constant per-token cost and no KV cache (RWKV-7 const speed and vram; Mamba 5x throughput) flatten the marginal cost of 100k-token tool sessions. Two caveats I did not verify this ask: prefix-cache reuse of a big shared system prompt (the caching law this harness relies on for byte-identical asks) is a transformer-KV economics pattern, and its analog in recurrent serving stacks (state snapshots per session) has different invalidation semantics; and speculative decoding interaction with RNN backbones is unmeasured here. Both are open engineering questions, not blockers.
