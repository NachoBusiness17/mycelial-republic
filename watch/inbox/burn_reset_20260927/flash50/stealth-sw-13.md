## 1. What the theorems actually say (anchor: SEP, fetched)

Source fetched this ask: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/goedel-incompleteness/ (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Godel's Incompleteness Theorems").

- First theorem (Rosser-improved form): any consistent formal system F within which a certain amount of elementary arithmetic can be carried out is incomplete; there are statements of F's language that are neither provable nor refutable in F. If F contains Robinson arithmetic Q, a sentence G_F can be mechanically constructed such that, if F is consistent, F does not prove G_F; if F is 1-consistent, F does not prove its negation. Rosser (1936) reduced the hypothesis to plain consistency.
- Second theorem: for any consistent F containing elementary arithmetic, F cannot prove its own consistency (not-Prov_F(bot)). The proof requires the provability predicate to satisfy Loeb's derivability conditions D1-D3, and the theorem is intensional: the consistency statement must genuinely express consistency, not merely be extensionally adequate (Rosser-style provability predicates can yield provable "consistency" statements).
- Scope conditions that AI arguments routinely ignore: F must be recursively axiomatizable (algorithmically decidable axioms) and expressive enough for arithmetic. Counterexamples the SEP names where the theorems do not apply: Presburger arithmetic, the theory of multiplication alone, Tarski's complete decidable theory of real closed fields.
- The SEP explicitly warns against the common misreading that the theorems show "unprovable truths" in any absolute sense: provability is always relative to a particular formal system.

## 2. The Loebian obstacle: why naive self-trust stalls (anchor: primary PDF, fetched)

Source fetched this ask: "Tiling Agents for Self-Modifying AI, and the Loebian Obstacle," Yudkowsky and Herreshoff, October 7, 2013 (early draft), MIRI. Downloaded via curl from https://intelligence.org/files/TilingAgents.pdf (390,743 bytes, PDF 1.5, 40 pages), text extracted with pypdf 5.x in the repo venv after NFC unicode normalization. Note: WebFetch reported 404 on the same URL while curl -L succeeded; the PDF is banked at flash50/_sw13_tiling_agents.pdf.

Verified passages (extracted text):
- Abstract: "Constructing a formalism in the most straightforward way produces a Godelian difficulty, the 'Loebian obstacle.' By technical methods we demonstrate the possibility of avoiding this obstacle, but the underlying puzzles of rational coherence are thus only partially addressed."
- Section 3 opens by stating Godel's Second Incompleteness Theorem in their notation ("systems at least as powerful as Peano Arithmetic prove their own consistency if and only if they are inconsistent") and generalizes via Loeb's Theorem.
- Under the paper's straightforward schema, "the offspring A0 must possess strictly weaker mathematical power than its parent A1, which is the Loebian obstacle." A proof-based agent can only approve a successor whose proofs it can verify, i.e. a weaker one, or it needs an infinite descending soundness schema (their partial workaround, credited to Fallenstein 2012).
- Attribution sentence verified in the PDF: "Thus it is the simultaneous intersection of the Vingean, tiling, and naturalistic principles which generates the Loebian obstacle." Self-governance difficulty is not incompleteness alone; it is incompleteness plus the demand that the agent model a world containing copies of itself, preserve its goals across rewrites, and accept no Cartesian dualism between itself and the world.
- The paper concedes its fixes are "by technical means ... not yet by fundamental rather than technical means," and that finding a fundamental decision criterion for self-modifying probabilistic agents "remains open."

## 3. Schmidhuber's Godel machine: incompleteness as a design constraint (anchor: author's page, fetched)

Source fetched this ask: https://people.idsia.ch/~juergen/goedelmachine.html (302 from idsia.ch followed). The page (promotional, self-authored) defines Godel machines as "self-referential universal problem solvers making provably optimal self-improvements," formalizing I. J. Good's 1965 intelligence-explosion remarks, inspired by Godel's 1931 self-referential formulas. Mechanics: the machine rewrites any part of its own code only after an initial proof searcher, itself part of the code, proves the rewrite useful, with the utility function, hardware, and initial code all captured in axioms. Any accepted rewrite is "globally optimal - no local maxima!" because the machine must first prove that continuing to search for alternative rewrites is not useful. The page stresses they run on ordinary computers: "No hypothetical super-Turing capabilities."
Citations given by the page: arXiv cs.LO/0309048 (2003, revised Dec 2006); "Godel machines: Fully Self-Referential Optimal Universal Self-Improvers," in Artificial General Intelligence (Goertzel and Pennachin, eds., 2006); "Ultimate Cognition a la Godel," Cognitive Computation 1(2):177-193, 2009; Steunebrink and Schmidhuber, AGI-11 2011 and Springer 2012.
Limits the incompleteness frame makes visible: the optimality guarantee holds only for rewrites provable inside the machine's own axiom base; anything unprovable there is unreachable by design, so the machine is deliberately incomplete. The page itself discusses consistency assumptions and proof-search cost only lightly; I flag that as a gap in the source, not a claim about the papers.

## 4. The empirical contrast: Darwin Godel Machine (anchor: arXiv abs page, fetched)

Source fetched this ask: https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22954, "Darwin Godel Machine: Open-Ended Evolution of Self-Improving Agents," Jenny Zhang, Shengran Hu, Cong Lu, Robert Lange, Jeff Clune.
- The DGM "iteratively modifies its own code" including its own ability to modify that code, validated empirically on coding benchmarks rather than by formal proof, explicitly contrasting with the original Godel machine's provable-benefit requirement.
- Verified abstract numbers: SWE-bench 20.0% to 50.0%, Polyglot 14.2% to 30.7%, via an archive of agents (open-ended evolution) sampled and mutated by a foundation model.
- Safety: the abstract states all experiments used "safety precautions (e.g., sandboxing, human oversight)." The abstract itself does not mention reward hacking; that detail appears in secondary coverage and was not verified against the paper body this ask, so treat it as unverified.

## 5. The stronger wall is Rice, not Godel (anchor: encyclopedia, fetched)

Source fetched this ask: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice's_theorem.
- Statement: all non-trivial semantic properties of programs are undecidable; proved by H. G. Rice (1951 dissertation); generalizes the halting problem.
- Consequence for a self-governing AI's verifier: any behavioral specification worth wanting (termination on all inputs, equivalence to a reference program, "never does X") is non-trivial and semantic, hence undecidable to check over arbitrary programs. "It is impossible to write a program that automatically verifies for the absence of bugs in other programs."
- The escape hatches are structural, and they map onto AI-governance design: restrict the language or state space (typed/guarded subsets, finite-state model checking), accept over- or under-approximation (abstract interpretation), or move guarantees into syntax (type systems, Hoare-style proof annotations).
- Conceptual hygiene the ask deserves: incompleteness theorems are about truth versus provability inside a theory; undecidability results (halting, Rice) are about nonexistence of decision procedures over programs. For "can an AI verify its own successor against a safety spec," Rice bites directly; G2 bites on self-certification of the rule system itself; conflating them is the standard pop-Godel error.

## 6. Living with self-reference on purpose: reflective oracles (anchor: arXiv abs page, fetched)

Source fetched this ask: https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.04145, "Reflective Oracles: A Foundation for Classical Game Theory," Benja Fallenstein, Jessica Taylor, Paul F. Christiano (2015).
- Agents modeled as plain Turing machines predicting each other hit "standard diagonalization reasons" (liar-paradox self-reference, the halting problem's cousin). Reflective oracles answer questions about the outputs of oracle machines that themselves access the same oracle, dodging diagonalization "by randomizing some answers."
- Existence is proven, and agents equipped with them model coplayers as part of the environment and play a Nash equilibrium, with mixed-strategy randomness coming from the oracle's randomization.
- This is the constructive counterpoint to the Loebian obstacle: where proof-based self-trust is incomplete, probabilistic self-reference with calibrated randomization is consistent. (Note: a third recalled id, 1511.01017, also proved wrong on fetch, a LASSO/AMP paper; 1508.04145 is verified.)

## 7. Governance impossibility results, 2021-2026 (anchors: arXiv API + abs pages, fetched)

Located via arXiv API query all:"Impossibility Results in AI" (the au:Yampolskiy_R author query returned 0 entries; reported as run):
- "Impossibility Results in AI: A Survey," Mario Brcic and Roman V. Yampolskiy, arXiv:2109.00484, 23 pages, 103 references, published in ACM Computing Surveys 2023 per the abs page. Organizes impossibility theorems into five mechanism categories: deduction, indistinguishability, induction, tradeoffs, intractability. Key line for this ask: "deductive impossibilities deny 100%-guarantees for security." New results include "the unfairness of explainability," misalignment between agent clones, and limits on agent self-awareness. Recommended posture: formalize safety questions as constraint satisfaction, "the most prudent approach to long-term AI safety initiatives." Caveat: I read the abstract only; which classic theorems (Godel, halting, no-free-lunch) appear in the body is not verified this ask.
- "The Accountability Horizon: An Impossibility Theorem for Governing Human-Agent Collectives," Haileleol Tibebu and Hewan Shemtaga, arXiv:2604.07778 (cs.AI, submitted April 9, 2026, revised June 2, 2026). Formalizes human-agent collectives as state-policy tuples in a shared causal model, characterizes autonomy along four information-theoretic axes (epistemic, executive, evaluative, social), and proves an "Accountability Incompleteness Theorem": no accountability framework can simultaneously satisfy Attributability, Foreseeability Bound, Non-Vacuity, and Completeness once compound autonomy crosses a threshold ("the Accountability Horizon") and the interaction graph contains a human-AI feedback cycle. The abstract calls this structural, not an engineering gap: "transparency, audits, and oversight cannot resolve it without reducing autonomy," with a sharp phase transition below which legitimate accountability frameworks exist, and claims to be "the first impossibility result in AI governance." Caveat: abstract-level verification only; the 3,000-synthetic-collective experiments are not independently checked.

## 8. Synthesis: what the theorems imply for AI that governs itself

1. No self-certification. A consistent, arithmetic-capable, recursively axiomatized governor cannot prove its own consistency or safety from inside its own rule system (G2). Self-governance therefore requires something outside the governed system: an external verifier, a human gate, or an accepted axiom injection. This is the cleanest direct implication, and the estate's own architecture already obeys it (operator gates, pre-push hooks, spec-before-code).
2. Naive self-trust loops stall, but not fatally. The Loebian obstacle blocks the straightforward "I trust my successor exactly as I trust myself" loop; the Tiling Agents paper proves offspring under its schema must be mathematically weaker, and offers only partial technical workarounds (infinite descending soundness schemas, split decision criteria) with the fundamental decision criterion still open. Implication: unbounded self-improvement with preserved trust is not something a proof-based agent can bootstrap for free.
3. Provably safe self-improvement exists but is deliberately incomplete. Schmidhuber's Godel machine gets global optimality only for provable rewrites within its axiom base; unprovable-but-good rewrites are unreachable. Incompleteness shows up as a self-imposed ceiling on improvement, not as instability. The DGM shows the empirical alternative (validate-then-rewrite, no proofs) works in practice and leans on sandboxing and human oversight for safety, exactly the external-verifier structure implication 1 predicts.
4. The verification wall for governing code is Rice's theorem, and it is worse than Godel. Even with consistency assumed, no complete automatic checker can verify arbitrary Turing-complete governor behavior against a semantic spec. Every sound approach restricts something: the language, the state space (finite-state model checking), the completeness of the analysis, or moves guarantees into syntax. For self-governance design this reads as: constrain the governor's action space by construction rather than verify its behavior after the fact.
5. The Lucas-Penrose shortcut fails in both directions. Per the fetched SEP entry, the anti-mechanist argument (a machine cannot see its Godel sentence true while we can) needs the premise that humans can always see consistency of any given theory, which Putnam's objection makes "highly implausible"; Godel himself drew only a disjunction in his 1951 Gibbs lecture (mind surpasses machines, or absolutely unsolvable Diophantine problems exist), and Benacerraf's middle position (I am a Turing machine but cannot determine which) is consistent. So "humans transcend Godel and machines do not" is unsound, and equally "Godel breaks machine self-governance entirely" is unsound. The theorems constrain formal self-certification; they do not forbid machine intelligence or machine self-modification.
6. Probabilistic self-reference is a live escape hatch. Reflective oracles (Fallenstein, Taylor, Christiano) show self-referential reasoning about one's own and others' predictions is consistent once answers may be randomized, yielding Nash play. The lesson for self-governing AI: swap exact proof-based self-models for calibrated probabilistic self-models with external grounding; the impossibility is about complete deterministic self-knowledge, not self-knowledge per se.
7. Governance translation, 2026 state of the art. The impossibility literature has moved from meta-mathematics to governance: Brcic and Yampolskiy catalogue five mechanism classes of impossibility and conclude deductive results deny 100% security guarantees, recommending constraint-satisfaction framing; Tibebu and Shemtaga prove accountability itself goes impossible past an autonomy threshold under human-AI feedback cycles. Both converge on the same engineering posture the logic implies: cap autonomy below the threshold where external oversight becomes unverifiable, keep the verifier outside the governed system, and design for runtime monitoring and sandboxing rather than pre-hoc absolute proof.
