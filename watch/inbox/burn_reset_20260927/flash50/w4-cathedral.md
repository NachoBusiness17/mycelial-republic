## 3. The flying buttresses

The test is honest in both directions: v3 and v4 passing their gates proves the crypt and nave already stand and the building is in daily, honest use; what fails is only the removal condition, which is exactly the difference between a consecrated church and a finished cathedral.

**(c) The theoretical answer: "done" may be a category error, and the estate reached that result independently.** Lehman's laws of software evolution (M. Lehman, "Programs, Life Cycles, and Laws of Software Evolution," Proc. IEEE 68(9), 1980) hold that a real-world (E-type) system must continually evolve or grow progressively less useful; there is no terminal specification to satisfy because the world it serves moves. The refusal to date v6-v10 is folk-Lehman: instead of a completion state the estate maintains an evolution apparatus (improve loop: scout -> eval -> promote with human gate, compass:99). Building historiography says the same from the other side: Stewart Brand, *How Buildings Learn* (1994) - buildings that survive are the ones continuously adapted, and the adaptation machinery is the building's real product; Christopher Alexander, *The Timeless Way of Building* (1979) - the living quality comes from piecemeal growth under shared patterns, not a master plan executed once; Jean Gimpel, *The Cathedral Builders* (1980) - masters moved between lodges and died, and what persisted was the lodge's grammar. That is precisely the estate's correction-minting problem in period dress: the grammar must live in the ribs, not in the master's voice, or every master transition is an amnesia event. And Raymond's *The Cathedral and the Bazaar* (1997 essay, 1999 book) deserves an inversion here: the estate is a bazaar of throwaway seats building a cathedral for an audience of one. Raymond's objection to cathedrals was too few eyes; the estate's answer is that the bazaar parts (sessions, lanes, gopher fan-outs) are the labor while the cathedral parts (chain, ribs, gates, receipts) are what makes that labor compound instead of evaporate.

This yields the critical-path claim of the card: **the spire is blocked by the buttresses, not by the nave.** A loop that builds the next version must hold its own walls - mint its own corrections, render its own ribs at boot, verify its own outputs at the funded tier. The TBD gates on v6 are not missing design; they are waiting on buttress-independence. The estate even has the period cautionary tale mapped: at Beauvais, the choir vaults reached record height and came down in 1284; the tower fell in 1573 while the spire was still being pushed higher. Height before thrust-resolve is the one Gothic sin, and the estate's gate ladder ("no fake dates") is the structural engineer refusing to sign off on spire height until the walls carry. That refusal, currently, is the most honest stone in the building - and the cheapest next move is not another tier of ambition, it is wiring the ribs into the boot payload, because a buttress that is never connected carries nothing.
