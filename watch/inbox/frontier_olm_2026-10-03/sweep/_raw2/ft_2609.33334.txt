 Draft-Guided Eviction
 for Training-Free KV-Cache Compression

 Abstract 

 Training-free KV-cache compression methods such as SnapKV, H2O, and PyramidKV evict tokens at the end of prefill, aiming to preserve the attention mass that future queries are expected to use—optimizing what to keep. We show that this objective fails in two distinct ways. (1) Compensation: restoring the evicted attention mass can recover the attention-level target without recovering task quality. (2) Selection: covering more of the true decode-query mass can hurt quality when the recovered mass is fragmented rather than concentrated in coherent spans. These failures share a common cause: eviction occurs before the queries that determine the answer trajectory exist. We propose D raft- G uided E viction ( Dge ), which defers eviction until after drafting the first answer tokens using the full cache—just one decode step beyond prefill. Because the draft is generated from the answer’s own prefix, no cache entries are discarded before this trajectory signal becomes available. The per-head cache budget remains unchanged, and Dge can be applied directly to SnapKV, PyramidKV, H2O, and StreamingLLM without modifying their eviction scores. Unlike extra-pass methods, Dge changes when eviction occurs rather than what cache entries are selected. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Dge outperforms prior methods at every evaluated budget on five of six instruct-tuned backbones, achieving on LongBench, nearly matching FullKV at . The timing-only control Dge -W achieves the same score, demonstrating that the gain comes from when eviction occurs rather than what is selected—an effect we term trajectory anchoring . 

 1 Introduction 

 Serving long-context LLMs is a memory problem before it is a compute problem: the KV-Cache grows linearly with context length and batch size—roughly  GiB for an 8B model at 128K tokens, on par with the weights themselves—and every decode step reads the cache back. Training-free eviction is an attractive deployment strategy: keep a small per-head budget of past tokens, discard the rest, and change nothing else about the model. A productive line of work has refined which tokens to keep—StreamingLLM keeps attention sinks and recency ( Xiao et al., 2024 ) , H2O accumulates attention mass ( Zhang et al., 2023 ) , SnapKV max-pools the scores of a late-prompt observation window ( Li et al., 2024 ) , and PyramidKV schedules the budget across layers ( Cai et al., 2024 ) —but these methods share one recipe: when prefill ends, score the past tokens, keep the top- per head, and evict the rest, permanently and before the first answer token is generated .

 Two choices distinguish these training-free regimes: which queries score the cache, and when the cut fires. FullKV in Figure   1 (a) uses the real decode queries and never evicts; prefill-time eviction in Figure   1 (b) cuts before generation using only prefill-time scores; Lookahead Q-Cache (LAQ) in Figure   1 (c) also cuts at prefill, but replaces future decode queries with synthesized pseudo -queries ( Wang et al., 2025 ) . Our D raft- G uided E viction ( Dge ), in Figure   1 (d), instead drafts the first answer tokens on the full cache and evicts only afterward. It therefore observes the real decode queries before making the irreversible cut: Dge changes when the cut happens, rather than what scores the cache. 

 Despite these differences, existing approaches optimize a common implicit objective: preserve the attention mass that future computation would place on the discarded tokens. Selection maximizes captured mass, as in SnapKV and H2O ( Li et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2023 ) ; compensation attempts to restore what selection loses through merging entries or fitting biases and values ( Zhang et al., 2024b ; Wan et al., 2024b ; Zweiger et al., 2026 ) ; and allocation redistributes the budget across heads and layers ( Feng et al., 2024 ; Cai et al., 2024 ) . This objective is plausible, and its attention-level gains are real. However, whether increasing attention-mass preservation reliably translates into task quality has not been systematically examined under controlled dosage. 

 Our controlled observations reveal two limitations. (1) Compensation: mass is fungible, but content is not. Restoring evicted attention mass through merging, value absorption, or per-token correction does not necessarily recover the information carried by the original tokens. (2) Selection: more captured attention mass does not necessarily improve task performance. When retained mass is fragmented rather than concentrated in coherent spans, an extractive answer can lose the information needed for reconstruction. We establish both effects under controlled dosage in § 4 , with the corresponding sweeps in Figure   2 (a). Both limitations arise because eviction occurs before the queries that actually consume the cache exist: prefill-time signals can only estimate future decode queries, however they are constructed. 

 We propose Dge , which changes when eviction occurs rather than what is kept. Prior methods improve what a prefill-time cut keeps, while leaving the cut at the end of prefill ( Kim et al., 2025 ; Zweiger et al., 2026 ; Wang et al., 2025 ) . Dge instead drafts the first answer tokens on the full cache and evicts only afterward, allowing the decision to use real decode queries rather than estimates. The draft is the answer’s own prefix, not an auxiliary pass, so the per-head budget is unchanged and eviction leaves the prefill critical path. A single decode step is often sufficient, and § 6 shows that the deferral itself, rather than a sharper selection signal, drives the gain. We attribute this to what we call trajectory anchoring : the initial answer tokens, produced on the full cache, fix the model’s decoding trajectory before compression, and the compressed cache then carries the rest, as Figure   2 (b) shows. 

 Contributions. 

 • 

 Draft-guided eviction. We introduce Dge , a training-free KV-cache compression framework that opens a new when -to-evict axis: the model first drafts answer tokens with the full cache and evicts only afterward, keeping the per-head budget and leaving the scoring rule a free slot—the base evictor’s own (the timing-only control Dge -W) or the draft’s real queries (the default). 

 • 

 Controlled analysis of attention-based eviction. We identify two failure modes of optimizing attention mass— compensation and selection —and show that attention-level preservation can decouple from downstream task quality under controlled dosage. 

 • 

 Empirical validation. Across six instruct-tuned backbones from 3B to 14B parameters, Dge outperforms prior methods at every evaluated budget on five backbones and achieves an average LongBench score of , compared with for LAQ and for SnapKV, within of uncompressed FullKV. The same deferral lifts PyramidKV, H2O, and StreamingLLM, while Dge -W also reaches , isolating eviction timing as the source of the gain.

 2 Related Work 

 Selection and allocation: which tokens to keep. 

 The dominant training-free family scores past tokens at the end of prefill under a per-head budget. SnapKV ( Li et al., 2024 ) max-pools observation-window scores; H2O ( Zhang et al., 2023 ) and ScissorHands ( Liu et al., 2023 ) accumulate attention; FastGen ( Ge et al., 2024 ) adapts budgets per head; PyramidKV ( Cai et al., 2024 ) schedules them across layers; AdaKV ( Feng et al., 2024 ) and HeadKV ( Fu et al., 2024 ) reallocate them across heads; and StreamingLLM ( Xiao et al., 2024 ) , ThinK ( Xu et al., 2024 ) , selection ( Devoto et al., 2024 ) , and Quest ( Tang et al., 2024 ) use sinks, recency, or key statistics. These methods improve which tokens survive, but commit before the first answer token exists. Dge leaves the per-head budget unchanged and can leave the scoring rule unchanged too, deferring the same selection decision until after the initial answer tokens. On SnapKV’s own scores, this timing change alone already outperforms the selection rules we evaluate. 

 Compensation: restoring what selection discarded. 

 A second line of work attempts to repair the cut rather than improve selection. CaM ( Zhang et al., 2024b ) , LOOK-M ( Wan et al., 2024b ) , KVMerger ( Wang et al., 2024 ) , and D2O ( Wan et al., 2024a ) merge evicted entries into retained ones, with the vision analogue ToMe ( Bolya et al., 2023 ) ; FAST ( Zweiger et al., 2026 ) instead fits per-token biases and refits values. These methods can restore the attention mass targeted by the repair, but they cannot recover the original token-level content once it has been compressed away. Our controlled counterparts in § 4 expose this gap: attention-level compensation can improve without a corresponding gain in task quality. Dge avoids the repair problem altogether by generating the steering tokens while the cache is still intact, so the information needed to anchor the answer trajectory is never discarded. 

 Extra passes: sharpening the prefill-time signal. 

 A third line spends additional computation to improve the eviction signal. KVzip ( Kim et al., 2025 ) repeats the prefill pass; FAST, above, calibrates its biases and values on self-generated Q&A; LAQ ( Wang et al., 2025 ) synthesizes pseudo future queries; and LookaheadKV ( Ahn et al., 2026 ) trains adapters to predict such scores. These methods improve what to keep, but retain the same schedule: the cut still fires at prefill end, so the signal remains a proxy for the queries that actually consume the cache. Our experiments show that a matched LAQ run saturates with dose and, even at its best, trails a pure timing change. Other KV-efficiency methods change the representation, memory management, or decoding objective, including learned compression ( Zhang et al., 2024a ; Nawrot et al., 2024 ; Mu et al., 2023 ) , quantization ( Liu et al., 2024b ; Hooper et al., 2024 ) , PagedAttention ( Kwon et al., 2023 ) , InfiniGen ( Lee et al., 2024 ) , and speculative decoding ( Leviathan et al., 2023 ) ; concurrent analyses also question the attention-mass objective itself ( Feng et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2026 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ) . In contrast, Dge changes only the timing of eviction , using the answer’s own prefix to expose real decode queries before the cut; App.  F contrasts it with the draft-like designs.

 5 Draft-Guided Eviction 

 The two problems of § 4 share a cause: the cut fires before the
queries that matter exist. Dge removes the cause rather than either symptom
by making the real decode queries exist before the cut, and both
problems then dissolve: nothing is left to compensate, and the
coverage–contiguity trade-off is an artifact of pre-generation scoring—once
the queries reading the cache are the real ones, the spans an extractive answer
copies have already been read. 

 Policy. 

 Dge is a scheduling change, not a new scorer: it defers eviction past the
opening of the generation, as App.  E sets out: 

 1. 

 Draft (phase A). Prefill normally; generate the first 
answer tokens with the full cache, capturing the real decode
queries per layer through forward pre-hooks—no extra pass, the draft being
the beginning of the final answer. Prefill’s own forward emits token one, so
 is the base evictor and the minimal dose, used throughout. 

 2. 

 Evict (phase B). Score every past token, keep the per-head
budget, and edit the cache in place—once, irrevocably. The scorer is a free
slot. By default Dge uses the mean attention the draft queries pay to
each token, which the draft provides at no cost; the timing-only Dge -W keeps
the base evictor’s own scorer—SnapKV’s window, PyramidKV’s layer budgets,
H2O’s accumulator or StreamingLLM’s sinks—unchanged. 

 3. 

 Continue. Resume on the compressed cache, keeping the draft
tokens as ordinary output. 

 § 6 finds the two scorers equivalent on average: the
contribution is when the cut fires. The change is correspondingly
small to deploy: two stock generate() calls around one in-place cache
edit, with no training, no extra pass, and no hyper-parameter beyond 
(App.  E ). 

 Cost. 

 Dge leaves the asymptotic peak unchanged, as Figure   3 (a) sketches,
and the dose itself is free: total latency is flat in . The cost is one
cache surgery, on short answers and on long in
 Table   3 (b), against a TTFT of SnapKV’s that no
score-based prior evictor there beats. If the answer terminates within the
draft, no eviction occurs and the output is bit-identical to FullKV’s—the
rational policy, since the cache is freed at answer end. 

 A hypothesis for why Dge works: trajectory anchoring. 

 The first tokens are the generation’s high-information steering segment:
for extractive QA they contain the copied span; for summarization they fix
topic and structure. The draft segment is also precisely where prefill-time
eviction bites. Produced from the whole cache, the draft anchors a trajectory
a compressed cache then carries—consistent with the timing-only
 Dge -W matching the average of Dge across Table   2 and carrying

 7 Discussion 

 Sharper benchmarking. 

 The standard protocol—evict at end-of-prefill, then generate—folds two
decisions into one number; one deferred-eviction row separates them, and
averaged over backbones and budgets a timing-only deferral closes almost all of
the SnapKV-to-FullKV gap of Table   2 : the reported penalty is timing,
not selection quality. 

 What the objective measures. 

 Attention-output fidelity is a geometric quantity § 4 ’s mechanisms
already deliver near their ceilings; task quality asks instead for retrievable
content and a stable trajectory. 

 Limitations and research directions. 

 Trajectory anchoring is inferred from ablations, not isolated, and decoding
is greedy throughout, so how far it survives sampling is open. LongBench’s short answers cap the doses it can
probe—at a third of Llama-3.1-8B’s answers terminate inside the draft—pointing at
long-form, agentic and multilingual workloads. The draft holds the full cache,
so the prototype’s working set is FullKV’s ( in the native
GQA layout, App.  G ); batching it in a paged-attention server
would turn the TTFT gain into throughput. 

 8 Conclusion 

 Training-free KV-cache compression evicts at the end of prefill to preserve
the attention mass future queries will use—optimizing what to
keep—and that objective fails in two ways: restored mass does not restore
task quality, and extra coverage can hurt when it arrives fragmented. Both
occur because eviction fires before the queries that will read the cache
exist. Dge removes that cause by deferring eviction until after
drafting the first answer tokens on the full cache; it drops into
SnapKV, PyramidKV, H2O and StreamingLLM alike, changing when 
eviction occurs, not what is selected. It leads every prior method at
every budget on five of six backbones ( on LongBench vs FullKV’s
 ), and the timing-only control Dge -W reaches the same score: the gain
is trajectory anchoring , from the deferral itself. Before optimizing
 what to keep, fix when to evict.

 Appendix A Experimental details 

 Backbones and context. 

 We evaluate six instruct backbones. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct [ Dubey et al., 2024 ] 
is the primary model (128K native context via RoPE scaling); backbone checks use
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 [ Jiang et al., 2023 ] (32K context, a different
architecture family), Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [ Qwen Team, 2024 ] (a third
architecture family with 28 layers and 4 KV heads, ChatML template),
Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct (the largest backbone, 48 layers and 8 KV heads, same
ChatML protocol), Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (a smaller model of the same instruct
lineage, for a scale check), and Llama-3-8B-Instruct (8K native context, no RoPE
scaling; the shorter-context predecessor of the primary backbone). All share the
per-head KV-Cache geometry Dge operates on and differ only in long-context
adaptation and instruction-tuning generation, contrasted in Table   6 . 

 Datasets and metrics. 

 LongBench [ Bai et al., 2024 ] , all 16 English datasets: single-document QA
(NarrativeQA, Qasper, MultiFieldQA), multi-document QA (HotpotQA, 2WikiMQA,
MuSiQue), summarization (GovReport, QMSum, MultiNews), few-shot learning (TREC,
TriviaQA, SAMSum), synthetic retrieval/counting (PassageCount,
PassageRetrieval), and code completion (Lcc, RepoBench-P). We report the official
per-dataset metric—F1 for QA, ROUGE-L for summarization, classification
accuracy for TREC and the synthetic tasks, and edit similarity for code—and
the official per-dataset max-new-token budget. Prompts exceeding the truncation
limit are middle-truncated (first half last half) following the official
protocol; the limit is tokens for the Llama and Qwen backbones
(matched across methods) and for Mistral. 

 Decoding and prompt protocol. 

 Greedy decoding throughout, so results are deterministic and there is no
seed variance to average over. We use the repository’s corrected Llama-3 chat
template and stop-token set (adding <|eot_id|> and a per-dataset
newline stop for the few-shot sets, which otherwise over-generate); Mistral uses
its [INST] wrapping. Every method sees the identical decoding pipeline
and matched sample sets. 

 Compression settings. 

 Per-head budget ( unless noted), observation
window , max-pool kernel (the SnapKV recipe). The compressed cache
is stored per query head (GQA layout). Dge draft length 
( for both Dge and the timing-only Dge -W in the main tables,

the policy coincides with the base evictor by construction (the first token comes
from the prefill forward), so —one full-cache decode step—is the
smallest dose the method admits; whether it is also sufficient is
backbone-dependent, as § D shows.

 All diagnostics share one harness (released with the code).

exactly what the eviction code would see without altering the forward pass.

 Appendix F Draft-like passes compared 

 Several training-free compression methods insert an extra forward pass
between prefill and generation because the observation window is a weak
estimate of the queries the answer will actually ask. Table   8 
compares them along three design choices: what the pass generates, whether
its compute survives into the output, and which axis its product feeds.
Repeat-prefill [ Kim et al., 2025 ] replays the prompt and scores keys by
reconstruction; self-study [ Zweiger et al., 2026 ] generates synthetic
Q&A about the context to calibrate biases and values, at an up-front
per-context cost; Lookahead Q-Cache [ Wang et al., 2025 ] 
synthesizes pseudo future queries and re-scores retention with them.
All three discard the pass afterwards, and all three still cut at prefill
end: the pass only sharpens what is kept. Speculative decoding
 [ Leviathan et al., 2023 ] appears because Dge is mechanically
closer to it than to the eviction scorers—a draft generated by the model
itself, kept when it is right—but it targets latency, not memory.
LookaheadKV [ Ahn et al., 2026 ] is the trained sibling of the same
 what -axis move, predicting lookahead scores draft-free; we compare
training-free passes only.

 tokens, so the compute is not an overhead to amortize but output the
user receives either way; and its product is not a better score but a later
cut. The ablations say the second difference is the one that pays: with

 Appendix H Attention-matching loss 

 Definition. 

 Fix a layer and head. A compression keeps a retained
set of past keys and drops ; let range over the observation 
queries—in SnapKV the window queries at the end of the prompt, used as
an estimate of the not-yet-seen decode queries. Write the full-attention
log-partition and its retained restriction as 

 (4) 

 The training-free attention-matching loss is the mean log-partition deficit,
which we measure directly at compression time, 

 (5) 

 where is exactly the softmax mass the observation query places on the
evicted keys—the observation-query analogue of the evicted mass of
 Equation   2 . So the loss is a monotone transform of
the dropped attention mass: it is zero iff the retained set carries all of
 ’s attention and diverges as the evicted mass approaches one. Minimizing it
over under a size budget is the window-query counterpart of FAST’s
mass-matching objective [ Zweiger et al., 2026 ] , the one its NNLS fit solves; SnapKV’s top- selection
on the pooled window-query scores is its greedy solution, and the per-token
bias that FAST fits to reweight the retained logits shifts 
by exactly the recoverable part of ( 5 ) (a run with closes
the deficit to ). 

 The loss does not predict the downstream deficit. 

 The crucial point
for this paper is that ( 5 ) is an observation-query, prefill-time 
quantity: it scores how well reconstructs attention for the window queries,
on the full cache, before any decoding. Equation   3 shows the error
that actually accrues is over the real 
decode queries , whose weight is set by when is dropped, not by
how well the prefill observations were matched. Figure   8 makes this
quantitative at scale on Llama-3.1-8B ( ). Across the sixteen sets the
loss varies over a narrow band ( – ) yet SnapKV’s
deficit from FullKV ranges from to points, and the two are
essentially uncorrelated (Pearson , i.e. the loss explains
 of the deficit variance): the sets SnapKV hits hardest—Qasper
( ) and GovReport ( )—sit at low-to-mid loss, while the
highest-loss set (TREC, ) loses only . Keeping the identical selection
and evicting one decode step later ( Dge -W 2 ) drives the deficit to on
average (worst case ) at every loss. The
quantity the what -axis optimizes is orthogonal to the error the

 Appendix J Needle-in-a-Haystack retrieval 

 The LongBench averages aggregate generation quality; Figure   9 isolates
 retrieval under the same tight budget. We run the standard
Needle-in-a-Haystack probe on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct at : the needle
sentence is planted at depths across context lengths ( k– k), for
 probes per method, and a cell is scored by the fraction of the expected
answer’s words the model recovers. 

 This is the regime most favorable to prefill-time eviction: the retrieval
question is in the prompt, so SnapKV’s window queries can see which
tokens matter, and a query-aware selector should simply keep the needle. It does
not. At SnapKV recovers only against FullKV’s —it
drops needles across the whole depth length grid—and H2O, whose
accumulated-attention rule is not query-aware, falls to . Deferring the
same SnapKV selection by a single decode step ( Dge 2 ) restores :
every needle, at every depth and length. The -point recovery is bought
without changing which tokens are scored or how, only when they are
dropped—the Table   2 story reproduced on a task where the

 Draft-termination fractions and the two regimes.

end, no eviction ever fires, and Dge is bit-identical to FullKV—so it is
worth asking how much of Dge ’s gain this path accounts for. Measuring
it directly in Table   16 , re-tokenizing every saved answer, shows it is a
small share: at the headline dose only of the 
samples terminate in the draft, and termination is confined to short-answer QA
( – ) and passage_count ( ), with Summarization, Few-shot and
Code at . Even at the overall figure is . The gain

 Appendix M What the Diagnostics Ruled Out 

 Each alternative below was settled by the held-out probes of
 Appendix   B in under a day of compute, before any LongBench run. We log them because the
 pattern is the point: every entry improves an in-window quantity and
fails on held-out decode behavior—the same miscalibration
§ 4 measures. 

 • 

 Repeat-prefill selection. Re-forwarding the context to harvest
“future-like” queries (the KVzip recipe, as a selection signal): 
held-out coverage with mean run length —repetition queries attend to
the locally-next token, shredding selection into singletons. Killed by the
decode-replay coverage probe. 

 • 

 Key-kernel mass-flow transport. Predicting where evicted
attention mass re-flows from a key-similarity kernel (instead of the empirical
co-attention of Appendix   C ): correlation with the true decode-time
flow . Killed by the offline transport-validation replay of
 Appendix   C . 

 • 

 Layer-only allocation. Re-allocating budget across layers
without per-head granularity collapses the achievable held-out coverage gain
from (free per-head) to : the variance that matters lives
across heads, not layers. The free per-head variant is what
 Figure   2 (a) plots on its allocate axis, under the family
label cross-layer allocation : at its coverage headroom a full
sixteen-set run scores LongBench over SnapKV, still under a point. Killed by the allocation headroom probe, which
bounds the axis as a whole: on the same held-out split a better scoring
signal is worth points of against for reallocating
the per-head budget. 

 • 

 Capped block waterfilling. Waterfilling the budget over
fixed-size blocks with per-block caps: never separated from plain top- 
within noise at matched budgets, while adding a tunable. Dropped on the
granularity ladder of Table   5 . 

 • 

 Per-token bias alone. The closed-form of
 Appendix   C without value absorption moves attention-output error by
 at —the wrong way; re-weighting retained logits cannot
restore mass whose value content is gone. Killed by the same offline replay that validates absorption
( – ).
