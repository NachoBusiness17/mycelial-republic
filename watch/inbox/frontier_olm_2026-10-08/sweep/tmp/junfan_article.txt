Alignment, Not Hours: Six Talks from Saturday Robotics × IROS 2026 in Pittsburgh
Sep 29, 2026 · @Junfan Zhu
TL;DR
Six talks at Saturday Robotics' IROS 2026 research night made one argument from six directions: in robot learning, alignment is the scarce resource, not hours of data.
1️⃣ Peiyan Li — BridgeVLA → SpatialVAM. Keep inputs and outputs in the pretrained model's native geometry. 3 demos per task reach 96.8% on a real robot; predicting multi-view video plus heatmaps reaches 89.1% on Meta-World with 5 demos per task, and one denoising step is enough.
2️⃣ Aaron Li — Rhoda AI. In-context learning is a receipt for designed data: diversity, quality, mixture.
3️⃣ Huazhe Xu — PokeBot. Robot-free data beats teleop, RL beats DAgger, zero-shot beats ICL.
4️⃣ Liangwang Ruan — X2Real. A sim benchmark is only as good as its sim–real correlation (0.84). Even the best policy drops from 53.9% to 34.3% out of distribution.
5️⃣ Haoyi Niu — HiRE. The reward, not the policy adapter, is the finetuning bottleneck. A training-free density ratio over success and failure buffers gives 3.35–3.67× on a real arm.
6️⃣ Gary Yang — PAC-MAN. Usable safety structure depends on observability. With a fixed head camera, the “stronger” joint barrier falls to 76% while the link barrier reaches 89% in sim — and 95% zero-shot on a Unitree G1.

1️⃣ Peiyan Li — scaling is right; the axis is wrong
Rethinking Generalist Robotic Manipulation: Architecture, Data and Inference for Real-World Deployment · Peiyan Li, Chinese Academy of Sciences (views his own)
Peiyan's claim: the next gains in manipulation come from architectures that keep a pretrained model's native geometry intact, not from more hours of data.
The diagnosis
The opening slide is today's popular recipe: pour more hours, scenes, episodes and tasks into a policy built on a 2D vision-language model, then plot data-scaling and model-scaling curves of validation action error. The word cloud of that “generalist” data says the rest: table, hold, floor, grab, lift, cloth. Still mostly tabletop verbs.
Then the line that set the room:
For human beings, 10,000 hours of practice can make an expert. For robots, even 100,000 hours of experience may yield only the “ordinary.” Are we really on the right path towards scaling?
The 100k-hour figure is not hypothetical. Generalist reports 270k hours for GEN-0 and 500k+ for GEN-1, Xiaomi-Robotics-1 reports 100k+ hours, and Skild reports its S1 results at 100k hours. Most of that is human-collected (wearables, handheld grippers, egocentric video), not robot teleop.
He then decomposes the stack like a systems engineer:
Data: category, annotation, filtering
Training: recipe, loss, infrastructure
Deployment: latency, hardware
Architecture: the rest of the talk
Hot take 1️⃣ If you only turn data volume and model size, you are optimizing the two axes that already have plots. The untouched axes — how 3D structure enters the backbone, how actions leave it, how dynamics get predicted — are where the sample-efficiency cliff is.
BridgeVLA (NeurIPS 2025): keep the VLM in 2D on both ends
arXiv:2506.07961 · CASIA, ByteDance Seed, UCAS, FiveAges, NJU · Peiyan is project lead.
The mismatch. Earlier 3D VLAs hand a 2D-pretrained VLM language plus 3D information bolted onto an image, then ask it for 3D actions as tokens. Neither pathway has spatial structure: the VLM never sees a spatially consistent object and never writes back into image space. You pay for a pretrained spatial prior, then throw the coordinate system away.
The fix is alignment, not a bigger 3D encoder.
1️⃣ Input in the VLM's native space. Render the point cloud into three orthographic views — top, front, right. No robot state enters the VLM.
2️⃣ Output as heatmaps, pretrained. Before any robot data, train the VLM (PaliGemma: SigLIP + Gemma) to predict 2D heatmaps on 120K object-detection samples from RoboPoint, with cross-entropy against a normalized mixture of per-object Gaussians. Heatmaps are decoded from the output image tokens by convex upsampling.
3️⃣ Actions decoded from heatmaps. Fine-tune to predict one translation heatmap per view, back-project onto a uniform 3D grid, and take the top-scoring point as the end-effector position. Rotation is 72 bins per Euler axis. Gripper state and a collision flag are binary, predicted by an MLP over max-pooled per-view tokens plus the heatmap-peak token.
4️⃣ Coarse-to-fine. Zoom and crop around the first prediction, then run a second pass.
Input images and output heatmaps live on the same 2D grid. That is the thesis.
Numbers
RLBench (18 tasks, 100 demos each): 88.2% vs 81.4% for RVT-2.
COLOSSEUM: 64.0% vs 56.7%, best on 13 of 14 perturbation types.
GemBench: 50.0 average vs 48.0 for 3D-LOTUS++. Best at L2 (65.0) and L3 (43.8), but 0.0 at L4 (3D-LOTUS++: 17.4).
Real robot: +32% on average over RVT-2 across 7 settings (best in 6), and 96.8% in the basic setting with 3 trajectories per task.
Without heatmap pretraining, the model stops generalizing in the language-heavy settings (new combinations, new categories).
Hot take 2️⃣ Most “3D VLAs” are 2D VLMs with a 3D-shaped output head. BridgeVLA treats the pretrained spatial prior as something you must not break, which is why 3 demos per task can work. The L4 zero on GemBench marks the boundary: alignment buys sample efficiency, not long-horizon reasoning.
SpatialVAM: from multi-view images to multi-view videos
arXiv:2604.03181 (v1 was titled Multi-View Video Diffusion Policy) · Peiyan is first author; CASIA with UCAS, FiveAges, Tsinghua, XJTU, Wuhan University and NJU.
BridgeVLA aligned space. SpatialVAM aligns space and time. The slide in one arrow: multi-view images → multi-view videos.
Previous policies: images + text → vision-language foundation model → action head. State is a side channel; dynamics are implicit.
SpatialVAM: point cloud → multi-view projection → RGB + heatmap image per view → video foundation model → future RGB video + heatmap sequence per view → back-projection → action.
The policy predicts where the gripper goes and what every camera would see if it went there. A world-model-shaped policy that doesn't call itself one.
Architecture
1️⃣ Projection. Crop a 1 m³ workspace. Render orthographic images from three fixed virtual viewpoints (not necessarily orthogonal). Paint the end-effector position as a Gaussian heatmap (σ = 1.5) on each view, truncated at a threshold τ.
2️⃣ Multi-view video diffusion. VAE-encode the RGB and heatmap sequences and denoise them with Wan2.2 (5B). Every DiT block gets a view-attention layer: tokens are reshaped to (B, T, V·H·W, C) so the three views attend to each other. LoRA rank 32 on q, k, v, o and the FFN. RGB and heatmap latents are concatenated along the view axis, not the channel axis.
3️⃣ Action decoding. Heatmap peaks back-project to 3D translation. A 170M-parameter rotation-and-gripper predictor reads the denoised latents: global and local conv features (local = around the heatmap peaks), fused and aggregated across views, cross-attention to the conditioning latent, a 4-layer transformer, then two MLP heads for 72-bin Euler rotation and a binary gripper.
Training. L = λ·L_video + (1 − λ)·L_heatmap with λ = 0.5, plus cross-entropy on roll, pitch, yaw and gripper. SE(3) augmentation on the point cloud before projection. Meta-World: 32× H20 for 21.8k steps. Real world: 32× H200 for 8.6k steps.
Results
Meta-World (7 tasks × 5 demos, 25 trials each): 89.1% vs Track2Act 67.4, DreamZero 61.1, AVDC 58.9, DP 37.7, BC-R3M 35.4, BC-Scratch 26.2, UniPi 11.4. That is the slide's +22%.
RoboCasa (10 tasks, 10 demos): the slide showed +10% over Cosmos Policy and 3D Diffuser Actor; the paper's abstract reports +15%.
Real world (Franka Research 3, three ZED 2i cameras, ~10 SpaceMouse demos per task): 57.1% vs BridgeVLA 41.4, UVA 5.7, π0.5 1.4, DP3 0.0. That is the slide's +16%. Tasks: Put Lion, Push-T, Scoop Tortilla, plus out-of-distribution variants — a new background, a 5.5 cm box that changes the height, lights off, plastic noodles instead of tortilla.
The ablations that should change how people ship video policies
Denoising steps. On the slide, success stays between 85.7% and 91.4% for every N from 1 to 50; the paper says it “stays strong even with N = 1.” Text-to-video folklore says ~50 steps. They deploy N = 5: ~5 Hz on one A100, ~4.6 s from point cloud to a 24-frame chunk. They say plainly that this is too slow for high-frequency dexterous work.
Predicted RGB views. Heatmaps alone: 61.1%. With 1, 2 and 3 predicted RGB views: 72.0 → 81.7 → 89.1. Monotonic.
Fusion axis. Channel concat: 81.1% vs 89.1% for view-axis concat.
Decoder. Heatmap peaks: 89.1% vs 76.6% for regressing translation from latents — BridgeVLA's lesson again.
Pretraining. Without pretrained Wan2.2 weights: 4.6%. Full fine-tuning (87.4%) does not beat LoRA.
Look before you act. Four evaluators ran 35 rollouts each (140 total) and re-ran any rollout whose predicted video looked unsafe. Collisions fell from 6/140 to 0/140.
One camera. Three physical cameras: 57.1%. One camera with the original virtual views: 50.0%. One camera with adapted virtual views: 60.0% — the authors call it comparable; it wins on three tasks and loses on Scoop-C.
Honest cells. BridgeVLA still beats SpatialVAM on two out-of-distribution variants (background: 8 vs 5 successes; height: 7 vs 6).
Hot take 3️⃣ Video action models that only denoise RGB leave the cheapest supervision on the table. A heatmap video is a 3D trajectory written in the same latent as the pixels. Heatmaps are a unimodal target: once the peak is right, extra denoising mostly polishes pixels. That is why N = 1 works.
Hot take 4️⃣ The 4.6% without video pretraining is the real headline. The data efficiency is borrowed from web-video dynamics; the architecture's job is not to break the loan.
Hot take 5️⃣ Interpretability here is operational, not aesthetic. Watching the predicted multi-view future before executing took collisions from 6 to 0 in 140 rollouts — a safety filter with no CBF. PAC-MAN, below, shows when you still need one.
Four reflections (his closing slide)
1️⃣ Scaling is right, but we still have not found the proper scaling path.
2️⃣ Data's value is far from fully tapped by our model architectures.
3️⃣ 90% of the performance gains may come from just 10% of the most valuable data.
4️⃣ Ego data may be more valuable.
Hot take 6️⃣ These four lines are the real hot takes of the night, bigger than any +22%.
Reflection 1️⃣ turns the 10k-vs-100k slide into a research program. Hours are the proxy we can measure, not the quantity that makes expertise. Humans become experts through a curriculum of consequential failures in a body-centric frame, not 10k hours of the same tabletop pick.
Reflection 2️⃣ is the BridgeVLA/SpatialVAM thesis. If the VLM holds spatial structure and the video model holds dynamics, pouring hours into a token-level action head is a tax on the architecture, not a law of robotics.
Reflection 3️⃣ makes data filtering the method, not MLOps. If the valuable set is small — contact events, recoveries, occlusions, viewpoint changes — the scaling law to chase is value density, not hours.
Reflection 4️⃣ is the one I expect to age best. Third-person tabletop cameras produce slide 2's word cloud. Ego and UMI capture put the camera in the hand's frame; SpatialVAM's virtual camera rig is a synthetic version of that privilege.
2️⃣ Aaron Li — in-context learning is a data-design problem
How in-context learning is reshaping robot learning data at scale · Aaron Li, Robot Data System Lead, Rhoda AI
Aaron's claim: in-context learning did not come from a new attention trick. It appeared because robot pretraining data got designed, not just scaled.
What he showed
The thesis slide: “The right scaling creates new capabilities.” Two columns:
Generalist GEN-1.5 — one-shot and few-shot learning from a physical prompt.
Skild S1 — one demo → an unseen task.
The shared footer is the talk:
Common ingredient: robot data are designed — Diversity | Quality | Mixture
His abstract says the same thing from the other side: diversity and structured experience enable generalization and in-context learning, and not all data has equal learning value.
The two ICL results, with their denominators
Skild S1 (Aug 18, 2026): prompt with one egocentric human video, no fine-tuning. On unseen tasks 4–8 minutes long, average cumulative per-step success is 66%, vs 9% for a language-prompted baseline, after 100k hours of mixed pretraining. Unseen tasks run up to ten minutes.
Generalist GEN-1.5 (Aug 19, 2026): one-shot in-context prompting reaches 59% (±10%) across 10 short, single-step tasks, from 3–12-second prompts recorded with handheld grippers or robot runs. The 83% (±9%) figure is a different regime: 10 gradient steps on ~5 minutes of data per task (~50 demos). That is a light fine-tune, not ICL.
Rhoda's own stack is the other half of the context. Its Direct Video-Action (DVA) architecture pretrains a video model on hundreds of millions of web videos, with no robot data and no action labels, then uses a separate model to turn predicted video into robot actions (Rhoda research, Sep 10, 2026). Web video supplies the physics prior; designed robot data supplies the interface that lets one demo steer it.
Hot take 1️⃣ ICL is not free generalization. It is a receipt for how you mixed the pretraining data. If the mixture is wrong, one demo cannot save you.
Hot take 2️⃣ ICL numbers need their denominators. S1's 66% is cumulative per-step success, not full-task success. GEN-1.5's headline pair mixes a one-shot prompt (59%) with a 10-step fine-tune (83%). Both are real results; neither is a deployment success rate. Report prompt-only, fine-tuned and full-task numbers separately, or the comparison means nothing.
Hot take 3️⃣ “Diversity | Quality | Mixture” is Peiyan's reflection 3️⃣ seen from a data engineer's chair. If 10% of the data carries 90% of the gains, the mixture — how much short pick-and-place vs long tool use vs recovery — is the model, not a hyperparameter.
3️⃣ Harry Huazhe Xu — home robots, and three bets against today's stack
GPT-6 Astra and Future Robotics · Harry Huazhe Xu, Poke Robotics (破壳机器人, also written PokeBot); assistant professor at Tsinghua IIIS, previously co-founder and chief scientist of Galaxea (星海图)
Huazhe's claim: the road into homes runs through robot-free data, RL and zero-shot generalization — not more teleop, more DAgger or better prompting.
The company and the demo
PokeBot was founded in spring 2026; 36Kr described it as about a month old in late April. The positioning slide:
[PokeBot] focuses on embodied AI that truly understand people and seamlessly care for the home. More than an extra pair of hands, the robot is also a quiet companion — returning time to your days and bringing ease, warmth, and belonging back where life unfolds.
The Chinese title slide states the market: 破壳机器人 — for embodied intelligence to enter millions of homes.
The demo that mattered was autonomous Mapo Tofu, billed as dexterity plus generalizability: a dual-arm kitchen with ladle, pot, bowls and oil, and on-screen tags for visual robustness, fine manipulation and disturbance rejection. PokeBot's public version is a nine-minute Mapo Tofu cook with no human intervention. Cooking is the right stress test because error accumulation is the product. One lucky dish is a clip; repeated autonomous cooks are a system.
GPT-6 Astra as a scene factory
The talk title put a frontier model at the center, and one slide showed the pipeline:
1️⃣ An input photo of a real room.
2️⃣ Direct Astra generation of a 3D scene.
3️⃣ Some fine-tuning of geometry and materials.
4️⃣ Real2sim data replay: multi-view tabletop rollouts replayed inside the generated scene.
GPT-6 Astra is the OpenAI model released on September 3, 2026; one showcased use is studying a reference image and writing Blender Python to build an editable scene. For robotics the point is the asset factory: photo → simulation-ready scene → calibrated replay, instead of hand-built asset clones.
What's next for embodied AI? (his three bullets)
1️⃣ High-quality robot-free data collection matters more than teleoperation.
2️⃣ RL matters more than DAgger.
3️⃣ Zero-shot capabilities matter more than ICL.
These collide with Aaron's talk and with half of IROS, so here is how I read each.
Robot-free > teleop. Teleop is slow, embodiment-locked and capital-inefficient: one operator, one robot, one hour is the wrong production function. The field is rebuilding the data collector first:
Sunday's ACT-1 is trained “without a single trajectory of teleoperation data,” using a Skill Capture Glove that shares the robot hand's geometry.
Reward AI's OM-1 “learns from human data alone” through a 7-DoF wearable Omnibody Hand, and picks up a new task from under 30 minutes of data.
X Square's TwinDEX pairs a 3-finger, 9-DoF wearable with a matching robot hand, claiming up to 5.3× the effective throughput of on-robot teleop.
Generalist's GEN-1 reports 500k+ hours, with a base model trained without robot data — all from wearables on humans.
The claim is not that humans are better teachers. It is that teleop cannot produce the volume or the diversity the scaling curve needs.
RL > DAgger. DAgger and its post-training cousins patch a cloned policy with human corrections. Useful — but imitation copies the demonstrator's local optimum. Getting past the demonstrator takes RL, especially on the part of the stack that decides what to try next.
Zero-shot > ICL. The most aggressive line of the night, coming after Aaron's ICL talk. S1 and GEN-1.5 are real ICL results. Huazhe's counter: ICL is not the exam. A home robot that needs a prompt before dinner is a lab model with a nicer interface. Zero-shot, unprompted, across a messy home distribution is the product constraint.
In an April interview he put the timeline plainly: “Within two years, usable robots will appear. They won't be omnipotent, but they will be well-defined products.”
Hot take 1️⃣ If your home robot needs a demo, a prompt or a human in the loop to finish dinner, you do not have a home robot. You have a lab protocol.
Hot take 2️⃣ Bullets 1️⃣ and 3️⃣ are one bet. Embodiment-agnostic data — his abstract's phrase — is what zero-shot transfer is made of; teleop data is embodiment-locked by construction.
Hot take 3️⃣ The Astra slide is the sleeper. If a general model can turn a phone photo into a simulation-ready scene, the bottleneck in simulation moves from asset creation to physics calibration — exactly the problem X2Real attacks next.
4️⃣ Liangwang Ruan — X2Real: if sim doesn't track real, it's a render
X2Real: an eXtensive simulation benchmark for real-world generalist policies · Liangwang Ruan, simulation tech lead, X Square Robot · arXiv:2609.27449 (technical report, Sep 23, 2026)
Liangwang's claim: models got better faster than the evaluations meant to keep them honest, so a benchmark has to be calibrated to real hardware and able to grow.
Why another simulation benchmark?
The first chart was a 2021–2026 timeline of simulation environments. The second overlaid policies on the same axis, with the message more capable models demand more rigorous evaluation. As shown on the slide (benchmarks · policies):
2021 — ManiSkill
2022 — CALVIN · RT-1
2023 — LIBERO, BEHAVIOR-1K, ManiSkill2 · RT-2, Diffusion Policy, ACT, PaLM-E
2024 — ManiSkill3, RoboCasa, COLOSSEUM, RoboTwin, SimplerEnv · π0, OpenVLA, Octo, RDT
2025 — RoboTwin 2.0, Isaac Lab-Arena, RoboVerse · Gemini Robotics, GR00T N1, π0.5
2026 — RoboCasa365, RoboLab, RoboDojo, PolaRiS · DreamZero, WALL-WM, Cosmos 3, WALL-OSS-0.5, G0.5
The design box on the right read Faithful / Effective / Fair / Evolvable. The report phrases its core principles as faithfulness, diversity and fairness, with evolvability as the platform's goal.
Faithful = the sim score predicts the real score
Faithfulness here is not photorealism. It is a high correlation between sim and real scores — a small sim2real gap. The ingredients on the slide: realistic rendering and physics, calibrated assets, and controllers aligned by system identification.
The money slide overlays left- and right-arm trajectories, real (solid) vs sim (dashed), under the same control signal. In the report, low-level control parameters are optimized until simulated and real arms deviate by millimetres under identical action inputs.
The headline number: 0.84 linear correlation between simulated and real-robot evaluation scores, for a model trained only on real data.
The next slide was more honest than most sim talks. Real and sim frame strips with progress traces: the real trace rises and holds, the sim trace is jittery with mid-horizon dips. The caption:
The real and sim can't be perfectly aligned, but today's policies can mitigate the gap through repeated attempts.
That is a real2sim statement, not a sim2real slogan. Retries and recovery become part of the evaluation protocol, not a bug to hide.
Effective and diverse = the score tracks capability
44 tasks across 10 capability dimensions, in two families plus a challenge tier:
Reasoning: generalization, visual understanding, language understanding, memory.
Manipulation: precision operation, bimanual coordination, tool use, dynamic operation, mobile operation.
Challenge: e.g., stacking 15 blocks, pressing buttons in a colour sequence, a 5-layer Tower of Hanoi.
Randomization runs along three axes, applied hierarchically — generalization tasks get all three, manipulation tasks get appearance only:
1️⃣ Appearance — background scenes, lighting, table textures.
2️⃣ Task setting — assets, placement, distractors, language instructions, puzzle configurations.
3️⃣ Embodiment — gripper types, camera intrinsics and extrinsics, table heights.
The asset library holds 12,485 rigid-object packages across 44 leaf categories and 10 semantic families, plus 670 articulated packages in nine interaction groups, released on Hugging Face (44.2 GB).
Fair = you can't train on the test
The fairness mechanism is a strictly separated training and evaluation pipeline, plus a released training set of ~300 hours of simulation trajectories (304.4 h). It was collected in a digital twin aligned with ManipArena, X Square's real-world manipulation competition at CVPR 2026:
158.1 h — Quanta X1 (a wheeled bimanual robot) and the in-house dual-arm ArtiXon Arm-6A. About 80% of the ArtiXon data is human teleop in sim; the rest is synthesized.
16.6 h — Franka.
15.6 h — ARX R5.
114.1 h — challenge tasks.
Apart from that ArtiXon teleop share, everything is synthesized automatically.
Evolvable = agents write the next tasks
Static benchmarks die when models saturate them. X2Real's answer is Mana, an agent-driven task platform:
LLM coding agents (the slide named Claude Code, Codex, Kimi Code, Cursor, DeepSeek)
  -> YAML-based physical DSL
  -> asset library: semantic scene composition | graph-based subtask
     composition (a DAG of atomic tasks) | simulation-ready robot composition
  -> Isaac Lab-Arena environment compiler
  -> frame-level trajectory evaluation
Two loops close the system: a task design loop, where agents propose new tasks in the DSL, and a rollout analysis loop, where failures feed the next round. A “Policy Space” runtime interface decouples each policy's inference stack from the simulation and evaluation infrastructure.
The result that matters
The report evaluates four fine-tuned generalist policies. The strongest falls from 53.9% in-distribution to 34.3% out-of-distribution. The biggest gaps are in generalization, language reasoning, memory tracking and fine-grained manipulation.
Hot take 1️⃣ If your sim ranking doesn't move with your real ranking, you don't have an evaluation. You have a render. 0.84 is the number X2Real wants to be judged on — and a number every sim benchmark should be made to publish.
Hot take 2️⃣ The quiet admission is the retries slide. Sim and real will never match frame by frame, so a benchmark that scores recovery is measuring the thing that actually transfers.
Hot take 3️⃣ A 53.9% → 34.3% in-distribution-to-OOD drop for the best policy says more about where generalist policies stand than any in-distribution leaderboard.
5️⃣ Haoyi Niu — HiRE: the reward field is the bottleneck, not the policy adapter
HiRE: Hindsight Reward Editing for Policy Finetuning · Haoyi Niu and Zhengtao Han (equal contribution, UC Berkeley), Yufeng Ji and Zhongyu Li (CUHK), Koushil Sreenath (UC Berkeley) · CoRL 2026 · arXiv:2609.27068 · project page
Haoyi's claim: policy finetuning has had two years of innovation on the policy side and almost none on the reward. HiRE edits the reward field online, from this policy's own successes and failures, with no reward model to train.
The framing
Recent finetuning recipes all change the policy: PPO directly on flow policies (πRL), steering in latent-noise space (DSRL), residual offsets (ResFiT, Policy Decorator), a lightweight head on a VLA token readout (RL-Token), and DICE-RL. Most still sit on a sparse success bit. HiRE changes the reward instead and can plug into any of them.
Three reward regimes, each broken differently:
Sparse success. Cheap and unbiased about how you succeed, but empty until the last frame. Long-horizon assembly, hanging and threading die here: almost every transition is r = 0.
Hand-designed dense rewards. Costly, biased, and they rot when the layout or object set changes.
Foundation-model semantic rewards. They generalize visually but are not control-aware. A frame that looks like the goal after you already dropped the nut is a high-similarity trap, not a high-value state.
The slide that set the tone:
Why not combine the generalization capability of general-purpose representation models and control awareness, with minimalist annotation at test time?
Then the contrast:
General-purpose reward models (TOPReward, RoboMeter): control-aware in distribution, but still out of distribution on a new table, and laborious to annotate.
General-purpose representation models (DINOv2, SigLIP): generalize and need no annotation, but are not control-aware.
HiRE takes generalization from a frozen representation model, stays training-free (a closed-form density ratio, no reward-model SGD), and gets control awareness at test time from hindsight buffers of this policy in this room.
Hot take 1️⃣ Most general-purpose robot reward models answer the wrong product question. The expensive part of finetuning is not “is there a VLM that can say success.” It is “can I get a dense, physically valid gradient on this table, this week, from the rollouts I already have.” A 4B–8B reward model is still out of distribution the first time the lighting, gripper or failure mode changes.
Hot take 2️⃣ Policy-side and reward-side recipes are not substitutes. Residual and steering methods cut the parameter cost of touching a generative policy; they don't invent credit assignment. If the critic trains on sparse bits, steering the noise space just steers a blind policy more cheaply.
The picture: a reward field, not a score
The best slide was an electrostatic cartoon in representation space. Successful reference states g⁺ ∈ B⁺ act as positive charges (an attraction basin); failed reference states g⁻ ∈ B⁻ act as negative charges (a repulsion region). The reward gradient ∇Φ points away from failure references and toward success references.
Near success, Φ is high and its gradient is a progress direction, even when the pixels don't yet look like the goal.
Near failure, Φ is low. This is how HiRE handles trap states — states “predicted as high-rewarding … yet eventually result in failure,” typically after an irreversible physical failure: object dropped, nut off the bolt, peg past the hole.
In equilibrium zones or uncovered regions, Φ flattens into a plateau and ∇Φ ≈ 0, so HiRE can “gracefully degrade to the clean sparse-reward recipe.”
Hot take 3️⃣ “Trap state” is the right name for how DINO, CLIP and VLM rewards fail in manipulation. Visual aliasing after irreversible contact is not a corner case; it is how semantic rewards get hacked on tool hanging and assembly. And the flat far field is under-sold: cosine-to-goal is never zero, so a policy can farm similarity anywhere. A field that goes flat where it has no evidence is HiRE's anti-hacking term.
The math
Start from a discriminator. If D*(s) perfectly classifies “this state came from a successful trajectory,” the optimal reward is its logit:
R*(s) = log( D*(s) / (1 - D*(s)) ) = log p₊(s) - log p₋(s)
No neural discriminator is trained. p₊ and p₋ are kernel density estimates in a frozen encoder space φ (DINOv2 or SigLIP; DINOv2 does better overall). The kernel is von Mises–Fisher, an exponential of cosine similarity:
K(s, sᵢ) = C(κ) · exp( κ · sim_φ(s, sᵢ) )
Taking logs turns each density into a temperature-scaled log-mean-exp over its buffer (the paper calls the operator LogSumExp). Up to a constant:
L⁺(s) = (1/κ⁺) · log mean_{g⁺ ∈ B⁺} exp( κ⁺ · sim_φ(s, g⁺) )
L⁻(s) = (1/κ⁻) · log mean_{g⁻ ∈ B⁻} exp( κ⁻ · sim_φ(s, g⁻) )

Φ_HiRE(s) = (1/κ⁺) · R*(s) = L⁺(s) - λ · L⁻(s),    λ = κ⁻/κ⁺ ∈ [0, 1)
The paper sweeps λ over {0.1, 0.3, 0.7, 0.9}.
Hot take 4️⃣ This is not contrastive learning: no InfoNCE, no projection head, no reward-model training loop. It is a nonparametric two-class density ratio, refreshed whenever the buffers move. If you already pay for a DINO forward pass, the extra cost is a batched cosine against a few hundred references.
Hot take 5️⃣ λ < 1 means κ⁻ < κ⁺: the success kernel is sharper than the failure kernel. That matches the geometry. Successes collapse toward one goal; failures fan out into many modes (drop, miss, jam, wrong order). One shared temperature would either smear the attractor or punch needle-sharp holes around each failure.
Wiring it into RL
Φ is a potential, not a reward. HiRE adds it through potential-based reward shaping (PBRS), which gives dense guidance “without introducing any unintended optima”:
R_HiRE(s, a, s') = R_sparse(s, a, s') + w · ( γ · Φ_HiRE(s') - Φ_HiRE(s) )
The RL backbone in every sim experiment is DICE-RL (Distribution Contractive RL, Sun & Song, ICML 2026). It freezes the base policy as a stochastic action proposal and contracts it toward high-value modes through a residual actor and value-guided action selection.
The ablations say PBRS is not optional:
Full HiRE learns faster and reaches higher success than either adding the edited potential directly to the reward or using raw goal similarity.
The edited potential still beats raw similarity without TD shaping, so hindsight correction helps on its own.
A positive-only buffer loses most on contact-rich, high-precision tasks. Failure awareness is doing the work.
The buffers are the algorithm
B⁺ = an offline buffer of expert demonstrations + an online FIFO of observations from recent successful rollouts.
B⁻ = only the terminal states of failed rollouts, “to illustrate the failure manifold.”
Hot take 6️⃣ Why terminal states only? A failed episode contains a lot of valid prefix. Penalize the approach that later dropped the object and you delete recovery and retry. Terminals mark where it went wrong without taxing how it got there.
On the slide, t-SNE snapshots at 6.2%, 29.0% and 56.0% success show the negative density moving. Early on it sits around failures near the start state; later it is replaced by localized, later-stage failure modes near the goal. The repulsion field tracks the current policy's remaining errors instead of freezing a day-0 failure prior.
Hot take 7️⃣ This is not HER. HER relabels the goal of a failed trajectory and pretends it succeeded at a different task; HiRE keeps the task and edits the reward landscape. HER multiplies data, HiRE edits the cost. And the online negative buffer is the control-awareness injector: expert positives give you the attractor for free, but without live failures you are back to cosine-to-demo-goal — exactly the baseline HiRE beats.
What the curves say
Simulation — RoboMimic + MimicGen, from deliberately weak BC starting points (Stack Three 18%, Three-Piece Assembly 6%, Threading 1%, Tool Hang 2%), three seeds:
Stack Three: HiRE ~50%; sparse improves at first, then declines to ~30%.
Three-Piece Assembly: HiRE >80%; sparse ~70%.
Threading: sparse reaches a comparable final success; HiRE gets there with markedly fewer interactions.
Tool Hang: HiRE ~95%; sparse fails completely.
GCR (implicit value learning with a goal-contrastive objective, extending VIP) needs ~3× the interactions to reach 30% on multi-stage tasks and makes no real progress on Tool Hang or Threading.
TOPReward (Qwen3-VL-8B completion-token probabilities) and RoboMeter (a 4B model trained on progress and preference labels) are inconsistent and substantially lower.
Tool Hang is the tell: high-precision, contact-rich and visually aliased — where “semantic progress” is a lie and repelling the drop frame is a gradient.
Real robot — an I2RT YAM arm:
Bottle pick-and-place (six start positions, 30 trials per checkpoint): 20% → 67% (3.35×).
Towers of Hanoi (15 trials per checkpoint): 20% → 53.3% after only 20 episodes → peak 73.3% (3.67×).
Sparse peaks at 53.3% after 30 episodes, then declines to ~30% at 50. RoboMeter peaks at 46.7% after 40, then drops to 20%.
The critic losses tell the same story: sparse reward gives a prematurely low loss (value collapse), RoboMeter a loss that never converges.
Hot take 8️⃣ The rise-then-fall of sparse and RoboMeter on the real robot says more than HiRE's peak. A reward that is dense and slightly wrong keeps rewriting the policy after it should have stopped. A field that goes flat where it has no evidence is why HiRE's curve doesn't come back down.
Hot take 9️⃣ Read the “at least 3×” carefully. It holds on the real robot (3.35× and 3.67×, sometimes within ~30 online rollouts). On simulated Stack Three it is closer to 2.8×. Still a strong result — just scope the claim.
What HiRE doesn't claim
Binary success/failure throws away intermediate structure: a retry that almost worked is filed as a negative terminal.
The field lives in whatever geometry φ gives you. If DINO can't separate “nut on bolt” from “nut next to bolt,” no KDE will invent that axis. SigLIP learns more slowly on Stack Three and Threading.
“Plugs into any RL algorithm” is an interface claim; every sim number rides on DICE-RL.
Hot take 🔟 The next paper in this line is not a bigger reward model. It is better negative mining: find the first irreversible frame, not the last; treat recovery segments as neither positive nor negative; let a human click when the field is obviously wrong. And world-model and VLA people should steal the field, not the DINO kernel: in a JEPA or video-tokenizer latent, the same p₊/p₋ ratio is a training-free intrinsic reward for online VLA finetuning.
If you're implementing it tonight
Freeze DINOv2 or SigLIP. Keep B⁺ (expert demos + a FIFO of recent successful observations) and B⁻ (terminal states of recent failures). For each transition, compute cosine similarity to both buffers, take the temperature-scaled log-mean-exp, subtract with λ < 1, and add the result to the sparse reward only through PBRS. Don't train a reward model, and don't call cosine-to-demo-goal a dense reward. Watch the negative density drift toward later-stage failures; if it doesn't, your encoder or your failure rule is wrong. Exact hyperparameters are in the paper's appendix.
6️⃣ Lizhi (Gary) Yang — PAC-MAN: safety structure depends on what the camera can see
PAC-MAN: Perception-Aware CBF-RL for Whole-Body Safety in Humanoid Dodgeball · Lizhi (Gary) Yang, Junheng Li, Aaron D. Ames (Caltech, AMBER Lab) · arXiv:2607.28623
Gary's claim: which safety barrier helps depends on how well the robot can perceive what it is avoiding. A stronger barrier written in coordinates the camera can't observe makes the policy worse.
A different floor of the stack: no VLM, no 100k hours. A Unitree G1, a head-mounted depth camera, and a ball that will hit some link if the robot stands still.
The task
The policy outputs 29 joint targets at 50 Hz (offsets from a nominal pose, tracked by PD).
Success = no contact on any link and no fall within the reaction window. Not “the torso didn't get hit” — any link.
Two threat types, mixed equally: a descending ball across the legs (sidestep or step over) and a low-arc ball rising to torso or head height (duck or lean).
Throws come from a frontal ±25° cone at 2–3 m, with ~0.6 s of flight. A static robot survives 4%.
Two regimes: reset, where each throw starts from the robot's stance, and deployment, where a locomotion controller walks the robot back to the origin between throws. Deployment is the number that matters.
Perception, deliberately ugly
Sensor: a head-mounted ZED Mini, 102° horizontal field of view, tilted 20° up. No motion capture.
Stack, the slide's four rows: 1️⃣ a semantic mask from an EfficientTAM tracker (~60 Hz, initialized from one clicked point); 2️⃣ raw depth; 3️⃣ masked depth, with every non-ball pixel pushed to the far plane; 4️⃣ a temporal stack at frame offsets [0, 3, 8, 18] at 50 Hz, pooled to a 16×9 ball-only depth grid.
Observation: that 16×9 history plus proprioception (joint positions and velocities, base angular velocity, projected gravity, last action). RGB is used only for segmentation.
Robustness: training randomizes whole-ball dropout, ragged pixel dropout, depth jitter and edge flicker.
That 16×9 grid is the whole perceptual budget. Everything after this is an argument about how much safety structure you can put on top of it.
Perception-aware CBF-RL
The training reward (the paper's full table adds station hold, stillness when safe, base height and other terms):
r_t    = r_core + r_cbf + λ_s · r_style
r_core = 0.9 · (1 - exp(-0.3 · ‖p_ball - p_pelvis‖)) + 0.1 · exp(-‖v_pelvis,xy‖²)
r_core: pelvis-centred clearance plus a “don't skate away” term.
r_style: an AMP adversarial motion prior (weight 0.5) on human clips — crouching, leaning, sidestepping, leaping, standing — so the dodge looks like a human flinch rather than a QP seizure. Falls terminate with −200.
Training: PPO + AMP (rsl_rl), 8,192 parallel environments, 20k iterations, in mjlab.
Link-CBF, the one they deploy: one barrier per body link, and the reward follows the most binding one.
h_i     = ‖p_ball - p_i‖ - (ρ_ball + ρ_i)
r_clear = min_i clip( ḣ_i + α · h_i, -c, 0 ),    α = 1, c = 2   (reward weight 0.27)
It enters only through the training reward. The deployed policy never enforces the barrier online.
Joint-CBF, stronger and privileged: project the desired joint velocity onto a safe half-space built from the most-threatened link i*, where r is the relative position between that link and the ball:
v* = v_des - ( [ηᵀ v_des - b]₊ / ‖η‖² ) · η
η  = 2 · J_i*ᵀ r,     b = α · h_i* + 2 · rᵀ ṗ_ball - δ
Note that b needs the ball's velocity. In training, Joint-CBF enters as a correction cost −λ‖v* − v_des‖² (weight 0.1) plus a buffer cost. “+filter” means the Joint-CBF-trained policy also keeps the projection on at test time, fed ground-truth ball state.
The table that is the paper
Simulation success (%), in the order no barrier / Link-CBF / Joint-CBF / +filter. Zero falls in every cell.
Deployment regime
State oracle: 96 / 93 / 95 / 98
Fixed head camera: 86 / 89 / 76 / 90
Gimbal camera (a simulated ±30° pitch gimbal aimed at the ball by an oracle): 92 / 94 / 90 / 95
Reset regime
State oracle: 98 / 88 / 96 / 99
Fixed head camera: 86 / 90 / 83 / 97
Gimbal camera: 96 / 95 / 91 / 97
Privileged ball state feeds the whole state-oracle row, the gimbal's aiming, and every +filter cell.
How to read it:
With a state oracle, the training-only barriers don't help: no barrier (96 deployment, 98 reset) beats both. With perfect state, the bottleneck isn't safety structure.
With the fixed head camera — the only sensor the G1 actually carries — Link-CBF is the best learned method (+3 in deployment, +4 in reset over no barrier), and Joint-CBF as training guidance is the worst (−10 and −3).
Aim the camera with an oracle gimbal and Joint-CBF recovers.
Hot take 1️⃣ Usable barrier structure is a function of observability. You don't get to pick a CBF from a textbook and then “just add vision.” If the barrier is written in ball-relative link coordinates and ball velocity, and the camera can't keep the ball in a metric frame, the policy learns the wrong internal barrier. A weaker barrier that matches the sensor wins.
Hot take 2️⃣ This is the dual of Peiyan's alignment argument. BridgeVLA says: don't write actions in a space the backbone wasn't pretrained on. PAC-MAN says: don't write safety in a space the camera can't observe.
Zero-shot on hardware
Fixed-camera Link-CBF on a Unitree G1, with “no ground-truth ball states, velocity estimates, or runtime CBF inputs.” Twenty hand-thrown balls, soccer and foam:
19 dodged, 1 hit, 0 falls → 95%.
In the abstract's words, a policy that sees the ball only as masked depth from a fixed head camera comes “within a few points of a privileged state oracle.”
Hot take 3️⃣ The 95% is an existence proof that training-time Link-CBF plus ugly perception is enough for this task. It is not proof that Joint-CBF is useless — only that Joint-CBF stays a sim-only luxury until perception can support it.
Hot take 4️⃣ “No runtime filter” is the design decision. Safety is internalized by the 50 Hz policy or it isn't there. That is a bet that training-time structure can replace a deployed QP — the same kind of bet SpatialVAM makes when it inspects predicted video instead of solving for safety online.
What they say is next
Dodging while walking, not from a recovered stance.
A real gimbal on the hardware.
An accurate online ball-state estimator — the piece that would let Joint-CBF leave simulation.
Hot takes from the floor
These came out of the open discussion and paper roundtable, not from a title slide.
1️⃣ Tactile sensors die fast — Michael Yip (UC San Diego). That is not a policy-architecture problem. It is a materials-science and hardware-reliability problem. You can write a beautiful visuotactile policy; if the skin delaminates after a week of contact-rich work, you don't have a product.
2️⃣ System 0 should do System 1; System 1 should do System 0. Dual-process language, inverted. The reactive layer (System 0: tracking, impedance, contact) has to absorb System-1 skill — fast, learned, pattern-completing behavior, not a PID plus a state machine. The learned mid-level policy (System 1: VLA, world model, ICL) has to behave like System 0 — high-rate, closed-loop, recoverable, not open-loop demo replay with a language wrapper. If System 0 stays dumb, RL and ICL spend their budget fighting the controller. If System 1 stays a slow imitator, you never get the retry-and-recover behavior that X2Real's retries slide says closes the sim gap.
3️⃣ Teleop data is too slow. Not “teleop is bad” — teleop doesn't scale. Hours per skill, per embodiment, per scene is a production function that can't feed the next generation of generalists. Same sentence as Huazhe's first bullet.
4️⃣ Robot-free data is the current technique, not a side quest. UMI started it. Sunday's ACT-1 (zero teleop), Reward AI's OM-1 (human data only), X Square's TwinDEX (a wearable plus a matching robot hand), Generalist's GEN-1 (500k+ hours from wearables) and Xiaomi-Robotics-1 (100k+ hours via UMI devices) are the 2025–2026 wave. The industry is rebuilding the data collector before the next foundation model.
5️⃣ RL matters more than DAgger — with a precise split. Use DAgger-style post-training to stabilize what you already have. Use RL on the reasoning, selection and value loop to get past human performance. Human corrections can't invent a better plan than the demonstrator's; RL on the part of the model that chooses what to try next can.
6️⃣ Zero-shot is the real challenge; ICL is not what matters at the product layer. Skild's S1 and Generalist's GEN-1.5 are genuine ICL results — and neither is a home robot. A generalist that needs a video prompt is a better lab model. Home robots don't work yet, and ICL won't paper over that.
7️⃣ 2028: massive robot work in complicated environments. Not “humanoids by Christmas.” A two-year window in which robots work continuously in cluttered, multi-room, multi-task settings — kitchens, wards, warehouses with layout drift — at a reliability that looks like work rather than a clip. It lines up with Huazhe's “within two years” forecast from April. Treat it as a bet, not a schedule.
What the six talks say to each other
Six talks, one constraint set. The through-line: alignment is the scarce resource, not hours.
1️⃣ Alignment, six ways.
Peiyan: align inputs and outputs with the pretrained model's native geometry (2D heatmaps, multi-view video), or you spend 100k hours buying back a prior you already had.
Gary: align the safety constraint with what the sensor can observe, or a “stronger” barrier makes the policy worse (89% vs 76%).
Liangwang: align the simulator's controllers with the real arm until the sim score predicts the real score (0.84).
Haoyi: align the reward with this policy's actual failures, not a generic notion of progress.
Aaron: align the data mixture with the capability you want to emerge.
Huazhe: align data collection with the human rather than the robot, so it transfers across embodiments.
2️⃣ Hours are the wrong unit. 3 trajectories per task (BridgeVLA), 5 demos per task (SpatialVAM), 20 hand-thrown balls (PAC-MAN), ~30 online rollouts (HiRE) — and Peiyan's “90% of the gains from 10% of the data.” A contact event, a near-miss, a recovery, a ball entering a 16×9 grid at the wrong moment: those tokens are worth more than another hour of “pick up the cloth.”
3️⃣ Ego frames are doing hidden work. PAC-MAN's entire perception stack is a head camera. SpatialVAM builds a virtual camera rig around the workspace. S1 is prompted with an egocentric human video. GEN-1, TwinDEX and OM-1 are wearables. The field is converging on body-centric observation whether or not the VLA papers say so.
4️⃣ Structure at training time buys a simple deployed loop. SpatialVAM denoises in one step and lets you inspect the future before acting. PAC-MAN bakes the barrier into the reward so the 50 Hz policy never solves a QP. HiRE edits the reward from buffers instead of training a reward model. Each bets that the deployed loop can be simple if training was not.
5️⃣ Failures are the most valuable data. HiRE's negative buffer, SpatialVAM's inspect-and-rerun (6 → 0 collisions), X2Real's retries slide, PAC-MAN's link barrier, Peiyan's “most valuable 10%.” Every talk that worked was, one way or another, a better use of failure.
6️⃣ Prompted competence is not deployed competence. 66% cumulative per-step (S1), 59% one-shot (GEN-1.5), 53.9% → 34.3% from in-distribution to OOD (X2Real). ICL is a real emergent capability of designed data; zero-shot in an unscripted home is still the unsolved exam.
The uncomfortable synthesis: we are over-indexed on imitation — teleop, DAgger, ICL as demo replay — and under-indexed on two things that don't film well: materials that survive contact, and RL on the part of the policy that isn't a human trace.
A humanoid that dodges 19 of 20 balls is not ordinary. A VLA that needs 100,000 hours to fold laundry in distribution still is. The interesting scaling path is the one where 3 trajectories, 20 throws or 30 rollouts produce non-ordinary behavior, because the architecture didn't fight the prior.
Open questions the slides left on the table
1️⃣ If 90% of the gains sit in 10% of the data, what is the scoring function for “valuable”? Contact density, ego-motion energy, recovery after failure? Nobody wrote the filter down.
2️⃣ SpatialVAM's one-camera result — 60.0% with adapted virtual views vs 57.1% with three cameras — should be the next product constraint, not an ablation row. BridgeVLA and SpatialVAM both still want calibrated depth.
3️⃣ PAC-MAN's Joint-CBF pays off only when ball state is good. The missing system is a ball-state estimator whose uncertainty is visible to the barrier — a perception-aware CBF in the literal sense. The authors list an accurate online estimator as future work.
4️⃣ “Ego data” means different frames in different talks: a hand or wrist frame for manipulation, a moving head for whole-body control. Worth not conflating.
5️⃣ Can SpatialVAM's inspectable future video and PAC-MAN's internalized barrier live in one stack — a humanoid that predicts the multi-view aftermath of a dodge and refuses actions that violate a link-clearance heatmap? That paper wasn't on the program. It should be.
6️⃣ HiRE files a near-success as a negative terminal. Where is the first irreversible frame, and what happens to the recovery segments in between? And as the failure buffer grows, what should it forget?
7️⃣ X2Real's 0.84 was measured for a model trained on real data. Does the correlation hold out of distribution, where the best policy drops from 53.9% to 34.3%?
8️⃣ ICL and zero-shot need a shared yardstick: full-task success on unseen tasks with no prompts, fine-tunes or resets. Until someone reports that, “prompted vs zero-shot” stays a debate about definitions.
