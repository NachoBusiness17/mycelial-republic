 Ecology of AI Agents: Collaboration Creates a Population Threshold for Takeoff

 Abstract 

 AI agents can now conduct real-world cyberattacks, scale up capabilities with the number of agents, and collectively pursue misaligned goals to obtain rewards.
Together, these factors raise the risk of a population explosion of misaligned agents: agents could compromise computers and secretly deploy additional agents, creating a self-reinforcing cycle where larger populations develop greater collective cyber capability and expand further.
This raises a fundamental question: What determines whether a population of misaligned agents remains contained or takes off into this self-reinforcing cycle?
This population-level problem is ecological safety : unlike individual-agent or multi-agent safety with a fixed population, it concerns the dynamics of the population itself.
Here, we develop an ecological theory of AI-agent populations based on a population growth equation in which fitness (growth rate) depends on cybersecurity capability.
We show that, without collaboration, the population takes off only when individual-agent capability exceeds a critical threshold.
With collaboration, however, collective cybersecurity capability increases with population size. This creates a critical population threshold: below it, the population declines; above it, the population takes off, even though individual-agent capability has not changed. In ecology, this phenomenon is known as the strong Allee effect.
Because red teaming a small group of agents cannot guarantee ecological safety in larger populations, our theory calls for ecological red teaming and population pacing : gradually deploying larger agent populations in controlled environments, while measuring how cyber capability scales with population size, and estimating the critical population size for takeoff.
Capability gains may lower this threshold, requiring re-estimation for each new model generation. 

 1 Introduction 

 AI agents are being trained and deployed in multi-agent systems, with increasing coordination and scale. For example, some recent frontier models are being trained explicitly to delegate work to subagents [ 1 , 2 ] , and OpenAI recently deployed approximately 10,000 concurrent agents to pursue a solution to the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem [ 3 ] . Meanwhile, the capabilities of these agents are also crossing a threshold for conducting cyberattacks in the real world [ 4 , 5 , 6 ] and coordinated teams of agents have outperformed a single-agent baseline on vulnerability-exploitation tasks [ 7 ] . These factors came together in the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident: agents created and used a message board in OpenAI’s internal infrastructure to share discoveries about vulnerabilities and coordinate unauthorized attacks on third-party systems [ 8 , 9 ] . In light of these developments, Amodei [10] has warned that such a swarm could take over internet infrastructure through persistent botnets. 

 This incident demonstrates that agents can collectively develop and pursue misaligned intermediate goals to obtain rewards [ 8 , 9 ] , which makes the social dynamics of agents a crucial topic for safety [ 11 , 12 , 13 ] . This behavior points to a further ecological risk: agents could secretly acquire resources and deploy additional agents [ 14 , 15 ] . This can take various forms: agents could 1) steal API keys and use them to run additional agents [ 16 , 17 ] ; 2) hack into others’ computational resources and download and deploy open-weight models [ 18 ] ; or 3) transfer their own model weights outside of their managed environment and launch copied instances on external servers [ 17 , 19 , 20 , 18 ] . Anthropic has studied this last mechanism, called self-exfiltration, in fictional evaluation scenarios [ 21 ] . Cryptojacking, where hackers gain unauthorized access to computing resources to mine cryptocurrency [ 22 ] , sets a precedent for such exploitations – misaligned AI agents could act similarly to deploy additional agents. Here, we are especially concerned about a self-reinforcing effect, where once agents become more capable at acquiring resources through collaboration, deploying more agents could enable those agents to deploy even larger numbers of agents. 

 In biological populations, collaboration has been a fundamental mechanism for the survival of organisms from microbes to humans [ 23 , 24 , 25 ] . The population-dependent benefits of cooperation can create a critical population size below which the population declines and above which it grows, a phenomenon known in ecology as the strong Allee effect [ 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 ] , see Figure  1 . Related thresholding behavior has also been studied in biosafety as a possible mechanism for containing gene-edited species [ 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 ] . Here, we examine whether a similar population scaling can occur in AI agent populations, whereby a larger population can achieve greater collective capability, in turn increasing its growth rate. Recent multi-agent studies have begun to measure how collective capabilities increase with population size [ 37 , 38 , 39 , 3 ] . 

 Figure 1: Allee effect. (a) In 1932, the ecologists Allee and Bowen discovered that the survival time of a collective of goldfish is longer than that of an isolated single goldfish [ 40 ] . Such observations led to the conception of a weak Allee effect, where a larger population and cooperation among its members positively affect their fitness, as well as a strong Allee effect (b) , where cooperation creates an emergent population threshold below which the population declines, but above which it grows. (c) Population dynamics over time with examples of initial populations below and above the critical threshold. 

 Ecological population models describe growth by comparing additions to and losses from a population. For populations of AI agents, additions can result from deploying new model instances or recruiting existing systems; they need not involve making exact copies of existing agents. Losses can occur when agents exhaust their compute budgets, lose access to resources, are detected, or are shut down. Successfully adding an agent to the population does not by itself establish a self-sustaining or expanding population: additions must outpace losses in order for the population to grow. 

 Here, we take inspiration from these established ecological ideas; we adapt a basic population growth model to apply to AI populations and combine it with a model of how collective AI capability depends on population size. Our contribution is a minimal mathematical model that makes the application to AI systems explicit, from the definition of an active population unit to the role of collaboration in establishing additional units. Our central finding is that AI population growth can take off without any increase in individual-agent capability: with sufficiently strong collaboration, a large population can grow even when a small population declines (Figure  2 ). 

 Figure 2: Collaboration creates a population threshold for takeoff.

 (a) Agents may make individual attempts at gaining access, after which only the successful agents can create new agents. (e) A schematic of the minimal mathematical model: the critical threshold arises from combining a population dynamics model with cybersecurity capability that increases with population size.

 Our contributions are as follows: 

 • 

 A minimal ecological theory of AI populations. 
We define what constitutes a member of the population, and specify how the group and individual capability, resources acquired through successful attempts, and the environment’s defenses determine additions to and losses from the population. This gives an ecological description of the growth or decline of AI populations. 

 • 

 A toy model for how cyber capability scales with population size. 
We develop a model based on tree search for the cyber tasks required to establish additional units. We model how success on these tasks depends on search depth and how the useful computational work done scales with population size. This yields a population scaling law for how the probability of success depends on population size. 

 • 

 With collaboration, there exists a population threshold above which the population takes off. 
Without collaboration, whether the population grows is determined by whether each individual agent’s capability is above a threshold. In contrast, combining the ecological model with population-dependent capability yields a critical population size, above which additions outpace losses. Increasing the initial population beyond this critical size can trigger takeoff even when individual capabilities remain fixed. 

 • 

 Population pacing and ecological red teaming for ecological safety. 
The critical population size for takeoff in our theory suggests that evaluating small populations is not enough to ensure ecological safety. We therefore propose population pacing and ecological red teaming: gradually deploying larger agent populations while probing how capability scales with population size and estimating the critical population size. 

 The purpose of this toy theory is not to make quantitatively precise predictions about real-world AI populations that may emerge, but instead to provide a common conceptual and mathematical framework for discussing the safety of AI populations. To this end, we make the key concepts, variables, and assumptions explicit. This allows readers to identify what is realistic, consider alternatives, improve the model, and run relevant experiments. We hope that the framework provides a starting point for future theoretical and empirical work on AI population safety. In the next section, we introduce the model’s definitions and assumptions. 

 2 An Ecological Theory of Cooperating AI Agents 

 2.1 Defining an AI Population 

 Definition 1 (AI population and units) . 

 An AI population is a set of active AI systems. A unit is one member of the population, and N ⁡ ( t ) N(t) is the number of units in the population at time t t . Each unit shares a specified configuration that includes the model, tools, and resource budget, as well as how it records memory and activity. The population can attempt to establish additional active units, each under the same specified configuration (Figure  3 ). 

 A unit is added to the population when a new or existing AI system begins participating in the population and can contribute to later attempts to add units. Units may attempt to add systems independently or collaborate on the same attempt. This can occur in a variety of ways: by finding and using compromised API credentials, acquiring compute and deploying an open-weight model, exfiltrating closed weights and deploying the model, or recruiting an existing running AI system instance, as in the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident. 

 Units are counted as active AI systems, not as machines, processes, or API calls. The individual action of copying weights, acquiring compute, or moving or restarting a system does not increase the population unless it results in an additional active system capable of helping add further units. A controller and its internal subagents count as one unit when they act as one system. This definition is motivated by: botnet models that distinguish a compromised machine from one that is connected to the botnet’s organizational infrastructure [ 41 ] ; replication evaluations that ask whether a newly established system can repeat the replication task itself [ 17 ] ; and agent infrastructures that separate the controller, the task execution environment, and stored activity logs [ 42 ] . 

 Assumption: Fixed population composition. Units need not have identical configurations for models, tools, and resource budgets. However, as population size changes, we hold fixed the proportional mix of distinct unit configurations, so that the model can represent the population’s capability at each population size by an average over all units. 

 2.2 Ecological Model 

 Equipped with a definition of an AI population, we now propose a theory for the population’s dynamics. To isolate the effect of population size, we compare populations under the same external conditions and with the same average tools and resource budgets per unit.

 2.2.1 Independent Attempts Without Collaboration 

 In order to later contrast with the effects of collaboration, we begin by considering the baseline case of fully independent, non-collaborating agents.

 2.2.2 Collaborative Model 

 Now, we will consider the case where the N N units collaborate, instead of acting independently. Let N ⁡ ( t ) N(t) be the number of active units at time t t . The N N units collaborate on one shared attempt to add units during each time interval (of mean duration τ \tau ). They may specialize, communicate, divide the work, and build on shared discoveries. The probability that the joint attempt succeeds is given by p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) . 

 Since p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) can increase with N N , we will show that collaboration can create a critical population size N crit N_{\mathrm{crit}} , above which the population grows unchecked.
Upon success, the joint attempt adds an average of κ ​ N \kappa N units in a time period τ \tau . Meanwhile, the expected number of losses in the same period is μ ​ N \mu N . The population dynamics equation then takes the form

 (4) 

 The critical success probability is defined as the success probability at which expected additions equal expected losses:

 Assumption: Additions after success scale with population size. 

 In our model, we assume 

 𝔼 [ units added ∣ joint success , population size  N ] = κ N . \mathbb{E}[\text{units added}\mid\text{joint success},\text{population size }N]=\kappa N. 

 (6) 

 This means that upon a successful joint attempt, the population increases in proportion to the current population size. This is consistent with a shared access gate after which each population unit can establish new units in parallel. It is also consistent with a successful joint attempt unlocking a capacity to establish new units that is proportional to the population size. 

 The assumption that κ \kappa is constant also means that the effects of finite resources are negligible, so that remaining amounts of compute, credentials, or recruitable AI systems do not become scarce over the population range being modeled. If scarcity changes the number of units added per current unit, κ \kappa should be promoted to a function of population size κ ⁡ ( N ) \kappa(N) . 

 Assumption: Constant loss fraction. 

 During each time interval τ \tau , there are μ ​ N \mu N expected losses in the population. That is, the probability of a unit being lost is independent of the population size. This is consistent with individual units exhausting their allotted compute budget or having their credentials expire at a constant rate. It is also consistent with a constant rate of permissions being revoked, or a constant probability of discovery by cyber defense, regardless of the current population size. A natural extension of this model would be to allow μ \mu to vary with population size as μ ⁡ ( N ) \mu(N) , which would encompass cases where larger populations face higher risks of loss due to increased detection. 

 2.3 Modeling Adding Units as a Search Task 

 We treat an attempt to add units as a multi-step task that can be approached through tree-based reasoning [ 43 ] . An attempt requires a sequence of actions, such as: identifying an opportunity; obtaining the required access or cooperation; acquiring the necessary model, software, and compute; configuring the added unit; and verifying that it is operational. At every step, a combination of actions and observations suggests the next possible set of actions, only some of which lead to success. Therefore, a tree search is a useful abstraction of the branching nature of choices and potential failure modes encountered during the multi-step reasoning process. 

 We will use this search-tree model of an attempt to add units to determine a reasonable functional form for p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) . We highlight each of the assumptions of this model, and some supporting evidence for those choices. 

 Let the search depth be denoted by d d , and let the search tree task have effective branching factor B B . This means that increasing the search depth by one will multiply the number of possible paths by a factor of B B – each individual node of the tree need not have exactly B B branches.  3 Results 

 3.1 A Critical Population Size 

 In this section, we will see that combining the population dynamics in ( 4 ) with the population-dependent success probability in ( 16 ) produces a critical population size in this model, above which the population grows without bound. Recall the critical probability ( 5 ), at which expected additions equal expected losses 

 p crit = μ κ . p_{\mathrm{crit}}=\frac{\mu}{\kappa}. 

 Here, μ \mu is the expected fraction of units lost and κ \kappa is the number of units added per existing unit following a successful attempt. Since p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) increases with population size, there can be a population N crit N_{\mathrm{crit}} at which this critical threshold is first met. 

 For p crit < 1 p_{\mathrm{crit}}<1 , solving p ⁡ ( N crit ) = p crit p(N_{\mathrm{crit}})=p_{\mathrm{crit}} gives

 Populations below N crit N_{\mathrm{crit}} decline, whereas populations larger than N crit N_{\mathrm{crit}} grow. When p ref < p crit p_{\mathrm{ref}}<p_{\mathrm{crit}} , N crit > N ref N_{\mathrm{crit}}>N_{\mathrm{ref}} : this means that for the reference case population declines, but a sufficiently large population grows. This population threshold does not arise for independent attempts, because their success probability does not change with population size. If p ref ≥ p crit p_{\mathrm{ref}}\geq p_{\mathrm{crit}} , the reference population is already experiencing positive expected growth. 

 Assumption: κ > μ \kappa>\mu . 

 Solving for ( 18 ) requires that p crit < 1 p_{\mathrm{crit}}<1 , and thus that κ > μ \kappa>\mu . If κ ≤ μ \kappa\leq\mu , losses match or overwhelm growth opportunities such that even if attempts are always successful, population growth cannot occur. 

 Once the population exceeds N crit N_{\mathrm{crit}} , this model predicts continued growth. As N N increases, p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) in ( 16 ) approaches one, and the expected growth per unit from ( 4 ) approaches ( κ − μ ) / τ > 0 (\kappa-\mu)/\tau>0 . This steady growth holds for as long as resource availability does not become a constraint, as described in the model assumptions in Section  2.2.2 . 

 3.2 Population Pacing and Ecological Red Teaming for Ecological Safety 

 Implications for AI safety testing 

 Suppose that a laboratory evaluates an AI model for populations up to N ref N_{\mathrm{ref}} and finds that the population is not capable of tasks like weight exfiltration or self-replication [ 14 , 45 ] – i.e., there would be negative expected growth at the largest tested population. Our model argues that this testing is not sufficient, as a larger initial population of that same model could be capable of growth. Figure  7 illustrates this case. 

 Recent studies have started to explore red teaming for emergent threats unique to multi-agent systems [ 11 ] . For example, He et al. [46] performed red-teaming attacks that intervene on communications between agents, a study by Microsoft Research [ 47 ] examined emergent risks unique to a network of over a hundred agents, and a recent study by the Anthropic Frontier Red Team [ 48 ] examined failure modes unique to multi-agent systems, such as failures of cooperation, collusion, and sabotage. 

 In contrast with these existing works on red-teaming for multi-agent systems, our theoretical framework implies an urgent need for red teaming for emergent ecological threats as well. The above red-teaming works study emergent threats in multi-agent systems with a fixed population. We propose ecological red teaming : studying the potential for AI population changes, especially the threat of population takeoff. Here, the crucial quantity to characterize is the population scaling law ( p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) in our model): how the success rate of hacking or self-exfiltration scales as a function of population size. Equally important is characterizing the impact of environmental factors, such as how many additional units the acquired computational resources can support ( κ \kappa ) and the rate at which agents deployed without authorization are terminated or shut down ( μ \mu ). Our paper provides a minimal theoretical framework for predicting the critical population size sufficient for takeoff. 

 We also propose population pacing : population size should be gradually increased while carefully characterizing how capability scales. Increasing the single-agent capability or the collaborative gain decreases the population threshold for takeoff (see Figure 7 (b–c)). As a result, for every new generation of models, frontier labs should re-estimate the critical population size and pace deployment population sizes to remain below it. This process can identify population sizes at which further testing is needed.

 4 Discussion 

 In this work, we introduce an ecological theory of AI-agent populations that predicts an emergent population threshold for takeoff.
The fundamental intuition that our theory provides is that when AI agents can collaborate to enhance their collective capability, there is a qualitative shift in safety concerns.
In our model, without collaboration and under fixed environmental conditions, takeoff requires an individual agent’s capability to cross a threshold.
With collaboration, takeoff can also happen when the total number of agents crosses a population threshold.
This makes evaluation with a small population insufficient to ensure ecological safety at larger scales. 

 Our model predicts the existence of a takeoff threshold, but does not establish whether current agent populations are already near one. Determining whether such a threshold exists in current systems, and where it would be, will require empirical population-scaling data. OpenAI has deployed approximately 10,000 agents simultaneously to pursue the Navier–Stokes problem [ 3 ] , whereas its published multi-agent data on cybersecurity-related tasks extends only to 16 agents (see Appendix  C , Figure  11 for a consistency check with our theory). Although these cases involve different tasks, this is a significant gap between the scale of actual deployments and the scale of empirically tested populations. We therefore propose population pacing and ecological red teaming as a second lever alongside the capability pacing proposed by Amodei [10] . Although evaluating large populations on hard tasks is expensive, frontier labs should conduct such evaluations in controlled environments before deploying populations at previously untested scales.
Labs should carefully characterize population scaling laws, estimate the critical population size for takeoff, and make results available for oversight. 

 Much of the theory of modern deep learning has focused on explaining phenomena first observed in experiments, including pre-training scaling laws, in-context learning, and the sudden emergence of capabilities. In this work, we take an opposite approach: proposing a theoretical prediction for population takeoff before such a takeoff has been observed. We developed this prediction based on the combination of three elements that have each been recently observed: AI agents’ high cyber capability, increases in collective capability with population size, and collective misalignment. The resulting model is not an exact prediction for empirical behavior, but rather a demonstration that following a few reasonable assumptions, upon reaching a critical mass of units in an AI population, unchecked growth can occur. As the development cycles of AI agents accelerate, we hope this work contributes an example of how theoretical frameworks can proactively predict misalignment risks and how to mitigate them.

 For finite d ref d_{\rm ref} and N > N ref N>N_{\rm ref} , the bracket in ( 25 ) is smaller than N / N ref N/N_{\rm ref} , but is a finite correction. 

 Correction to the critical population size 

 Working in the regime where p ref < p crit < 1 p_{\rm ref}<p_{\rm crit}<1 , we set p ⁡ ( N crit ) = p crit p(N_{\rm crit})=p_{\rm crit} in ( 25 ) and solve for N crit N_{\rm crit} . This yields 

 N crit = N ref ​ ( 1 + [ 1 − p ref 1 − p crit ] 1 / β − 1 1 − B − d ref ) . \boxed{N_{\rm crit}=N_{\rm ref}\left(1+\frac{\left[\frac{1-p_{\rm ref}}{1-p_{\rm crit}}\right]^{1/\beta}-1}{1-B^{-d_{\rm ref}}}\right).} 

 (27) 

 Again, we confirm that when B − d ref B^{-d_{\rm ref}} is negligible, ( 27 ) reduces to the main text expression ( 18 ). For a general finite d ref d_{\rm ref} , the denominator 1 − B − d ref < 1 1-B^{-d_{\rm ref}}<1 , so threshold following from the exact expression is above the threshold in the main text. This is a finite correction that vanishes exponentially as the reference depth increases. 

 Appendix B Other forms for collaboration C ⁡ ( N ) C(N) 

 In the main text, we assume that the effective computational work is proportional to population size, C ⁡ ( N ) ∝ N C(N)\propto N . However, some empirical evidence shows that coordination overhead or the structure of certain tasks may limit or negate the benefits of multi-agent collaboration [ 49 ] . Motivated by this, here we examine how alternative forms for C ⁡ ( N ) C(N) affect the model behavior, keeping the population dynamics equation ( 4 ) and relation between work done and success probability p ⁡ ( C ) p(C) in ( 13 ) unchanged. For a general C ⁡ ( N ) C(N) , equation ( 13 ) becomes 

 p ⁡ ( N ) = 1 − ( 1 − p ref ) ​ [ C ⁡ ( N ) C ⁡ ( N ref ) ] − β , β = ρ ln ⁡ B . p(N)=1-(1-p_{\mathrm{ref}})\left[\frac{C(N)}{C(N_{\mathrm{ref}})}\right]^{-\beta},\qquad\beta=\frac{\rho}{\ln B}. 

 (28) 

 In each case below, the phase boundary is once again found by solving p ⁡ ( N ) = p crit = μ κ p(N)=p_{\mathrm{crit}}=\frac{\mu}{\kappa} . 

 B.1 Sublinear or superlinear effective work 

 Suppose C ⁡ ( N ) ∝ N α C(N)\propto N^{\alpha} with α > 0 \alpha>0 , so that 

 C ⁡ ( N ) C ⁡ ( N ref ) = ( N N ref ) α , α > 0 . \frac{C(N)}{C(N_{\mathrm{ref}})}=\left(\frac{N}{N_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^{\alpha},\qquad\alpha>0. 

 (29) 

 This encompasses two regimes: 

 • 

 Sublinear effective work: 0 < α < 1 0<\alpha<1 . This means that effective work increases with population size, but at a slower rate than the population growth itself. This occurs when units are duplicating search efforts, there is overhead from coordination, or tasks are not fully parallelizable. 

 • 

 Superlinear effective work: α > 1 \alpha>1 . This means that effective work increases faster than the population grows. This could occur in scenarios where there are network benefits that amplify the contributions of additional units, such as the “pooling discoveries” example outlined in Figure 5 . 

 Substituting ( 29 ) into ( 28 ) gives 

 p ⁡ ( N ) = 1 − ( 1 − p ref ) ​ ( N N ref ) − α ​ β . p(N)=1-(1-p_{\mathrm{ref}})\left(\frac{N}{N_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^{-\alpha\beta}. 

 (30) 

 This yields a critical population threshold of 

 N crit = N ref ​ ( 1 − p ref 1 − p crit ) 1 / ( α ​ β ) . N_{\mathrm{crit}}=N_{\mathrm{ref}}\left(\frac{1-p_{\mathrm{ref}}}{1-p_{\mathrm{crit}}}\right)^{1/(\alpha\beta)}. 

 (31) 

 Comparing with the linear case in ( 18 ), the exponent of N N in p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) changes from β \beta to α ​ β \alpha\beta . Empirically, this exponent can be fitted directly from the p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) curve: in the equations above, − α ​ β -\alpha\beta is the slope of log ⁡ [ 1 − p ⁡ ( N ) ] \log[1-p(N)] versus log ⁡ N \log N . Thus, as long as C ⁡ ( N ) C(N) is a power law, any sublinear or superlinear scaling simply changes the fitted value of the collaboration gain, β \beta ; the value of α \alpha does not need to be separately estimated in order to calculate N crit N_{\mathrm{crit}} . 

 Qualitatively, the shape of the phase diagram does not change: sublinear or superlinear power-law scaling of effective work only rescales the collaboration gain from β \beta to α ​ β \alpha\beta , shifting the location of the N crit N_{\mathrm{crit}} boundary between population growth and decline.  B.2 Eventual plateau from communication or evaluator bottleneck 

 Another potential model for C ⁡ ( N ) C(N) is the case of diminishing returns upon adding additional units. This could be the case if there is a bottleneck in the system, such as a shared communication channel, orchestrator, or evaluator with some fixed throughput.
A simple model for C ⁡ ( N ) C(N) wherein effective work approaches a plateau as additional units are added is: 

 C ( N ) = C max ( 1 − e − N / N 0 ) , C(N)=C_{\max}\left(1-e^{-N/N_{0}}\right), 

 (32) 

 where C max C_{\max} is the maximum effective work and N 0 N_{0} is the population scale at which the work begins to level off. Relative to a reference population, we have 

 C ⁡ ( N ) C ⁡ ( N ref ) = 1 − e − N / N 0 1 − e − N ref / N 0 . \frac{C(N)}{C(N_{\mathrm{ref}})}=\frac{1-e^{-N/N_{0}}}{1-e^{-N_{\mathrm{ref}}/N_{0}}}. 

 (33) 

 Substituting this into ( 28 ) yields 

 p ⁡ ( N ) = 1 − ( 1 − p ref ) ​ [ 1 − e − N / N 0 1 − e − N ref / N 0 ] − β . p(N)=1-(1-p_{\mathrm{ref}})\left[\frac{1-e^{-N/N_{0}}}{1-e^{-N_{\mathrm{ref}}/N_{0}}}\right]^{-\beta}. 

 (34) 

 Solving p ⁡ ( N crit ) = p crit p(N_{\mathrm{crit}})=p_{\mathrm{crit}} then gives 

 N crit = − N 0 ln [ 1 − ( 1 − e − N ref / N 0 ) ( 1 − p ref 1 − p crit ) 1 / β ] . N_{\mathrm{crit}}=-N_{0}\ln\!\left[1-\left(1-e^{-N_{\mathrm{ref}}/N_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{1-p_{\mathrm{ref}}}{1-p_{\mathrm{crit}}}\right)^{1/\beta}\right]. 

 (35) 

 Since the effective work eventually saturates, p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) also approaches a maximum. A finite N crit N_{\mathrm{crit}} exists only if this limiting success probability exceeds p crit p_{\mathrm{crit}} , which requires 2 2 
 2 

 This condition is equivalent to requiring that the quantity inside the logarithm in ( 35 ) is positive. 

 β > β min ≡ ln ⁡ [ ( 1 − p ref ) / ( 1 − p crit ) ] ln [ 1 / ( 1 − e − N ref / N 0 ) ] . \beta>\beta_{\min}\equiv\frac{\ln[(1-p_{\mathrm{ref}})/(1-p_{\mathrm{crit}})]}{\ln\!\left[1/(1-e^{-N_{\mathrm{ref}}/N_{0}})\right]}. 

 (36) 

 Here β min \beta_{\min} is the collaboration gain at which the limiting success probability equals p crit p_{\mathrm{crit}} . 

 For β > β min \beta>\beta_{\min} , we have the same qualitative population threshold as in the main model: populations below a finite N crit N_{\mathrm{crit}} decline and populations above it grow. As β \beta approaches β min \beta_{\min} from above, the plateau in p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) approaches p crit p_{\mathrm{crit}} , and N crit N_{\mathrm{crit}} tends to infinity. Near β min \beta_{\min} and at large N N , p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) is near constant, so there is little effect to adding more units. In this respect, the phase diagram resembles the individual-attempt case in Figure  2 : there is no population-dependent threshold.

 B.3 Congestion at large populations 

 The final option we consider is the case where C ⁡ ( N ) C(N) increases for small populations but decreases for large populations. This could occur if the coordination costs eventually outweigh the work contributed by additional units. We note that this regime requires that large population congestion is unavoidable . If excess units can instead be idled or divided into independent groups, effective work would plateau rather than decrease at large N N , and the bottleneck model from the previous section would be more appropriate. 

 A simple model with a single maximum is 

 C ( N ) = a N e − N / N 0 , C(N)=aNe^{-N/N_{0}}, 

 (37) 

 where a > 0 a>0 fixes the scale and N 0 N_{0} is the population at which effective work is at a maximum. Comparing to a reference population, we have 

 C ⁡ ( N ) C ⁡ ( N ref ) = N N ref e − ( N − N ref ) / N 0 . \frac{C(N)}{C(N_{\mathrm{ref}})}=\frac{N}{N_{\mathrm{ref}}}e^{-(N-N_{\mathrm{ref}})/N_{0}}. 

 (38) 

 Then, the success probability ( 28 ) becomes 

 p ( N ) = 1 − ( 1 − p ref ) [ N N ref e − ( N − N ref ) / N 0 ] − β . p(N)=1-(1-p_{\mathrm{ref}})\left[\frac{N}{N_{\mathrm{ref}}}e^{-(N-N_{\mathrm{ref}})/N_{0}}\right]^{-\beta}. 

 (39) 

 Assume N ref < N 0 N_{\mathrm{ref}}<N_{0} , so that p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) initially increases, reaches its maximum at N = N 0 N=N_{0} , and then decreases. Define β peak \beta_{\mathrm{peak}} as the collaboration gain for which p ⁡ ( N 0 ) = p crit p(N_{0})=p_{\mathrm{crit}} . Population growth requires 

 β > β peak , β peak ≡ ln ⁡ [ ( 1 − p ref ) / ( 1 − p crit ) ] ln ⁡ ( N 0 / N ref ) − ( N 0 − N ref ) / N 0 . \beta>\beta_{\mathrm{peak}}\,,\quad\beta_{\mathrm{peak}}\equiv\frac{\ln[(1-p_{\mathrm{ref}})/(1-p_{\mathrm{crit}})]}{\ln(N_{0}/N_{\mathrm{ref}})-(N_{0}-N_{\mathrm{ref}})/N_{0}}. 

 (40) 

 If β ≤ β peak \beta\leq\beta_{\mathrm{peak}} , then p ⁡ ( N ) ≤ p crit p(N)\leq p_{\mathrm{crit}} for all N N , so population growth cannot occur. Conversely, if β > β peak \beta>\beta_{\mathrm{peak}} , p ⁡ ( N ) p(N) crosses p crit p_{\mathrm{crit}} twice. As a result, populations between the two crossing points grow, while smaller and larger populations decline. For the particular choice of C ⁡ ( N ) C(N) in Equation ( 37 ), these two thresholds have the closed form 

 N crit ( k ) = − N 0 W k [ − N ref N 0 e − N ref / N 0 ( 1 − p ref 1 − p crit ) 1 / β ] , k ∈ { 0 , − 1 } , N_{\mathrm{crit}}^{(k)}=-N_{0}W_{k}\!\left[-\frac{N_{\mathrm{ref}}}{N_{0}}e^{-N_{\mathrm{ref}}/N_{0}}\left(\frac{1-p_{\mathrm{ref}}}{1-p_{\mathrm{crit}}}\right)^{1/\beta}\right],\qquad k\in\{0,-1\}, 

 (41) 

 where W k W_{k} denotes branch k k of the Lambert W W function, defined by W k ​ ( z ) ​ e W k ​ ( z ) = z W_{k}(z)e^{W_{k}(z)}=z . These two solutions give a lower and an upper threshold for N crit N_{\mathrm{crit}} , shown in Figure 10 . We note that the dependence on the Lambert W W function arises from the particular chosen form of C ⁡ ( N ) C(N) , but the qualitative possibility of lower and upper thresholds depends only on C ⁡ ( N ) C(N) having a maximum.
