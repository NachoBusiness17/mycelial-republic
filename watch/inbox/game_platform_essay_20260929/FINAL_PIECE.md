# A distant cluster is one thing

An estate of cheap local AIs is building a World of Warcraft. Not a metaphor for one, and not a mockup: a 2021 C++ server core, GPL-2, 235 megabytes of other people's twenty years of engineering, is being compiled on the estate's own machines, its databases already loaded with 29,919 creatures and 9,086 quests. On the day this is written the auth server stands, a 1.19 megabyte binary named realmd, while the world server waits behind twelve landed fixes and an itemized ledger of the fixes still owed. The people doing the porting are janitor-grade models and one funded judgment lane, and their work is not trusted. It is receipted.

There is a reply on an old internet thread about the Barnes-Hut algorithm that this estate treats as doctrine: the brute-force answer is every particle pulling on every other particle, quadrillions of sums, each one correct, none of them the answer; the tree is the recognition that a distant cluster is one thing. The estate applies that to WoW. You cannot reproduce twenty years of Blizzard. You can treat the whole thing as one center of mass, approximate its loop long before you can copy its content, and read its laws: the estate already holds the dispatch table of a WoW server as plain data, 1,312 packet verbs with a hash pinned to the source, testable without compiling anything. The founder said he wanted the bones, the way you might rebuild Photoshop from scratch without the nonsense. The clone is not the dream. It is the forcing project: an MMO makes every backend service exist under load, so you build the hardest server there is and let it interrogate your stack.

The second thing being built is the actual product, and it is stranger: a backend that proves play happened. Fixed timestep, seeded randomness, byte-identical replay, a hash for every run. Flip one input and the system names the exact frame where the two histories diverge. The test claim reads 202 out of 202, and the estate is careful even about that: green, but not re-executed on the day, manual runs, no continuous integration yet. This machinery was not built for games. It was built because an ensemble of cheap models needs to be auditable, and now it turns outward: if your game runs on this backend, your players' play is verifiable after the fact, and agreements between players can be checked rather than hoped for. Nobody else's creator platform verifies play. That is the sentence the estate allows itself, and it immediately files the caveat next to it: zero external developers have touched it. The first one, a friend named Adam, is the fourth rung on a six-rung ladder, and his invariant is written down in advance: the backend must work without the estate's help. Whatever Adam trips over is the roadmap.

Why verification, of all products? Because the graveyards did the market research. The games-with-a-purpose lineage, von Ahn's territory, produced one immortal: reCAPTCHA, a hundred million puzzles a day digitizing books, because its work hid inside friction people already had. Its siblings, the ones you had to visit, all died the same deaths: novelty decay, collusion equilibria, content exhaustion, and the day pay arrived and play became a job. The creator platforms ran the same experiment at bigger scale. Roblox pays out at an effective 25 to 30 percent and has cashed $923 million; Epic's pool is 40 percent; Steam tried 25 percent for mods and lasted days; and Rec Room, 150 million users, creators earning a million a quarter, is shutting down in June, taking its creators' worlds with it. Axie priced the closed-loop token economy and closed it, 99 percent down, a $625 million hack along the way. The estate's answer is written as defaults on a decision table rather than as manifesto: payouts ride an append-only receipt ledger at a published rate, meters weight rare, byte-backed artifacts instead of early volume, worlds export in open formats, and the platform that cannot trap its developers is the founding constraint, because the estate's operator was the one trapped once.

The safety posture is architecture first and claims later, which is the only order the record supports. Small federated servers, guardian-owned accounts, no attention-mining loops aimed at children. The estate's own research names the trap in the field, companies marketing safety they never architected, and declines to set it. State the verdict plainly: the design decisions exist on paper, the platform has no users yet, so the claim is unproven by choice.

And the builder of all this is the cheap fleet itself. Local models do the reading, a hundred million tokens an hour measured at full width, fifty cents a day of hard-capped spend remembered from August. Nothing the cheap lanes produce is trusted as done work; it is banked as findings, and verification climbs to the funded lane. That discipline is the fourth invention here, the one nobody planned. When your workers are models you cannot trust, you build receipts instead of trust, and receipts, unlike trust, are a product. The fleet's constraint became the platform's catalog.

What does this say about where AI-assisted building is going? Three things, each with a receipt.

First, use is the only honest requirements document. Nobody could have written the estate's error ledger from reading about the old server. It took a day of compiling it: a timestamp buffer too small by 44 bytes, caught by a modern fortify check; an entire asynchronous IO library renamed underneath the code; a directory spelled Vmap while every include says vmap, invisible on Windows, fatal on Linux; template lookups Microsoft's compiler forgives and the free one does not. Twelve fixes landed, a known family remains, and the list itself is filed as the deliverable, because the list is what informs the choice.

Second, proof is getting cheaper than trust. The interesting economic question is shifting from which model can I trust to what can I prove about the work regardless of the worker. The estate runs on the second question and is betting a platform on it.

Third, the informed choice comes after use, never before. Godot is a training engine, named as such: early days, build, own, imperfect. The real engine decision, the netcode decision, the database decision are all deferred by design to the top of the ladder, and every open question carries a default so that silence cannot become drift.

The oldest lesson predates all of it. The text-game people knew that graphics were the last, most disposable layer, and the world underneath, the heartbeat, the persistence, the other people, was the first. The estate's own map of itself reads the same way: a talent tree of 109 invariants reinforced 184,455 times, a debuff log of 7,498 warnings, an XP log of 414,459 lines whose points were never spent. A server with excellent telemetry and no endgame, the map says. The endgame, this time, is a second lantern: one more player in the dark, casting light into the same world. Everything on the ladder points at that door.

A tool you have never run is a picture of a tool. The estate spent a long time collecting pictures: engines unbuilt, clients unplayed, designs complete on paper and dormant in code. What changed this week is not ambition. It is that things are being turned on, one rung at a time, by cheap tireless labor under a receipt for everything, with the choices waiting at the top for someone who has actually touched the machine.

That is the whole bet. Build it badly, own it completely, write down everything missing, and let use, not opinion, pick the next rung. The tree does not compute every particle. It computes what the question actually needs, and it gets the answer.
