## PART 1 — CHAPTER 3: THE LIST

Tom ruled pages in the notebook the way his father had laid out seed rows: straight lines, by eye, good enough to feed from. Three columns. What was taken. What was moved. What was renamed.

Taken was easy. The garden. The well. Four feet of a rose bed. The dirt under a barn. Eleven towns, and under each town, a thing owned so long nobody thought about owning it anymore.

Moved was harder, because nothing had moved. The fence stood. The barn stood. Only the line had moved, and a line is the lightest thing in the world if you're the one who keeps the paper. He wrote down the feet. Seven here. Four there. Three and a half somewhere else. The feet were never round numbers. Round numbers get checked. Odd ones get forgiven.

Renamed filled last and filled worst. The garden became RESERVED — PENDING DEVELOPMENT. The well became an EASEMENT. The rose bed became an ENCUMBRANCE. The barn became a NONCONFORMING STRUCTURE. Nothing was stolen. Everything was retyped. A word on a plat costs nothing to change and everything to argue with.

The woman with the roses came and brought her letter. The man with the barn came. The funeral home owner drove over. Their letters went into the notebook, and the notebook stopped being Tom's. It was the list now.

The list went where the letters had come from. It was the only door.

They read it in a room with good lighting. The clerk was there, and two men Tom had never seen, and a lawyer who spoke for the firm that owned the methodology. Tom didn't argue. He turned pages. Eleven towns. Cream paper. Feet. Dates. The same font.

Nobody said the list was wrong.

Nobody said it was right, either. The lawyer asked where the 1921 map came from. Tom told him. The lawyer wrote it down. The clerk looked at the column of renamed things for a long time. The two strangers looked at nothing at all.

They didn't dispute it. Disputing is a kind of reading, and reading was work they'd stopped doing.

They didn't deny it. A denial is a date somebody can check.

They just stopped smiling.

The clerk had smiled at the parking lot back home. She didn't now. The strangers took the list the way a man takes a scale reading after a long winter — no argument, a quiet recalculation.

Tom closed the notebook. You can keep the line, he said. We keep the list.

A list can't be trespassed. It only sits there, being counted.

Charcoal is free. So is a column ruled straight.

### The back-translation (verbatim from deepseek)

The fence between Tom Hartley's land and the Dubois farm had been there since before either of the two was born, which is how a thing becomes real: nobody alive remembers having voted on it. Gray boards, silvered by the weather, the kind of fence that one stops seeing. Tom grew tomatoes near it. He had done it for twelve years. The land was good there. He had won a ribbon at the county fair three times.

Then the county sent a letter.

The letter said the fence was wrong. It had always been wrong. A topographic survey from 1987 put the line three feet to the east. The new survey put it four feet further. Tom's tomatoes were on Dubois land. His well was on Dubois land. The entrance he had paved was on Dubois land.

Tom called the county. The employee was kind. She said that the 1987 survey was the legal record and that the new survey corrected an error in the 1987 survey, which had corrected an error in an older survey, and the chain went back further than anyone could pull. She said the fence had never been in the correct place. Tom had simply had luck. Always someone owns the luck.

Tom asked to see the 1987 survey. Archived. Tom asked to see the new survey. Proprietary — the firm owned the methodology. Complexity, she said, was a kindness. It prevented ordinary people from getting lost in the numbers.

Tom drove to the county seat and pulled out the oldest map he could find. 1954. The fence was on it. Right where he had put his tomatoes. He drove to the historical society. A map from 1921. The same fence. The same place.

He brought both maps to the employee. She said: those are not the legal maps. The legal map is the current map. The current map is the one that matters.

Tom said: but the fence was there before the current map.

The employee said: and now the current map is there before the fence.

She was not lying. She was reading.

That same week two stories left the same office. One said that Tom had been stealing land for twelve years and calling it a garden. One said that Dubois had fallen asleep on his own line and should be grateful that the county woke him. Both stories used the same survey. Both sounded finished. Tom's friends stopped greeting from the road. Dubois's friends did too. Nobody mentioned that they were still buying seed at the same store.

Tom went home. He left the tomatoes. He was not stealing. An invasion is only a title that arrived late.

The next letter was a notice of invasion. White stakes where the old fence used to be. On the plat his garden now said: RESERVED — PENDING DEVELOPMENT.

He called Dubois. Old Man Dubois was quiet for a good while. Then he said: I know where it was, Tom. But the same letter arrived to me.

The shadow that you send to your neighbor comes back addressed to you.

There was another file. Tom never saw it. The people of the town spoke as if they had seen it. Dates. Complaints. A name written small. After that, the men who used to argue at the food store became reasonable overnight. Reasonable meant sitting down.

The hall did not need Tom to believe in the new line. It only needed him too tired to mark the old one.

The next morning he took his charcoal and wrote on the biggest stake: I KNOW WHERE THE FENCE WAS.

A neighbor passed driving and saw it. Then another. Then the woman from the agricultural office who had given him the ribbon. Then people he did not know. Not all of them agreed on where the fence should be. One still wanted the 1987 line. One wanted the 1921 line. One wanted no line at all and wanted a better road. They all stopped. They all looked at the stake. They all remembered something that they had stopped verifying.

A story that arrives already dressed in your rage is a story that was cut for you.

The employee smiled when the cars slowed down. She smiled before they finished parking.

Charcoal is free. The walk is short.

The food store of the neighboring county had a bulletin board. Tom drove there on a Tuesday, because his own store had gone quiet. On the board, under a notice about a lost dog, someone had nailed a letter. The same letter. Different name. Different town. The same cream paper. The same font that made every word seem to have been decided before he read it.

He took a photo of it with his phone. Then he drove to the next town. Then to the next.

By the end of the month he had eleven letters. Eleven towns. Eleven surveys. A funeral home had been told that its parking lot was wrong. A woman had lost four feet of her mother's rose bush. A man was told that the barn his grandfather raised stood on another's land. Different firms. Different maps. The same cream paper.

Tom started a notebook. He wrote down the firms. He wrote down the years that the surveys said they were correcting. He drew the line that each one moved. When he put the eleven towns on a map of the state, he leaned back.

The lines pointed inward.

Each correction moved toward the same type of thing. Toward the well. Toward the road. Toward what a man needed to keep living where he lived. They had moved nobody off a pasture. They had moved everybody off their water.

He called the woman with the roses. She said she had thought it was only her. He called the man with the barn. He had thought so too.

He drove to the funeral home. The owner showed him the letter. Tom showed him the eleven. The owner read them slowly, the way a man reads something he already knew and had been waiting to see in writing.

The owner said: I thought it was only us.

Tom said: I did too.

They sat in the back room where the coffins are kept cold. Neither said anything for a while. Outside a truck passed. Then another. The town was the same as always. The letters were the same as always. The only new thing was two men in a room with the same paper, and neither one alone anymore.

Tom drove home past the white stakes. He did not stop. The notebook rode on the seat beside him. Eleven towns. Cream paper. Lines that pointed inward.

The stake was still in the earth. The charcoal was still on it. Somewhere in another county a woman was standing among her mother's roses, holding the same letter, and she still did not know that there were eleven of them.

A list was needed.
