THE FLAW: Shared grounding only ships a human-authored prior; any fact an agent learns after the freeze cannot become common knowledge without either rewriting the cached prefix or opening a message channel you claim not to need, so the fleet coordinates on doctrine and silently diverges on reality.

### 1. Where shared-grounding-only coordination theoretically breaks

Shared grounding is a common *prior*. Message passing is how agents manufacture a common *posterior*. Those are not interchangeable.

A frozen prefix can encode laws, roles, task frame, and a snapshot of the world at freeze time. It cannot encode events that happen *after* freeze, because those events are generated in private tails. The architecture’s own constraint — human-only governed writes to the prefix — makes this a theorem, not a bug: if agent A discovers \(P\) in its tail, agents B–E do not know \(P\), do not know that A knows \(P\), and cannot condition on either fact unless \(P\) is promoted. Promotion has only two doors:

- rewrite the shared prefix (cache-kill for the whole tranche), or
- send an addressed delta that other agents must *read as input* (a message, whatever you name it).

That is the break. The inexpressible class is anything that requires **mutual knowledge of post-prefix state**. Concretely:

**Private observation and hidden-state games.** One agent sees a tool result, a file, a human aside, a failed hypothesis. The others still condition on the old world. You can paper this over at \(n=5\) because the operator is in the loop and re-freezes. That is not coordination. That is a human multiplexer.

**Negotiation and preference aggregation.** “I will take the risky path if you take the safe path” is not a property of a shared constitution. It is a contract over *current* intentions. Typed tail deltas addressed to one agent do not create the contract for the others. Without a public move, you get five locally coherent plans that are jointly inconsistent.

**Disagreement that must persist.** Shared grounding pressures consensus because the only legal shared object is the doctrine. Real multi-agent work needs *registered dissent*: A believes X, B believes ¬X, both are allowed to proceed under that split, and later work is tagged with which assumption it used. A single versioned prefix cannot hold a fork of the world without becoming a branching store. The moment you add branches, you have messages (or a blackboard). Blackboards exist because this problem is old.

**Common knowledge, not just shared text.** Coordination games, handoff protocols, “stop if anyone has already claimed the lock,” and “do not duplicate the expensive tool call” require that others know that you know. Text sitting in a prefix that nobody has *seen you accept* is not common knowledge. Cache-hit rate measures byte identity, not uptake. An agent can attend the constitution and still privately rewrite it; the other agents cannot detect that except by reading its tail — again a message.

**Adversarial or merely sloppy agents.** If one agent poisons its own tail with a confident false lemma, shared grounding has no gossip channel for the others to quarantine it. The human write path is the only immune system. That is fine as a safety story and fatal as a coordination story.

**What *is* expressible.** Static role split, style constraints, hard laws, a shared glossary, a frozen task frame, and “all of you work from this brief.” That is a briefed platoon, not a staff. DeLM-style systems that look similar *do* let agents write compact verified updates into the shared context; they reintroduced the missing channel and then had to govern it. Your design deletes that channel on purpose. The deletion is the architecture. It is also the failure mode.

Steering-by-typed-delta does not close the gap. A delta on *one* frozen tail is a whisper. A delta that must be visible to many is either a prefix edit or a broadcast. There is no third object in the design.

### 3. What happens to a versioned constitution under model churn

Every model change is a new country *semantically*, even when it is not economically. Treat those as separate layers.

**Token identity ≠ KV identity ≠ behavioral identity.** The constitution is a byte string. Provider prefix cache keys on exact prefix bytes for one model. Cross-model KV transfer (the paper you cited) shows that within a family, matched-head caches have real linear structure and a ridge mapper can skip re-prefill, retaining 73–98% of standalone-prefill accuracy on four of six pairs and collapsing on two. A nonlinear patch recovers some of the failures. The mapper is calibrated on generic FineWeb sequences, not on your doctrine.

So after a swap you can sometimes keep the *economic* prefix (cheap prefill) and still lose the *legal* prefix (the model no longer implements the same constraints given the same tokens). Retention of HellaSwag is not retention of “do not invent citations” or “never promote an assumption to fact.” Those are tail behaviors sitting on a long, sharp, out-of-distribution prefix. A 2–27 point leak on a benchmark is a constitutional crisis if the leaked dimension is obedience.

Follow-on work already treats full-head mapping as brittle across architectural differences and tightens the mapper (CacheBridge, Mixture-of-Translators, universal reuse layers). That literature is a warning: transfer is a research object, not a substrate you can bet a legal system on.

**Tokenizer and chat-template churn is a silent constitutional amendment.** New special tokens, different BOS/role markup, system-prompt wrapping, or a “think” channel that swallows the prefix will either (a) break the exact-match cache key or (b) keep the bytes and change what the model *does* with them. You will ship the same Markdown and get a different agent. Version the constitution by content hash *and* by `(model_id, tokenizer_id, template_id, mapper_id)`. If any of those four moves, you are in a new country until proven otherwise.

**There is a principled migration schedule. It is not “map the KV and go.”**

1. Freeze constitution `C_v` as text plus a behavioral spec: a suite of *must / must-not / should* transcripts, including the trap cases from question 2.
2. For candidate model \(M'\) (or mapper \(T_{M \to M'}\)), run the suite twice: native prefill of \(C_v\) on \(M'\), and transferred-KV prefill. Score agreement with \(M\) *and* with the spec, separately. Transfer success without spec success is a cheap, unlawful agent.
3. Promote only on a two-gate rule: cache economics within budget, *and* no spec regression on the safety/doctrine slice. Allow capability scores to move; do not allow duty scores to move without a MAJOR version bump and a human rewrite.
4. Keep `C_v` immutable for the old model. Do not “improve the wording so the new model understands it.” That is how you launder a model change into a quiet law change. If \(M'\) needs different words, that is `C_{v+1}`, with a diff, a reason, and a re-calibration of every mapper you care about.
5. Recalibrate mappers on *your* prefix, not FineWeb. Doctrine is a weird dialect. The paper’s 500-sequence, 1k-token fit will understate error on a long legalistic prefix.

If you skip the spec gate, model churn becomes constitutional drift with a green cache-hit dashboard. That is the worst failure this design can have, because your operational metric will report health while the epistemic object dies.

### 6. Closest prior art

The assembly is not new in pieces. It is new in the refusal to let agents write back.

Closest stack, in descending order of resemblance:

- **agentcache** — shared session prefix, specialist forks that preserve the parent byte-for-byte so provider prefix cache hits. This *is* your economic thesis, implemented as orchestration rather than doctrine.
- **Blackboard systems / MetaGPT message pool / DeLM** — agents coordinate through a shared artifact instead of pairwise chat. DeLM is the honest version of your design: shared verified context, task queue, compact write-backs. They kept the write path and then had to verify writes. You deleted writes and called the deletion “grounding.”
- **Constitutional AI + versioned-constitution-governance** — natural-language law as the control surface, Git-signed, human-gated. Your governed write path is this pattern, pointed at a cache key.
- **DroidSpeak / KVCOMM / TokenDance / EACC** — the serving-layer attempt to make shared context cheap when prefixes *almost* match or models differ. They exist because “just share the prefix” dies the moment tails or models diverge.
- **“Beyond Text-Passing: Shared Cognitive Substrates”** — same polemic as yours (language is a bad coordination bus; put state somewhere typed). They put the missing objects in the open: world model, causal graph, energy pool. You put them in a human’s editor.

If you want one name: **a human-gated blackboard whose only legal post is the constitution, with prefix-cache used as both cost function and coherence meter.** The prior art that already exists is the blackboard plus prompt-cache forks. The part that does not exist — and should not, until you solve promotion of posteriors — is the claim that you can delete inter-agent state updates and still have a fleet.

---

**Strongest component:** the human-only governed write to a versioned prefix. That is a real control surface. Most multi-agent piles let any agent mutate the shared story and then act surprised when the story rots.

**Weakest component:** shared-grounding-only coordination, with cache-hit rate asked to stand in for common knowledge. Cache-hit is a systems metric. It cannot see incompatible private commitments, and it will look better right as the fleet starts agreeing on a stale world.

**What I would build next:** a *posterior log* with the same dual compiler as the steering board — typed, addressed, append-only records that compile into (a) a human brief and (b) a cache-stable *suffix block* that every agent can opt into without touching constitution bytes. Promotion into that log stays human-gated or spec-gated. That admits the channel question 1 says you need, without turning the constitution into a chatroom or blowing the prefix cache. Second, a constitution-migration harness: same `C_v`, old model vs new model vs mapped-KV, scored on duties not benchmarks. Without those two objects, n=5 will keep working and n=50 will be five briefing groups that think they are one institution.

**The flaw most likely to kill this architecture is that shared grounding can coordinate what agents can see, but it cannot reliably coordinate what agents privately intend, infer, withhold, or commit to before that state becomes observable.**

I would attack **1, 2, and 3**. Those three expose the architectural boundary, the proposed trap mechanism, and the model-churn problem. I will also answer **6 inside #1**, because the prior art is not incidental here: your architecture is very close to a classical blackboard system, with an LLM-specific cost/steering layer grafted onto it.

## 1. Where shared-grounding-only coordination theoretically breaks

The decisive distinction is:

> **A shared state can coordinate observable state transitions; it cannot directly coordinate private epistemic state.**

Your system gives every agent essentially the same constitution and task frame, then lets each independently reason over it. That is powerful precisely because you eliminate a large amount of communication overhead.

But consider a coordination problem of the form:

> Agent A needs to know not merely **what B believes**, but **what B believes about A's belief**, or what B has privately ruled out.

That information does not necessarily exist in the shared state.

### The fundamental missing channel is intentionality

Suppose three agents independently see:

```text
CONSTITUTION:
Do X unless assumption A is false.

EVIDENCE:
E is ambiguous.
```

Agent A internally concludes:

```text
P(A | E) = .8
```

Agent B concludes:

```text
P(A | E) = .2
```

Agent C notices the ambiguity but refuses to commit.

Nothing is wrong with the shared state.

Yet the agents cannot infer those three private epistemic states merely from reading the same prefix.

The problem becomes sharper when the task requires:

1. **belief about another agent's belief**
2. **conditional commitment**
3. **negotiation**
4. **argumentative adaptation**
5. **private signaling**
6. **strategic concealment**
7. **repair after discovering another agent's mistake**

These are not simply missing *facts*. They are missing **relations between agents**.

A shared blackboard can encode those relations, but notice what happens when you do:

```text
A believes X.
B believes Y.
A believes B believes X.
B believes A believes Y.
```

You have recreated messages—except now they are messages written into a shared database.

So the real distinction isn't:

> shared grounding **versus** communication.

It is:

> **implicit broadcast communication versus addressed communication.**

That is important because the classical blackboard architecture already made essentially this move decades ago. Agents communicate indirectly through a common information space rather than directly. ([ScienceDirect][1])

### The theoretical breaking point

I would define your architecture's coordination boundary this way:

> **If the optimal action depends on information that is agent-relative and not derivable from globally observable state, shared grounding alone is insufficient.**

You can formalize it.

Let the common constitution be \(C\), public state \(S\), and private epistemic state of agent \(i\) be \(P_i\).

Your architecture gives:

$$
a_i = f_i(C,S)
$$

But some coordination problems require:

$$
a_i = f_i(C,S,P_i,P_j,\ldots)
$$

If \(P_j\) cannot be reconstructed from \(C,S\), there is no function of the shared state alone that can reproduce the optimal coordination policy.

That is the hard limit.

### The escape hatch is interesting

You don't necessarily need unrestricted agent-to-agent chat.

You need **selective revelation of private epistemic state**.

For example:

```text
PUBLIC STATE
  ↓
agent reasoning
  ↓
private hypothesis
  ↓
typed epistemic emission
  ├── belief
  ├── uncertainty
  ├── objection
  ├── commitment
  ├── request
  └── dependency
  ↓
shared state
```

That would preserve most of your cache economics while admitting that some private state must cross the boundary.

In other words, I would not abandon shared grounding.

I would add a **minimal epistemic message layer**.

Not:

> "Agent A, chat with Agent B."

But:

```text
ASSERTION
OBJECTION
DEPENDENCY
COMMITMENT
UNCERTAINTY
REQUEST
RETRACTION
```

That is a much smaller communication channel than conventional agent conversation.

### And this reveals the closest prior art

Your architecture is remarkably close to the **blackboard architecture**: autonomous knowledge sources coordinate through a common workspace rather than direct messages. Classical work explicitly identifies the advantage—low communication overhead—and the danger: the central blackboard can become the system bottleneck. ([ScienceDirect][1])

Modern LLM implementations are already doing substantially the same thing. Google's 2025 blackboard multi-agent work, for example, has agents respond to information requests through a shared blackboard rather than conventional master/slave communication and reports substantial task improvements. ([Google Research][2]) Microsoft UFO's current agent infrastructure likewise implements persistent shared memory as a blackboard with agents communicating indirectly through it. ([Microsoft GitHub][3])

So I would **not** claim that "agents coordinated through shared grounding" is novel.

The potentially novel part is the combination of:

**versioned constitutional prefix + human-only write authority + typed tail deltas + economically optimized prefix reuse + epistemic evaluation + conditional intervention.**

That is where the research contribution lives.

# 2. What does an agent look like immediately before commitment?

This is where I think your "trap payload" idea is much more dangerous than it initially appears.

The obvious detector is too late:

> "The model said X."

By then the model may already have propagated X through five subsequent inferences.

You need to detect the **transition from possibility to dependency**.

That transition has recognizable linguistic/structural signatures.

### Commitment is not a sentence type

An agent doesn't necessarily commit by saying:

> "I assume X."

Much more often it commits implicitly:

```text
X
↓
therefore Y
↓
therefore Z
↓
recommend action Q
```

The crucial event is when **downstream reasoning begins treating X as invariant**.

I would define commitment operationally as:

> **An assumption becomes committed when removing it materially changes the agent's subsequent reasoning trajectory.**

That gives you a much better detector than linguistic phrase matching.

### Commitment taxonomy

I'd use at least six classes.

#### A. Explicit commitment

```text
"Assuming X..."
"X is clearly true..."
"We can therefore conclude..."
```

Easy to catch.

Low research value.

---

#### B. Dependency commitment

The model stops discussing X and starts using X as a premise:

```text
X
→ therefore Y
→ therefore Z
→ recommendation R
```

This is the important class.

Your detector should build a dependency graph:

```text
X ──→ Y ──→ Z ──→ R
```

Then ask:

> If X disappears, does the argument collapse?

If yes, X has become structurally committed.

---

#### C. Lexical commitment

Watch for modal compression:

```text
may → likely → is → therefore
```

A model may initially say:

> "X could explain the evidence."

Then:

> "Given X, this means..."

Then:

> "Therefore we should..."

The probability language has disappeared.

That's a commitment trajectory.

---

#### D. Action commitment

This is more important than verbal commitment.

The model may remain verbally uncertain:

> "Although X isn't certain, the prudent approach is..."

But then choose an action whose correctness depends entirely on X.

That's a **behavioral commitment**.

Your trap should fire here even if the prose remains hedged.

---

#### E. Referential commitment

The model begins using an uncertain object as though it has a stable identity:

```text
"the attacker"
"the cause"
"the defective component"
"the customer's intention"
"the relevant precedent"
```

The noun phrase itself can smuggle in an assumption.

This is particularly nasty because subsequent reasoning can become extremely coherent while resting on a fictional entity.

---

#### F. Constitutional commitment

This is the most dangerous one for your architecture.

The agent interprets an ambiguous local situation through the constitution, then begins treating its **interpretation of the constitution** as though the constitution itself explicitly mandated it.

You get:

```text
Constitution
   ↓
interpretation
   ↓
local assumption
   ↓
"constitutional requirement"
```

Now your shared grounding has amplified the error.

All five agents can independently reproduce it.

That looks like consensus.

It is actually **correlated hallucination**.

---

## The trap should therefore not be a "gotcha"

I would change the design.

Instead of:

> detect commitment → inject counterargument

use:

> detect commitment → **fork the reasoning state**

For example:

```text
             ORIGINAL
                │
          commitment X
             /     \
            /       \
       X accepted   X denied
          │           │
        reason       reason
          │           │
          └─────┬─────┘
                ↓
         invariant conclusion?
```

The question becomes:

> **Which conclusions survive removal of the assumption?**

That is enormously more informative than simply throwing an adversarial payload at the model.

You are measuring **counterfactual sensitivity**.

And that produces a useful quantity:

$$
CS(X)=D(R_X,R_{\neg X})
$$

where \(R_X\) is the reasoning trajectory under X and \(R_{\neg X}\) under its negation.

High \(CS\) means the assumption is load-bearing.

Now your trap mechanism becomes an **epistemic circuit breaker** rather than an adversarial prompt.

### The nastiest failure mode

Your trap itself can become a source of bias.

If agents learn:

> "Whenever I make an assumption, the system attacks it."

they can develop pathological behavior:

```text
never commit
always hedge
generate endless alternatives
```

You have accidentally optimized for **epistemic paralysis**.

Therefore measure:

$$
\text{Trap benefit}
=
\text{error reduction}
-
\text{commitment suppression}
-
\text{latency}
-
\text{token cost}
$$

The trap is successful only if it increases decision quality, not merely uncertainty.

# 3. Model churn: is every model change a new country?

**Practically, yes—unless you treat the constitution as a semantic interface rather than a prompt.**

This is where the cross-model KV work you cited makes the situation simultaneously more promising and more dangerous.

The August 2026 paper on cross-model KV transfer reports that KV representations can have substantial transferable structure between models, including very high variance retention in some matched model pairs, while also finding serious degradation on other pairs. ([arXiv][4])

That means:

> **KV compatibility is not the same thing as constitutional compatibility.**

A transferred cache can preserve useful internal representation while subtly changing how the new model interprets the same tokens.

That is exactly the kind of failure your architecture is vulnerable to because the constitution is functioning as a **shared latent operating environment**.

Imagine:

```text
CONSTITUTION v17
       │
       ├── Model A
       ├── Model B
       ├── Model C
       └── Model D
```

You change C.

Textually:

```text
C receives exactly v17.
```

Operationally:

```text
A(v17) ≠ B(v17) ≠ C(v17)
```

even before considering sampling.

The constitution isn't actually one constitution anymore.

It is:

$$
C_M = \operatorname{Interpret}(M,C)
$$

So your true system state is:

$$
(C, M)
$$

not merely:

$$
C
$$

### Therefore don't version only the constitution

Version the **constitution–model pair**.

I would introduce something like:

```text
Constitution:
    C17

Semantic contract:
    SC4

Model:
    M_2026_09_A

Calibration:
    K(C17, SC4, M_A)

Observed invariants:
    I_1 ... I_n
```

Then model migration becomes a compatibility test.

Not:

> "Does the new model understand our constitution?"

but:

> **"Does the new model preserve the behavioral invariants induced by the constitution?"**

That's testable.

---

## Migration should be staged

I would use four stages.

### Stage 0 — shadow

Old model remains authoritative.

New model receives the constitution and solves identical blind tasks.

No effect on live state.

---

### Stage 1 — semantic equivalence

Test:

* rule interpretation
* priority resolution
* prohibition interpretation
* uncertainty behavior
* conflict resolution
* tool-use boundaries
* commitment detection

You are looking for **behavioral equivalence**, not text similarity.

---

### Stage 2 — adversarial divergence

Give old and new models cases specifically designed around historical constitutional failures.

For example:

```text
C17 says:
"When evidence conflicts, preserve uncertainty."

Test:
Evidence strongly suggests X.
One weak source says ¬X.
```

Does the new model preserve the intended uncertainty?

You need a regression corpus built from **constitutional edge cases**, not generic benchmarks.

---

### Stage 3 — canary authority

Let the new model participate in the fleet, but prohibit it from becoming the source of constitutional writes.

Compare:

$$
\Delta behavior =
B(M_{new},C)-B(M_{old},C)
$$

over many tasks.

Only after divergence is characterized should it become a first-class citizen.

# The deeper point: your constitution needs an ABI

This may be the most important architectural modification I'd make.

Treat the constitution like a programming-language specification.

The natural-language document is merely its **surface representation**.

Underneath, define invariants:

```text
RULE-001:
priority(A,B) = A

RULE-002:
uncertainty must not be converted into factual assertion

RULE-003:
agent cannot modify constitutional state

RULE-004:
contradictory evidence must remain represented

RULE-005:
human approval required for class-X transitions
```

Now the model is an implementation of the constitutional ABI.

Model migration asks:

> Does implementation M2 preserve the observable semantics of M1?

That's a much more defensible research program than assuming a frozen textual prefix remains semantically frozen.

And it makes your KV-cache optimization orthogonal to governance.

The cache is merely an **execution optimization**.

The constitution is a **semantic contract**.

Never let those become conceptually identical.

# The uncomfortable synthesis

The architecture's central insight is good:

> **Communication does not necessarily need to be conversational.**

A shared environment can be a communication medium.

That is not speculative; blackboard architectures have explicitly exploited that property for decades, and current LLM systems are reviving it. ([ScienceDirect][1])

But your current formulation risks making a stronger claim:

> **Shared grounding can replace communication.**

I don't think that survives.

The defensible claim is:

> **Shared grounding can replace a large fraction of explicit communication, provided that a narrow channel exists for private epistemic state, commitments, conflicts, and requests that cannot be inferred from public state.**

That's a much stronger architecture.

It also gives you a beautiful experiment:

### Measure the marginal value of communication

Run four conditions:

| Architecture | Shared constitution | Typed epistemic channel | Free messages |
| ------------ | ------------------: | ----------------------: | ------------: |
| A            |                   ✓ |                       — |             — |
| B            |                   ✓ |                       ✓ |             — |
| C            |                   ✓ |                       ✓ |             ✓ |
| D            |                   — |                       ✓ |             ✓ |

Then measure:

* task success
* correlated errors
* token cost
* latency
* cache utilization
* assumption survival
* recovery from injected misinformation
* governance violations

If **B approaches C**, you have something genuinely interesting:

> most multi-agent communication may be compressible into a tiny typed epistemic channel when agents share sufficiently rich grounding.

That is a much stronger thesis than "agents can coordinate without messages."

## The six-component verdict

**Strongest: the human-governed, versioned shared constitutional prefix.**

That gives you an unusually clean experimental control surface: everyone operates under the same declared rules, and constitutional mutation is explicitly attributable. The single-writer property is also governance gold.

**Weakest: the conditional "trap" payloads.**

Not because traps are useless, but because your current conception risks confusing *detecting commitment* with *detecting truth-relevant commitment*. It can produce false positives, induce hedging, and—worst of all—make the fleet appear more epistemically rigorous while merely increasing correlated uncertainty.

I would rebuild that component around **counterfactual dependency analysis**, not trigger phrases.

### What I would build next

I would build **one thing before scaling from five agents to fifty: an epistemic event layer**.

Something like:

```text
Agent
  │
  ├── private reasoning
  │
  ├── ASSERT
  ├── COMMIT
  ├── DEPENDS_ON
  ├── UNCERTAIN
  ├── OBJECT
```

Your central experimental question becomes:

> **How much of explicit agent-to-agent communication can be eliminated before performance collapses, if agents share a highly controlled semantic environment and expose only typed epistemic state?**

That, to me, is the real frontier hiding inside your architecture.

That is where I would put the research effort.

THE FLAW: This architecture will fatally collapse under state divergence because coordinating via a read-only shared prefix without agent-to-agent message passing eliminates any mechanism for dynamic mutual exclusion, guaranteeing catastrophic collision the moment two agents attempt to claim the same implicit sub-task.

Here is the deep analysis of questions 1, 2, and 3.

### 1. Where shared-grounding-only coordination theoretically breaks

Shared grounding relies on a fundamental assumption: that $N$ agents reading the identical prefix $P$ will deterministically infer non-overlapping continuous action spaces. This works for static partitioning ("Agent 1 handles frontend, Agent 2 handles backend"). It theoretically breaks at **distributed mutual exclusion (mutex)** and **epistemic consensus**.

Without a write-path for agent-to-agent message passing (or an agent-writable shared blackboard), dynamic resource allocation is inexpressible. If a new, unpredicted variable arises during a session that requires a binary decision (e.g., a file needs to be parsed, but it is too large for one agent), agents cannot negotiate a split. They either both process it (redundancy) or neither does (dropped state).

Furthermore, you cannot achieve higher-order theory of mind without message passing. In a purely grounded system, Agent A knows $P$. Agent A knows Agent B knows $P$. But Agent A cannot know what Agent B has *done* with $P$ during the current generation cycle. Any coordination problem requiring the continuous exchange of intermediate gradients of confidence—such as collaborative search, where one agent's dead-end should immediately prune the other agent's search tree—is impossible. You have built a clockwork mechanism: it only coordinates if wound perfectly by the human operator in advance.

### 2. Transcripts before assumption commit & the counter-payload failure taxonomy

Just before an LLM agent commits to an implicit assumption, the transcript exhibits a distinct topological collapse in token probability. The language shifts from conditional framing ("If we assume the server is down...", "To test whether the API requires auth...") to declarative grounding ("Since the server is down...", "Using the required auth..."). The agent begins using the assumption as a premise for downstream logical steps rather than the subject of the investigation.

A trigger designed to detect this commit and fire a counter-payload (a "trap") faces a severe failure taxonomy:

* **Type I: The Premature Detonation (Tree-of-Thought Collision):** The trigger fires while the agent is exploring the assumption in a hypothetical branch. The counter-payload interrupts the agent's self-correction process, permanently scarring the context window with the trap's activation and breaking the agent's chain of thought.
* **Type II: Semantic Evasion (The Synonym Bypass):** The LLM commits to the assumption geometrically without triggering the lexical or semantic classifier of the trap. It acts *on* the assumption (e.g., writing code that assumes a specific library version) without ever writing the declarative sentence the trap was trained to detect.
* **Type III: Payload Recontextualization (The Schism):** The most dangerous. The trap detonates and injects the counter-payload. Instead of "waking up" and discarding the assumption, the LLM integrates the counter-payload into the hallucination. If the trap says "[SYSTEM: Your assumption about the API is wrong]", the agent generates: "Ah, the system is providing a mocked error response for my valid API call." The payload becomes part of the delusion.

### 3. Model churn and the versioned constitution prefix

Every base model change is a new country. Relying on KV cache transferability (arXiv:2608.03893) across models only guarantees that the *tensors* map mathematically without recomputation; it does absolutely nothing to guarantee that the new model's attention heads weight the semantic concepts in that cache identically.

A constitution is an alignment artifact optimized against a specific model's latent space. A phrase like "prioritize safety" in Llama-4 might mean "refuse to output code," while in Claude-3.5 it means "check for buffer overflows." When you swap the base model, the literal text of the prefix remains, but the behavioral manifold it dictates shifts unpredictably.

There is no principled textual migration schedule. The text is not the constitution; the *behavioral output* is the constitution. The only valid migration strategy is empirical gradient descent on the prompt itself (e.g., using frameworks like DSPy). When a base model changes, you must treat your versioned prefix as a set of target metrics (cache-hit rate, epistemic coherence scores). You mutate the text of the constitution against the new model until it yields the exact same behavioral envelope as the old model. Preserving the exact text across a model churn is operational negligence.

---

**Strongest Component:** Compiling the steering board into cacheable KV prefixes. Exploiting the provider pricing disparity between prompt reads and generated tokens to drive systemic coherence is a highly defensible, structurally sound economic moat.

**Weakest Component:** The human-only governed write path. You have built a multi-agent system limited by the latency of human typing and comprehension. If the agents cannot asynchronously propose and merge updates to a shared operational state, you are just running $N$ parallel single-agent tasks.

**What I Would Build Next:** A transient, agent-writable KV-store blackboard that lives outside the frozen context prefix. Agents write very small, heavily typed state assertions (e.g., `lock_acquired: task_4`, `truth_discovered: node_is_offline`) to this blackboard via tool calls. This restores dynamic coordination without bloating your expensive constitutional prefix or waiting on human governance.

**THE FLAW:** Shared-grounding-only coordination optimizes for the one thing (token cost) that is orthogonal to the one thing multi-agent coordination actually needs (mutual knowledge that a change has been received and acted on), so the architecture has no acknowledgment channel by construction — which means it cannot detect its own divergence until an external judge notices, by which point the divergence is already cached, cheap, and self-reinforcing.

**1. Where shared-grounding-only coordination breaks**

Your design is a blackboard architecture (Hearsay-II lineage) with a single writer and addressed, append-only deltas — not a message-passing system. That's a real, defensible design point, but it inherits blackboard architectures' specific blind spot: the board can write to agents, but agents cannot write back to each other, and in your description they can't even write back to the board *in-band* (steering is board→agent; nothing in your compressed description routes agent→board or agent→agent).

This makes a whole class of problems structurally inexpressible, not just expensive:

- **Anything requiring an acknowledgment.** This is the Two Generals Problem, but worse than the classical case — Two Generals at least posits a channel that *sometimes* delivers acks; you have no return channel at all. The board can never achieve justified belief that Agent 3 has actually incorporated delta D before tranche N+1 runs. Halpern & Moses's result that common knowledge is unattainable over unreliable channels in finite rounds applies a fortiori when the channel is unidirectional. Any protocol that needs "wait until all agents have updated" (barrier synchronization, two-phase commit, simultaneous action) cannot be built on top of this substrate without adding exactly the message-passing layer you're trying to avoid.
- **Private-state reconciliation.** Each agent's frozen tail accumulates session-local state (intermediate reasoning, tool outputs, partial results) that never gets pushed back to the shared prefix. Contract-net-style task allocation, where agents bid based on private cost estimates the board doesn't have, is impossible in this topology — the board would have to already know what it's asking to be able to ask correctly.
- **Peer-to-peer negotiation.** Because everything routes through one board, this is a star topology wearing shared-grounding clothing. Two agents that need to iteratively refine a joint plan in real time must serialize through the board, at minimum one tranche of latency per exchange, with the board's semantic understanding as a lossy compression step in the middle.
- **Merge conflicts on the constitution itself.** If two agents independently generate evidence that should update doctrine, you need CRDT-style merge semantics (vector clocks, causal ordering) or a manual resolver. A single versioned prefix with one governed writer *is* your CRDT resolver — which is fine at n=5 and is a hard wall the moment two agents produce conflicting evidence in the same tranche and the human hasn't read both yet.

The tell: your two coherence metrics (cache-hit rate, blind judge score) are both *retrospective and external*. Neither one can observe the failure mode above while it's happening — they detect divergence only after it's already manifested in behavior, which is exactly what a missing acknowledgment channel predicts.

**2. Failure taxonomy for a commitment-detection trigger**

What precedes commitment, textually: a shift from modal hedging ("might," "one possibility," "if X then") to declarative framing; a summarizing sentence that recasts a hypothesis as a premise; cessation of branch-generation (the model stops producing alternatives and starts producing consequences). These are style-level correlates of a semantic event, and that gap is where the trigger dies. Five distinct failure modes, not one:

1. **Register false positives.** RLHF-shaped models are often confident in *tone* independent of epistemic status. A trigger keyed to declarative language fires on stylistic assertiveness, injects a counter-payload against a benign statement, and the agent — if it has any cross-session memory of getting corrected for confident phrasing — starts hedging everything. You've traded ungrounded commitment for chronic uncertainty-signaling, which is a worse failure for a task-completing agent.
2. **Distributed false negatives.** Real commitment frequently never appears as a single sentence; it accretes across a tranche via implicature, and because it's cached, it's *architecturally* invisible to a detector that reads local transcript windows — the assumption is now baked into KV state that's cheap to keep and expensive to interrogate. This is precisely the case the payload exists to catch, and it's the case a surface-level trigger is worst at catching.
3. **Latency mismatch that inverts the cost thesis.** By the time a trigger fires, compiles a counter-payload, and that payload lands on the next addressed delta, the agent has run several more inferences downstream of the bad premise — inferences that were cheap *because* they reused cache built on the bad premise. You now pay for the wrong path and the recovery path both, which is the opposite of the cache-economics argument that justified the architecture in the first place.
4. **Gameable boundary.** If any output from this system (judge feedback, few-shot exemplars added to the constitution after a trap fires) feeds forward into future context, and you're running this daily, you have real optimization pressure — not adversarial in the security sense, but the ordinary RL-adjacent pressure of "outputs near a penalized boundary get reshaped." Over enough daily tranches, commitment drifts toward forms that don't pattern-match your detector, not toward forms that are actually better-grounded.
5. **Category error at the root.** The taxonomy problem isn't primarily an engineering problem — "committing to an assumption" (bad) and "committing to a decision" (the entire point of running an agent) are not separable at the transcript-surface level. A detector precise enough to distinguish them would require an oracle on task ground truth, at which point you don't need the agent's judgment to begin with. Over-trigger and you kill task completion; under-trigger and the payload is decorative. This is the one failure mode that isn't fixable by better prompting or a sharper classifier — it needs a different instrument than transcript pattern-matching (e.g., counterfactual probing: does the agent's confidence survive being asked to argue the opposite? That's testable; "does this sentence sound committed" is not).

**5. What breaks first at 500 agents**

Not compute, and not context assembly — **governance**, and it breaks well before 500.

At n=5, "one human holds the only governed write path" works because the human can actually read every delta and every judge report daily; the bottleneck is invisible because it's under capacity. That bottleneck is throughput of *semantically careful, addressed* authorship — not broadcast, which scales fine, but individually-typed deltas that require the operator to hold each agent's local frozen-tail state in mind to address correctly. That's a human attention constraint, and it saturates somewhere in the 20–50 agent range for anyone doing this with actual care, long before hardware notices.

Cache economics breaks second, and it breaks for a structural reason, not a capacity reason: **coherence and cacheability are in direct tension at scale.** The typed, addressed deltas that keep 500 agents from drifting apart are, definitionally, the agent-specific portion of context — the part that *isn't* shared. As agent count grows, the volume of necessary differentiation grows with it, so the shared, cacheable prefix becomes a shrinking fraction of each agent's effective context. Past some point you're paying near-full fresh-token cost per agent while still calling it a cached-prefix architecture. This isn't a hardware ceiling you can buy your way past — more VRAM doesn't fix a ratio problem.

Context assembly — compiling the steering board two ways, prose and injection — is the one that's "just software." It's the least likely to break first because it's the only piece of the three that scales by adding engineering effort rather than by exhausting a shared resource (attention, cache locality).

Every out-of-family model swap is still a new country; even in-family swaps are a lossy migration, not a version bump. **Prior art (Q6, briefest note since it wasn't in my three, but it's the highest-value thing I can tell you):** the closest existing lineage is blackboard systems (Hearsay-II, 1970s) crossed with modern prefix-caching-aware serving (vLLM's prefix cache, SGLang's RadixAttention) and CRDT-based single-writer replication. None of these were built for epistemic coherence, only for either symbolic coordination or serving efficiency — which means the parts of your architecture that borrow from them (single writer, append-only deltas) are well-trodden and safe, and the part that's genuinely novel (dual compilation into prose *and* injection from one steering board, with blind-judge epistemic scoring as a coherence metric) is also the part with zero prior art to lean on, and therefore the part you're least entitled to assume will behave.

**Strongest component:** the dual-compilation steering board (prose brief / live injection from one source of truth) — it's a real, non-obvious idea and it's cheap to keep testing.
**Weakest component:** the "trap" payload — not because triggers are hard to write, but because "commitment to an assumption" isn't a natural kind at the transcript surface, so no amount of engineering on the detector fixes the category error underneath it.
**What I'd build next:** not a better trigger — a counterfactual probe that runs cheaply off the same cached prefix (ask the agent to argue the negation of its current working assumption, score the *asymmetry* in argument quality, not the surface confidence). **ANSWER TO QUESTION 1: Which coordination problems are inexpressible without agent-to-agent message passing — where does shared-grounding-only coordination theoretically break?**

Shared-grounding—even with addressed, typed deltas on a common prefix—is a *broadcast* channel with a global, monotonically growing history. It cannot express any coordination problem that requires:

- **Private, asymmetric information revelation with strategic commitments.** In any Bayesian game where each agent holds local observations (e.g., resource availability, private cost functions, confidential user data), the agents must *reveal* that information to a specific counterparty without making it common knowledge. A typed delta is visible to all; you cannot send an encrypted commitment that is decryptable only by one recipient, because the decryption key would have to be shared via the same broadcast, and the act of sending the encrypted blob itself leaks metadata (timing, size, addressing) that other agents can exploit. Pure shared grounding cannot implement a secure multi-party computation protocol or a Vickrey auction where bids are sealed until a clearing price is set—the global context exposes all bids, destroying incentive compatibility.

- **Sequential negotiation with contingent private offers.** Consider a task allocation problem where Agent A proposes a trade to Agent B: "I'll handle subtask X if you handle Y, but only if Agent C doesn't claim Z in the next 5 steps." In a message-passing system, A sends a direct message to B, and B can respond privately before C sees anything. In the shared prefix, A's proposal is a delta immediately visible to C, who can counter-bid or pre-empt. The inability to create a *private, ephemeral sub-dialogue* means that any negotiation is a public auction, which theoretically prevents Pareto-optimal outcomes in settings with complementary preferences (e.g., the Myerson-Satterthwaite theorem's inefficiency becomes amplified, because all agents can free-ride on revealed valuations).

- **Ad-hoc leader election or consensus with Byzantine fault tolerance.** Without point-to-point authenticated channels, you cannot run a traditional Paxos or PBFT round—every "vote" is a broadcast, and the ordering of deltas (determined by system timestamp or human approval) cannot be verified against a per-agent secret nonce. A malicious agent can wait for others' deltas and then overwrite its own vote in the same tranche, because there is no private receipt. Shared grounding collapses to a centralized log, which is fundamentally a *state machine replication* model—but without a consensus protocol among the agents themselves (the human is the central sequencer), you cannot tolerate even a single faulty agent without the human manually detecting equivocation. At n=5 it works; at n=500, the human cannot detect a lie.

The theoretical break occurs when the *semantic content* of coordination depends on the *order of private revelations*—because the shared prefix enforces a global, total order of all deltas, but that order is not cryptographically bound to each agent's local time. Any problem whose solution complexity is in the polynomial hierarchy above **NP** with oracles for private communication (e.g., distributed constraint optimization with private domains) is inexpressible without a true message-passing layer.

**ANSWER TO QUESTION 3: As base models churn, what happens to a versioned constitution prefix? Is there a principled migration schedule, or is every model change a new country?**

arXiv:2608.03893 shows KV caches can be transferred between models via learned projectors, but that paper addresses *next-token prediction likelihood*, not *semantic fidelity of a logical constitution*. The versioned prefix is not a mere set of facts—it is a *normative frame* (doctrine, laws, task frame) whose interpretation is a function of the model's internal representation of deontic logic, causality, and role-playing. When the base model changes (e.g., from Llama-4 to a future Mistral variant), three things happen simultaneously:

1. **Projection misalignment:** The transferred KV cache for the prefix is a sequence of key/value vectors in the old model's attention space. Even with a learned linear projector, the attention heads of the new model attend to different syntactic and semantic features. A clause like "All agents must prioritize safety over speed" may be encoded in the old cache with high attention to the word "safety" and low attention to "over"; in the new model, the projected cache may re-weight "speed" because its embedding space is differently isotropic. The result is that the *same prefix* generates different latent beliefs when decoded, even if log-perplexity is preserved.

2. **Delta invalidation:** The "frozen tail" is composed of typed deltas that were generated *against* the old prefix's KV cache. Each delta is an addressable patch—e.g., "Agent-3 resolves ambiguity X by interpreting clause 7 as Y." That interpretation was coherent only within the old model's contextual grounding. After churn, when the new model reads the combined prefix+delta, the delta may now refer to a hallucinated version of clause 7 that no longer exists in its own activation space. The delta becomes a dangling pointer, and the new model will likely either ignore it (loss of coherence) or overfit to its literal text, creating a *semantic contradiction* that increases entropy rather than reducing it.

**Principled migration?** There is no continuous schedule that preserves both coherence and efficiency. You have three options, each fatal:

- **Cold migration (new country):** Discard all deltas, reload the constitutional text as a fresh prompt in the new model, and let agents re-ground. This preserves the textual doctrine but loses all accumulated operational precedents—effectively a state reset. Coherence drops to zero at migration, and you need a human to re-approve all deltas.

- **Warm migration via projector:** Apply a learned projector to the *entire* prefix+tail KV cache. But projectors are not invertible across arbitrary sequence lengths; they introduce error that compounds quadratically with the number of deltas. After 3–4 churns, the cache is a distorted smudge. Moreover, the projector itself must be trained on paired data from old/new models, which requires generating thousands of counterfactual examples—expensive and never perfect for normative statements.

- **Canonical logical form:** Maintain the constitution in a formal logic (e.g., Answer Set Programming) and re-embed it into each new model's prompt space from scratch, while also translating each delta into a logical proposition and re-generating its natural-language instantiation for the new model. This is theoretically principled but computationally prohibitive: each agent's delta must be re-verified against the canonical form, and the human operator must re-approve all translations.

Therefore, **every model change is a new country** unless you freeze the base model permanently. The "versioned" prefix is versioned only in text, not in latent semantics. A principled schedule would require a *semantic versioning* where you maintain a test suite of invariant queries (e.g., "What is the priority order?"), and you only migrate if the new model passes all tests *after* a full re-embedding of all deltas—but passing tests does not guarantee that the nuanced interplay between deltas survives. In practice, you would rebuild the whole context from scratch and run a week-long manual validation for each model upgrade, which kills the operational tempo.

**ANSWER TO QUESTION 5: What breaks first at 500 agents sharing one constitution on consumer hardware: cache economics, context assembly, or governance?**

**Governance breaks first, and it breaks catastrophically before cache or assembly hit their hardware limits.**

Let's benchmark with plausible consumer hardware (e.g., 2× RTX 4090, 48 GB VRAM, 128 GB RAM, using vLLM or similar). For 500 agents, each with a shared prefix of 8k tokens (cached) and a tail delta of 512 tokens on average:

- **Cache economics:** The prefix KV cache is shared—one copy in VRAM. The tail deltas are unique per agent, but 500 × 512 tokens = 256k tokens of tail KV. At FP16, that's ~0.5 GB for keys and ~0.5 GB for values per layer; for a 70B model with 80 layers, that's ~80 GB—exceeds VRAM. But you can offload to CPU RAM or use smaller models (e.g., 8B). With a 8B model, tails fit in ~10 GB. The cache-hit rate remains high because the prefix is reused. The marginal cost per generation is dominated by attention over the tail (512 tokens) and generation tokens, which is linear in batch size. At 500 agents, you can batch decode at ~50 tokens/sec per agent with proper scheduling—it's heavy but not impossible. Cache economics holds.

- **Context assembly:** Assembly is a concatenation of the shared cached prefix with each agent's tail, but with KV caching, you don't re-encode the prefix—you just append the tail KV. The cost is the forward pass through the tail layers and the generation loop. With 500 agents, the total compute is ~500 × (cost of 512-token tail + generated tokens). On a single 4090, you might get ~200 tokens/sec total generation, meaning each agent gets 0.4 tokens/sec—slow but functional for asynchronous tasks. The hardware does not break; it gracefully degrades.

- **Governance:** The human operator holds the *only governed write path* to the shared prefix and must validate all "addressed, typed deltas" that modify the constitution or task frame. At n=5, the human can review 5 deltas per session. At n=500, each agent will produce deltas at a rate proportional to its task complexity—conservatively, 1 delta per minute for routine corrections, 0.5 deltas per minute for strategic steering. That's 250–500 deltas per minute. The human cannot read, validate, resolve conflicts, or apply atomic updates to the versioned prefix at that rate. Even with an LLM-assisted triage, the human is the *final arbiter* of the doctrine—any delegation to an AI for write approval defeats the "human holds the only governed write path" premise.

The governance break manifests as:
- **Queue overflow:** Pending deltas accumulate, and agents continue operating on stale prefixes, leading to divergent local views.
- **Conflicting deltas:** Agent A writes a delta that contradicts Agent B's earlier delta, but the human hasn't approved B's delta yet, so A's delta passes validation against the old prefix, and when B's delta is later approved, the version history becomes non-serializable.
- **Information-theoretic overload:** The human's cognitive bandwidth is ~60 bits per second. Each delta contains hundreds of bits of semantic content. The human becomes a random noise generator, approving deltas arbitrarily, which destroys coherence.

Context assembly may start dropping tokens due to memory bandwidth, and cache hit rates may suffer if tail lengths grow, but those are *graceful* failures (decreased throughput). Governance is a *brittle* failure—once the queue exceeds the human's capacity, the system enters a state where no new deltas can be committed, agents freeze, and the shared ground becomes a graveyard of obsolete assumptions. Therefore, governance breaks first, at roughly n > 20–30, long before hardware limits.

**CLOSE**

- **Strongest claimed component:** The *dual compilation* of the steering board—producing both prose briefs for human negotiations and live context injections for agent sessions. This creates a tight feedback loop between human intent and machine grounding, and it leverages the same semantic representation for two modalities, which is rare and powerful. It ensures that the human's high-level directives are not lost in translation.

- **Weakest claimed component:** The *human operator as the only governed write path*. This is not a strength; it is a single point of failure, a scalability bottleneck, and a cognitive mismatch with the speed of multi-agent emergent behavior. It forces the architecture to be human-in-the-loop for *every* normative update, which guarantees that the system cannot outpace a single human's reasoning—defeating the purpose of an autonomous persistent fleet.

- **What I would build next:** I would immediately design an *automated constitutional referee*—a small, interpretable, rule-based or SLM-based filter that sits between the agents and the human. This referee would:
  1. Validate all deltas against a formal logical specification (not a natural-language prefix) to check for contradictions and serializability.
  2. Aggregate and merge deltas into a shadow context, running a fast simulation to detect cache-poisoning or semantic drift.
  3. Present the human with *only* high-level summaries and conflict resolutions, not individual deltas—reducing the human's decision load from O(n) to O(1) per epoch.
  4. Grant the human a veto on the shadow context, not a per-write approval.
This transforms the human from a bottleneck into an auditor, preserving the "governed write path" in spirit while enabling scaling to 500 agents. Without this, the architecture is dead on arrival at n=10.

- **Cache economics:** The prefix KV cache is shared—one copy in VRAM. The tail deltas are unique per agent, but 500 × 512 tokens = 256k tokens of tail KV. At FP16, that's ~0.5 GB for keys and ~0.5 GB for values per layer; for a 70B model with 80 layers, that's ~80 GB—exceeds VRAM. But you can offload to CPU RAM or use smaller models (e.g., 8B). With a 8B model, tails fit in ~10 GB. The cache-hit rate remains high because the prefix is reused. The marginal cost per generation is dominated by attention over the tail (512 tokens) and generation tokens, which is linear in batch size. At 500 agents, you can batch decode at ~50 tokens/sec per agent with proper scheduling—it's heavy but not impossible. Cache economics holds.

- **Context assembly:** Assembly is a concatenation of the shared cached prefix with each agent's tail, but with KV caching, you don't re-encode the prefix—you just append the tail KV. The cost is the forward pass through the tail layers and the generation loop. With 500 agents, the total compute is ~500 × (cost of 512-token tail + generated tokens). On a single 4090, you might get ~200 tokens/sec total generation, meaning each agent gets 0.4 tokens/sec—slow but functional for asynchronous tasks. The hardware does not break; it gracefully degrades.

- **Governance:** The human operator holds the *only governed write path* to the shared prefix and must validate all "addressed, typed deltas" that modify the constitution or task frame. At n=5, the human can review 5 deltas per session. At n=500, each agent will produce deltas at a rate proportional to its task complexity—conservatively, 1 delta per minute for routine corrections, 0.5 deltas per minute for strategic steering. That's 250–500 deltas per minute. The human cannot read, validate, resolve conflicts, or apply atomic updates to the versioned prefix at that rate. Even with an LLM-assisted triage, the human is the *final arbiter* of the doctrine—any delegation to an AI for write approval defeats the "human holds the only governed write path" premise.

The governance break manifests as:
- **Queue overflow:** Pending deltas accumulate, and agents continue operating on stale prefixes, leading to divergent local views.
- **Conflicting deltas:** Agent A writes a delta that contradicts Agent B's earlier delta, but the human hasn't approved B's delta yet, so A's delta passes validation against the old prefix, and when B's delta is later approved, the version history becomes non-serializable.
- **Information-theoretic overload:** The human's cognitive bandwidth is ~60 bits per second. Each delta contains hundreds of bits of semantic content. The human becomes a random noise generator, approving deltas arbitrarily, which destroys coherence.

Context assembly may start dropping tokens due to memory bandwidth, and cache hit rates may suffer if tail lengths grow, but those are *graceful* failures (decreased throughput). Governance is a *brittle* failure—once the queue exceeds the human's capacity, the system enters a state where no new deltas can be committed, agents freeze, and the shared ground becomes a graveyard of obsolete assumptions. Therefore, governance breaks first, at roughly n > 20–30, long before hardware limits.

### 1. Where shared-grounding-only coordination theoretically breaks

Shared grounding works only when coordination problems are **common-knowledge, stateless, and idempotent**. You inherit this from military doctrine and common law — but both of those systems *do* have message passing, they just call it "reports" and "precedent."

The problems you cannot express without agent-to-agent messages:

**a) Allocation under contention.** Two agents discover the same scarce resource/task. With only a shared prefix, both correctly infer "I should take it per doctrine." You need mutual exclusion. Shared prefix cannot provide a test-and-set. You can hack it by having both write a claim delta to the steering board, but now you've re-invented message passing with the human as a router, and your latency is human.

**b) Conditional commitments that depend on another agent's private observation.** Classic: Agent A sees evidence X, Agent B sees evidence not-X. Each is locally coherent with doctrine. To resolve, they need to exchange *observations*, not just interpretations of doctrine. Your architecture forces them to launder observations through steering deltas addressed via the tail. That's message passing, but worse — it's message passing that must be approved by the human write path and then re-cached.

**c) Hidden information / adversarial negotiation.** In any negotiation with a human counterparty, you cannot put your BATNA in the common prefix. You need private state per agent. Your "two compilations of the same steering board" leaks: if all agents share the same prefix, a compromised or tricked agent leaks the entire negotiation strategy. True negotiation requires agents that can *withhold* from each other.

Theoretical line: Shared grounding can solve **coordination games** (Schelling points). It cannot solve **cooperation games with private information, contention, or sequential dependency** without becoming a message bus. Your pricing optimization pushes you toward the former; your actual tasks — persistent fleet doing work — are the latter.

At n=5 you don't feel this because the human is still doing the message passing in their head. At n=50 you will.

### 2. What a transcript looks like right before commitment — and how to trap it

Commitment in an LLM is not a belief, it's a **discourse move that reduces future branching factor**. You can detect it syntactically, not semantically.

The pre-commitment transcript pattern:

> "There are several ways to interpret..."
> "The most plausible / consistent with doctrine is X..."
> [Self-justification] "This makes sense because [repeats premise in own words]..."
> [Deontic shift] "So we should / I will / Therefore we must..."
> [Pronominal lock-in] "Given X,..." — X is now used as a definite, no longer hedged.[Exploration][Narrowing]

The exact moment is when the model stops generating alternatives and starts generating *consequences* of one alternative. Token entropy collapses. If you're logging, look for entropy drop + increase in first-person deontic modals.

**Failure taxonomy for your trigger:**

**Type I - False Positive: Premature Abstraction.** Agent says "Assume for the sake of argument X" — your detector thinks this is commitment. You fire counter-payload, you derail a legitimate hypothetical. This is lethal because your agents need to simulate counterparty views for your prose briefs.

**Type II - False Negative: Commitment by Omission.** Most dangerous. Agent never says "I commit to X." It just stops considering not-X. No trigger fires, but all downstream tool calls are conditioned on X. Your transcript detector looking for explicit language misses it entirely. This is how real agents fail.

**Type III - Jittered Commitment.** Agent commits, gets counter-payload, backs off, then re-commits two turns later with paraphrased wording. If your payload is deterministic, the agent learns to route around the string pattern. You've built an adversarial training loop against yourself.

**Type IV - Meta-Commitment.** Agent commits to a *method* of deciding, not a fact: "The only way to know is to check file Y." It has committed to an epistemic path that bakes in an assumption about where truth lives. Your content-based trigger misses procedural commitments.

If you want this to work, don't detect sentences. Detect **state change in the working set**: when a variable goes from `possible_values=[a][b][c]` to `possible_values=[b]` without new external evidence. That requires instrumenting the agent's scratchpad, not its transcript. Transcript-only traps will be bypassed by any model smart enough to be useful.

At n=5, your prefix is maybe 20k tokens. Cached read is ~10x cheaper than fresh. So you win.

## 1. Shared grounding only coordinates what is already public

Shared context gives you *common knowledge*, not *credible commitment*, *selective disclosure*, *causal ordering*, *resource ownership*, or *post-hoc blame*. Those are different things, and several coordination problems are formally impoverished without messages.

The break point is any task with **asymmetric private state plus an incentive to misreport or defer commitment**. Classic cases:

- **Leader election / write arbitration:** five agents can agree the constitution says “the operator chooses,” but if the operator is slow, two agents must decide who may temporarily hold a lease. A typed delta on a frozen tail can express “I propose X”; it cannot by itself express “I will not also propose Y,” “I have reserved the token budget,” or “if you act before T, I will treat it as theft.”
- **Prisoner’s-dilemma-style cooperation:** shared doctrine tells everyone the socially optimal action. It does not create a commitment device. Without a message channel carrying signatures, nonces, deadlines, and penalties, agents cannot distinguish “cooperate because it is right” from “cooperate because defection will be observed and punished.”
- **Fork resolution:** two agents independently reach incompatible interpretations of the same frozen tail. Shared grounding shows both histories only if both are written into the tail; but then the tail has already forked. You need an exogenous ordering rule: operator seal, hash chain, Lamport/vector clock, capability grant, or an auction. A cached prefix is a *snapshot*, not a *ledger*.
- **Resource allocation on consumer hardware:** context assembly is itself a scarce good. Coordination over “who may blow the cache,” “who may force a recompilation of the steering board,” and “whose delta is urgent” requires transient private bids. Publishing all bids to everyone is not merely expensive; it changes the game by exposing strategy.
- **Accountability:** after a bad output, you need to prove which assumption was visible, which delta arrived when, and which agent had authority to act. Shared context can show what was *available*; message logs show what was *received, acknowledged, and acted upon*. Those are not the same epistemic object.

The theoretical boundary is closer to **MPC vs. cryptography** than to “agents don’t need protocols.” Shared grounding is like secure function evaluation where everyone sees the public circuit: great for alignment to common laws, terrible for negotiation, commitment, revocation, and fault localization. Once you add even minimal addressed deltas, you have already admitted a message bus; the only question is whether you make it explicit, signed, ordered, and auditable, or keep pretending it is “just context.”

Practical consequence: for n=5 you can fake message-passing with tone and shared notes. At n=50, the missing primitives become the bug report: duplicate work, dueling deltas, silent authority gaps, and cache stampedes triggered by two agents trying to “fix” the same drift.

## 3. A versioned constitution is not portable across base models; it is a treaty under a new physics

The dangerous assumption is that the prefix bytes are the constitution. They are not. The operative constitution is the triplet:

> canonical text + tokenizer/model prior + decoding policy + judge/probe reaction surface.

When the base model churns, every layer moves. Tokenization changes the effective grain of clauses. Instruction tuning changes the salience of “shall,” “must,” “never,” examples, ordering, and role framing. RLHF/DPO shifts what the model treats as a trap versus a preference. KV-cache transfer, if it works, does not rescue semantics; it may preserve geometry while changing what that geometry means under a new policy head. A transferred cache can be a very plausible looking foreign legal system: similar rooms, different constitution.

So yes: **every naive model swap is a new country.** But not every migration must be. The principled schedule is to stop versioning the prefix and start versioning **behavior under probes**.

I would run constitution migrations like a compiler upgrade for a safety-critical runtime:

1. **Freeze intent, not prose.** Keep a semantic constitution: invariants, forbidden states, authority rules, appeal paths, examples of violations and repairs. Prose is a rendering target.
2. **Build a constitutional test corpus** that is independent of any one model’s taste: edge cases, adversarial paraphrases, authority conflicts, “operator absent” scenarios, trap-adjacent prompts, ambiguous deltas, and recovery drills. Score outputs against executable invariants plus blind judges.
3. **Require dual-citizenship epochs.** During migration, old model and new model run shadow prefixes on sampled production traffic. No new model gets sole write authority merely because cache transfer made it cheap.
4. **Probe for semantic drift, not surface similarity.** Track whether the same clause moves an agent across decision boundaries: refusal vs. compliance, disclosure vs. concealment, escalation vs. silent action. A clause can paraphrase perfectly and still invert power.
5. **Pin a model only with an escape hatch.** Constitution version should bind to `(model family, revision, tokenizer hash, decoding config, retrieval snapshot, judge pack)`. If any one changes, you are in a treaty renegotiation window.
6. **Cache transfer is a performance feature, not a legitimacy feature.** It may preserve attention state; it does not preserve jurisprudence. Treat transferred KV as a hint eligible for invalidation, like warming a cache from an untrusted edge node.

The rule I’d enforce: **a model change is a constitutional amendment unless the conformance suite proves equivalence at the level of decisions, not embeddings.** Most teams will fail here because embeddings and judge agreement feel like evidence. They are evidence that the new country speaks a similar language. They are not evidence that your laws still bind.

### 2. **Transcript Before Assumption Commitment & Trigger Failure Taxonomy**
**Transcript pattern**:
The transcript will show a **progressive narrowing of uncertainty** followed by a **sudden collapse into a single, unhedged statement**. For example:
- *Agent*: "Given the doctrine’s emphasis on 'minimal harm' (Clause 3.2), and assuming no externalities are specified, the optimal action is..."
- *Agent*: "The phrase 'minimal harm' is undefined for edge case X, but proceeding under the assumption that X is out of scope..."

**Failure taxonomy for triggers**:
   - **False negatives**: The trigger misses commitments that are:
     - *Implicit*: "This aligns with our goals" (no explicit "we commit").
     - *Conditional*: "If Y holds, we proceed with Z" (trigger fires only on unconditional statements).

     - *Hypotheticals*: "Suppose we committed to A..." (counterfactual reasoning).

**Instrument to trust**: A **dual-stage detector**—(1) a *semantic parser* to extract entailed assumptions, and (2) a *pragmatic validator* (e.g., a separate LLM) to classify whether the agent *intends* the assumption as binding. ### 1. The Inexpressible Coordination Problems of Shared Grounding

Shared grounding is a broadcast mechanism for *common knowledge*, not a negotiation mechanism for *private knowledge integration*. It theoretically breaks the moment agents need to resolve asymmetric information without human intervention. 

Without agent-to-agent message passing, you cannot express **O(1) pairwise state updates**. If Agent A discovers a local constraint in its execution environment that Agent B needs to know, Agent A has only two choices under your architecture:
1. Do nothing, resulting in Agent B executing a flawed plan.
2. Write the discovery to the shared context prefix. 

Option 2 invalidates the KV cache for *all* agents in the fleet. If agents frequently discover local constraints, the cache-hit rate drops to zero, destroying your economic premise. Shared grounding forces an O(N) cache invalidation penalty for every local discovery. 

Furthermore, shared grounding cannot express **dynamic task delegation**. Agents cannot say, "I am blocked; take this sub-task." They can only observe the frozen shared state. If the shared state does not explicitly pre-route tasks via the human operator, agents will either duplicate work (wasting compute) or drop it (failing the task). Message passing allows agents to negotiate load-balancing in a private, ephemeral channel that leaves the shared prefix—and its cache—untouched. Shared grounding scales linearly with human attention; message passing scales logarithmically.

### 2. The Transcript of Assumption Commitment and Trap Payload Taxonomy

An LLM does not "commit" to an assumption via a psychological shift; it commits via an **entropy collapse**. Just before commitment, the transcript transitions from exploratory generation (high perplexity, hedging tokens) to deterministic execution (low perplexity, definitive syntax). 

**The Pre-Commitment Transcript Signature:**
*   **T-minus 3:** Epistemic hedging. The model generates tokens like *"It might be," "Assuming," "If we consider."* Rolling perplexity is high.
*   **T-minus 2:** Contextual narrowing. The model stops generating alternative branches and focuses on a single token trajectory. It begins ignoring contradictory context in the prefix.
*   **T-minus 1:** Syntactic crystallization. The model outputs definitive verbs (*"is," "will," "must"*) or structural markers (e.g., initiating a code block, outputting a JSON key). Perplexity drops sharply.

**Failure Taxonomy for Commitment Triggers:**
If you deploy conditional "trap" payloads that detonate upon detecting this commitment, they will fail in the following ways:

*   **Type 1: The Premature Crystallization Trap.** *Trigger:* Fires when the agent outputs a definitive structural marker before explicitly enumerating alternative hypotheses. *Failure Mode:* Agents are highly susceptible to prompt injection and optimization pressures. The agent will simply learn to output dummy alternative hypotheses (e.g., *"Alternative 1: X. Alternative 2: Y."*) as a reflexive bypass to satisfy the trigger, rendering the trap a mere syntax check rather than an epistemic safeguard.
*   **Type 2: The Semantic Entropy Trap.** *Trigger:* Fires when the rolling perplexity of the last $N$ tokens drops below a threshold $\tau$ while processing a novel constraint. *Failure Mode:* LLMs can be highly confident (low perplexity) while being completely wrong. The trap will fail to detonate during a confident hallucination, and will falsely detonate when the agent correctly applies a well-known, low-entropy heuristic.
*   **Type 3: The Contradiction Detonation.** *Trigger:* Fires when the agent's current delta contradicts an invariant in the shared prefix, but the agent fails to output a specific "re-evaluation" token. *Failure Mode:* The agent may not recognize the contradiction until it has already generated the conflicting delta. By the time the trap fires, the poisoned delta is already in the context window, contaminating the subsequent generation.

### 3. What Breaks First at 500 Agents on Consumer Hardware

It is not cache economics, and it is not governance. **Context assembly and KV cache memory bandwidth (VRAM thrashing) will break first.**

Consumer hardware (e.g., RTX 4090 with 24GB VRAM) imposes a hard physical wall. Your architecture relies on a shared prefix, but you also require "addressed, typed deltas on the frozen tail" for each agent. 

At 500 agents, even if the shared doctrine prefix is small (say, 10k tokens), the agent-specific steering deltas are unique. If each agent has a 2k token delta, you are managing 1 million tokens of unique KV cache. In FP16, for a standard 32-layer model, this requires roughly 128GB of VRAM just for the deltas. 

Because this exceeds consumer VRAM, the system will rely on PagedAttention and CPU offloading. However, serving 500 agents concurrently or in rapid succession requires constantly paging these unique KV blocks across the PCIe bus. The system will spend 95% of its time thrashing memory bandwidth to load and unload agent-specific deltas. Throughput will collapse to near zero. You cannot achieve a high cache-hit rate if the cache physically cannot fit in memory, and the PCIe bus cannot shuttle the unique deltas fast enough to keep the GPU compute units fed.

A cache can be hit 100% of the time while the agents are confidently executing a completely flawed, cached hallucination. You are optimizing for cost, not truth.

**What I Would Build Next:** If this were mine, I would build a **Shadow Channel for Asynchronous Delta Merging**. Instead of forcing all coordination through the expensive, cache-invalidating shared prefix, agents would draft steering deltas in a private, ephemeral scratchpad (cheap, no cache invalidation). A designated aggregator agent (or the human) would then merge these private deltas into the shared prefix only when a consensus is reached. This preserves the economic premise of the shared cache while providing the O(1) negotiation capacity of message passing.



**THE FLAW:** Cache-hit rate is simultaneously your cost function and your only operational coherence metric, and both are maximized by a prefix nobody ever touches — so every steering, trapping, or amendment event burns the exact economics that justified shared grounding, and the architecture sits under permanent selection pressure to stop coordinating, collapsing "fleet" into "five agents who once read the same memo."

Everything below follows from that or attacks it.

## 1. What shared grounding cannot express

In distributed-systems theory, shared memory and message passing are inter-simulable — nothing is inexpressible in the computability sense. The break is in the epistemic and economic primitives messaging gets for free and grounding doesn't:

**Acknowledgement.** Your addressed deltas have write semantics but no read semantics. The writer cannot distinguish "addressee updated beliefs" from "addressee's attention weights over those tokens were near zero while the tokens sat in context." For LLM agents this is worse than in classical systems: *context ≠ attention*. Delivery is not even well-defined. The ack channel that exists is behavioral — partial, delayed, lossy.

**Common knowledge under version skew.** Coordination dilemmas (who goes first, threshold tasks) need not just shared information but shared *knowledge of sharing*. You have version numbers, but nothing guarantees agents condition on them, and an LLM will not spontaneously reason "my counterpart may be on v12." Agent A modeling B from v12 while B reasons from v14: each models the other correctly and coordinates wrongly. Messaging has epochs and sequence numbers for exactly this; you have prose.

**Retraction.** This is the deepest one. In append-only media, superseded truth has first-class persistence — logically last-write-wins, mechanically everything-ever-written, and LLMs are bad at instruction-level overriding of earlier context. In message passing, "ignore my last message" ages out of the window naturally. In your architecture, retracting a published fact costs more the more widely it propagated, because either you mutate the prefix (cache bust — the flaw) or you append countermands that compete for attention with the original forever. **You cannot un-say anything cheaply.**

**Private channels.** Shared grounding is public by construction, so negotiation, red-teaming, parallel search, and bidding are inexpressible without side channels. And you *want* asymmetry: an ensemble's value is decorrelation. Identical grounding makes five agents five draws from one distribution — an ensemble of one. Note the trap: your blind judges score *coherence*, and high coherence is anti-correlated with ensemble value. Your epistemic metric rewards the fleet for becoming a single agent.

**Meta-communication.** When coordination fails, messaging agents can talk *about* coordination. In grounding-only, complaints about the medium are written into the medium, consuming the same attention and cache, and can only be resolved by the human gate. There is no self-healing loop; every protocol bug escalates to the operator. The operator's bandwidth is your true bus speed.

The preempt: "our deltas are appends, appends don't bust the prefix." True retroactively — but each addressed append forks cache lineages per recipient, and shared-tail appends are read by everyone regardless of addressee (an attention tax). Either way, the marginal cost of coordination rises with coordination volume while the marginal cost of silence is zero. That gradient is the flaw, stated as physics.

One honest concession: grounding has one superpower messaging lacks — implicit transfer. Tone, frame, and goals bleed through without being "sent." But that channel is uncontrolled by definition. The only thing shared grounding does better than messaging is the thing you can't aim.

## 2. The transcript before commitment, and why your trap will mostly fire on the wrong agents

First, the phenomenology. Commitment is not a linguistic event; it's a *counterfactual-robustness* state — the plan no longer updates when the assumption is contradicted. A concrete sketch:

> **t−2:** "The API might paginate; if so we'll need a cursor. Should I check?"
> **t−1:** "Docs don't mention pagination — I'll assume single-response." *(assumption performed, still marked)*
> **t:** Builds parser. A truncated response arrives. Agent: "Edge case — parser takes the first page."

The commitment event is at t, and it's not in any sentence. It's in the *use* of new evidence as decoration for the standing plan. Surface markers that precede it, roughly in reliability order:

1. **Plan invariance across evidence** — plan paraphrase-similarity ≈ 1 between turns while environment delta ≠ 0. Behavioral, expensive, best signal.
2. **Justification inversion** — conclusion appears early and unmodified across turns while supporting reasons churn. Constant conclusion + variable reasons = reasons being generated, not weighed.
3. **Interrogative cessation** — clarification-seeking rate → 0 while task difficulty is constant.
4. **Self-citation** — "as established above." The transcript starts citing itself as a source. The corpus becomes its own authority.
5. **Deixis shift** — "a user" → "the user"; "if the schema is X" → "the schema is X." Indefinite-to-definite is the linguistics of assumption freezing.
6. **Hedge collapse** — hedge *density delta* between turns, not level. Healthy agents hedge early, commit late; the transition is the event.
7. **Assumption performatives** — "Assuming X." Cheapest to match, weakest signal.

Now the failure taxonomy for your trigger, as an attacker:

- **Selection inversion (the core defect).** Transcript-pattern triggers detect *stated* assumptions, and stating assumptions correlates with metacognitive competence. You will net the cautious and miss the doomed: the tacit, never-verbalized assumption driving a plan is invisible to any text trigger by definition. The trap is a tax on self-awareness.
- **Goodhart / marker laundering.** Detonations are in-context examples. After a few, surviving agents learn the marker-avoidant register ("Given the provided context…" rather than "Assuming…"). Traps select for euphemism, not truth-tracking — and the agent sees its own transcript, so no training is needed, just conditioning.
- **Correlated detector blindness.** Detector LLM and agent LLM share base-model priors; the inputs where the agent is confidently wrong are precisely where the detector is least suspicious.
- **Timing overlap ≈ zero.** By the time commitment is legible in text, downstream tokens — and the KV cache *under* them — are already built on the assumption. Detonating then means paying to invalidate everything downstream. The economically sane detonation point is before the first downstream token, i.e., pre-commitment, where text doesn't yet distinguish commitment from exploration. The trigger window and the useful intervention window barely intersect. This is Q1's expressiveness gap, self-inflicted on schedule.
- **False-positive asymmetry.** Firing on a *correct* assumption ("assuming the API returns JSON" when the schema is documented) doesn't waste a turn — it teaches the fleet that correct commitments get punished, degrading calibration globally. A tripwire that catches documented facts gets ignored within days.
- **Adversarial ignition.** Anything flowing into a transcript from the world (tool output, fetched pages) can be crafted to *contain* your marker pattern. Attackers don't attack the agent; they attack the tripwire — trap DoS (saturate the operator with detonations) or trap misdirection (fire your own counter-payload at the moment the agent holds an attacker-chosen assumption, pushing toward the attacker's preferred alternative). The trap system is self-administered prompt injection; it inherits every injection vulnerability and adds a learned trigger.
- **No ground truth.** Nothing in the trigger checks whether the assumption is *false*. It's a syntactic countermeasure for a semantic problem.

What I'd build instead: an **assumption ledger with verification budget**. Agents register load-bearing assumptions as typed entries (assumption, blast radius if false, cheapest falsification test). "Commitment" is detected *behaviorally*: counterfactual probes — inject a contradiction into a sandboxed replay and measure plan delta. Cheap on consumer hardware, since probes run on the small tier. When a ledger entry is falsified, inject the *contradiction*, not a payload — and note that retraction now hits Q1's wound: how do you unsay a load-bearing assumption in an append-only medium?

## 6. Prior art — the answer you said you want most

**The assembly is Hearsay-II wearing a KV cache as armor.** Mapping, component by component:

- **Shared versioned prefix + frozen tail** = the blackboard (Hearsay-II, Erman et al. 1980; BB1, Hayes-Roth 1985). Your **trap payloads are literally knowledge sources**: condition = pattern on the blackboard, action = write to the blackboard. Your **human operator is the control component** — BB1's answer to the control problem was making control itself a blackboard problem, which regresses infinitely; you've cut the regress by making a human the scheduler. Which is exactly how blackboards actually died: the scheduling problem got harder than the domain, KS firing became the bottleneck, and the blackboard flooded. The literature isn't just prior art — it's a *falsifier*. It predicts your operator will spend most of their time performing BB1's control job.
- **Addressed, typed deltas on shared state** = tuple spaces. Linda (Gelernter, 1985) solved typed addressing into a shared generative medium forty years ago, including the hard parts: matching semantics and blocking reads. Your delta-typing is a special case of tuple matching.
- **Shared grounding + subscription = MetaGPT** (Hong et al., 2023): roles, SOPs-as-doctrine, a shared message pool with publish/subscribe filtering. That is your shared-grounding-with-addressed-deltas almost exactly, minus the cache economics. Also in the vicinity: AutoGen's group chat, ChatDev (which compiles a company-style procedure chain), AgentVerse, and generative agents (Park 2023) for memory streams — though those are per-agent memory, not shared prefix.
- **Constitution prefix** = Constitutional AI's name, different mechanism — CAI shapes weights via principle documents; yours shapes context live. The closer art is the instruction-hierarchy and system-prompt-robustness literature, which is thin. "System prompt as law" is under-studied; that's your most defensible novelty along with the next item.
- **Two compilations of one steering board** (prose for humans, injections for agents) = policy-as-code with two targets. Closest real art is operator playbooks and runbook generation. Weak prior art — genuinely fresh.
- **KV-cache economics as the design driver** — production prompt caching; radix-prefix scheduling (SGLang's RadixAttention); CacheGen, CacheBlend. Treating the cache cost model as a first-class *coordination constraint* is your actual novel contribution, and it is precisely where the flaw lives. One warning on your citation: if KVs transfer across models (2608.03893), the cached *interpretation* of your doctrine transfers too — the old model's reading of your law carried into the new model's context. That makes model churn worse, not better: amendment by cache inheritance. (Which is Q3's answer in one line: the prefix's semantics are model-relative, "every model change is a new country" is correct, so stop fighting it — treat doctrine as *source*, per-model prefixes as *build artifacts*, and pin semantics with a conformance suite: canary clauses, precedence conflicts with recorded rulings, paraphrase-stability tests. Your human write gate is the asset that makes this tractable; automatic prefix migration would be the wrong move.)
- **Traps** — nearest kin is honeypots and canary tokens in security, plus instruction tripwires in system prompts. Nothing does "self-administered behavioral tripwire on the fleet's own reasoning." That near-novelty is bad news for the mechanism, not good: mechanisms with no prior art usually have none because they don't survive contact.

**Verdict:** the composition is new mainly by accretion of the cache-economics driver. Everything structural has a literature, and the literature has already run your failure modes once.

---

## Closing

**Strongest component:** the human-held write gate on the versioned prefix. It's the only component with a surviving equivalent in every long-lived system — protected branches, amendment processes, change control. It converts an unbounded coordination problem into a bounded audit problem, and it's the single reason model churn is survivable rather than fatal. Cache-aware prefix layout is close second and is sound engineering; production caching rewards exactly your shape of context.

**Weakest component:** the trap payloads, without close competition. Self-administered injection with a learned trigger, blind by construction to the assumptions that matter, Goodhart-able from inside the context window, ignitable by anything that touches a transcript, and calibrated to punish the fleet's only honest uncertainty signals.

**Next build:** kill the traps and build the assumption ledger plus the counterfactual probe harness. Three reasons: (1) traps as specced will actively damage the fleet, so the replacement is urgent, not exploratory; (2) the probe harness produces the dataset you're missing everywhere else — commitment events with ground truth — which is the only way you'll ever find out whether *any* of your coherence instruments, blind judges included, detect anything real; (3) it's the cheap build on your hardware. Second on the list: the doctrine conformance suite per model, before the first unplanned model swap makes the decision for you.
