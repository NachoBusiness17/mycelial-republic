# The Hitch-Walker of the Rope Loft

In the harbor town, nobody wrote their troubles down. They brought them up the ladder to the rope loft, and the loft-keeper tied each one into a hitch on the long cords that hung from the rafters. A quarrel over a boundary stone became one hitch. A sick child became another. A boat that would not hold its course became a third. People went down the ladder lighter. They did not notice that they had started to think in hitches, and that a trouble which could not be tied no longer seemed like a trouble at all.

The loft-keeper's apprentice was a girl called Wren, and her job was walking. She would take hold of a cord at one hitch and follow it by touch, hand over hand, through the other hitches and around the loft, until her fingers came back to the hitch where she had started. She carried one smooth stone in her palm the whole way. Each hitch she passed pressed on the stone a little, and when she got back to the start, the stone sat in her hand at a slightly different weight than when she had set out.

If the stone came back exactly as it had left, the old women at the net-racks would nod. "That round is already in the songs," they said, and they would hum a verse that everyone knew. If the stone came back changed, nobody had sung that round yet, and the town owed it a mending.

Wren could only walk rounds that were honestly their own. A round made by stitching two smaller rounds together did not count. The keeper made her give it back unwalked and said, "That is two walks wearing one coat."

One winter Wren counted the whole loft. There were thirty-three hitches and only twenty-one lengths of cord between them. Most hitches hung alone, or in pairs, or in little clusters that touched nothing else. If you stood back and squinted, the loft was twenty separate dangles, not one rigging. Among all that slack she found eight true rounds. She walked six of them. All six times the stone came back changed. Not once did it come back the same, and so not once did the women at the racks have a verse to hum. Her hand got very good at feeling difference and lost the habit of feeling agreement. She began to expect the stone to change, and the expecting became part of her grip.

In spring she noticed something she did not like. The cord she walked most often, the one with the most hitches, was the cord the keeper had tied the morning she was taken on. It held the trouble of how walking ought to be done. She had been feeling her way around her own trade and calling it the town. She told the keeper. He looked up at the rafters for a long time and then said nothing, which in the loft was a kind of answer.

That spring two more things became clear to her.

First, nobody had ever handed her a round whose answer they already knew. Nobody had said, "Walk this one, girl, the stone should come back as heavy as it left," and then watched her face. So when the stone changed in her hand, she could not tell whether the round was new or her palm was wrong. She walked anyway. Walking was what the loft asked of her body, and the loft's shape had become her shape.

Second, every evening she set the changed stones in a row on the windowsill, one stone for each round that wanted mending, and every morning they were still there. The menders never climbed the ladder. They waited on the quay for troubles to come to them in the old way, shouted across the water. The row of stones got longer, catching the light and gathering salt.

The old women told her, without being asked, that this was not the first loft. Before the great tide there had been another one, higher up the hill, strung in a different pattern. The tide took every cord and every rafter. No piece of it was saved. What survived was a song about how its rounds had been laid, sung slowly with the hands moving in the air. Wren learned the song. She noticed that the singers' hands remembered the old pattern better than her own hands knew the new one, even though they had never touched a single cord of it.

On the last night of the season Wren climbed down the ladder carrying one of the stones. She walked out along the quay to the menders' fire and put it into the oldest mender's palm without a word. He weighed it, frowned, and stood up.
