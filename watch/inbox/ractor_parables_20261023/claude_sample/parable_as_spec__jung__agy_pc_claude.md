# The Heron in the Well

In the hill town the law of the well had been lost five times. The first time, the man who knew it died with his mouth shut. The second time it was written on a board, and the board went into a winter fire. The third time it was copied, and the copier left out the one line he did not like. Nobody remembered the fourth or fifth time, only that each one had ended with a drowned child, and that the town had grieved as if this had never happened before.

The well-keeper's widow, Orla, knew the law the way you know a cellar stair in the dark: from the bruise on your shin. When her hands began to shake, she did not send for a scribe. She took a candle down to the root cellar, where the onions grew pale shoots toward a light they would never reach, and she slept on sacking among them. For three nights she waited for a dream that could carry the law.

On the third night it came. A grey heron stood waist-deep in the well and would not drink until the moon lay flat in the bucket. When a stranger came to the lip of the well, the heron stepped aside and gave him the first water before it took any for itself. The rope was frayed, and a boy reached for a knife to cut it clean, but the heron caught his wrist in its beak and held it until he let the knife fall. At the edge of the stones a black dog slept with its back to the water and never woke.

Orla did not write the dream down. She painted it on the long wall of the inn, in ochre and soot, between the hearth and the door. Travelers took it for decoration. The innkeeper's wife thought it was gloomy and hung a bunch of dried lavender over the dog. Orla left the lavender where it was and explained nothing.

Then she sent for seven children from seven farms. None of them had ever played together, and their fathers were not on speaking terms. She brought them down to the cellar one at a time, with the door shut behind each one. She told each of them the dream, exactly as she had dreamed it, in the same words. Afterward she asked only one question: *What does the heron want?* She did not say the word *law*. She did not mention the well's history, and she did not point at the painting upstairs.

The cooper's girl said: you draw only at moonrise, the stranger drinks first, and you never cut the rope, however worn it gets. Orla nodded and put another log on the brazier. Nothing had leaked out of the dream between her mouth and the girl's ear.

The miller's boy said the heron was hungry and wanted fish. Orla did not scold him. She held his answer the way you hold a dropped stitch up to the light. She had to know whether the knot was in her dream or in the boy.

That was why the dog was there. The dog meant nothing. Orla had put it in the dream on purpose, the way you leave a coin on the floor to find out who sweeps around it and who pockets it. The tanner's son said gravely that the dog had to be fed before anyone drew water, or it would wake and bite. He had built a whole commandment on an animal that never stirred. He had wanted a master badly enough to make one out of a sleeping dog. Orla knew then that the fault was in his listening and not in her telling, and she sent him home with an apple.

Then three children in a row, who had never met and had not looked at the dog once, stumbled at the same place. They could not say why the heron took the boy's wrist. One thought it was angry, one thought it was playing, and one did not know. Orla sat a long while among the onions. Then she climbed upstairs and washed her face. That night she went back down onto the sacking. Her own shadow had got into the dream at exactly that spot, and she did not know what it was until she dreamed the scene again. In the second dream the heron held the boy's wrist and looked steadily at the rope. After that, all three children who heard it understood.

When all seven had been heard, Orla had a mason carve on the well-stone only the things every one of them had said in the same words, without having heard the others: the moon, the stranger, the rope. Two children had said the heron was lonely. She let that go. It might have been true, but it could not hold up a roof.

The lavender still hangs over the dog on the inn wall. Children sometimes ask about it. Nobody has drowned since.
