# The Hum at the Narrow Bridge

The town of Vell had one bridge, and the bridge had one gate, and at the gate sat a girl named Orra with a reed no longer than her finger. Behind her stood the Long Hall, its hundred doors and its hundred porters, broad-shouldered men who could carry anything and charged by the hour whether they carried or not.

Every cart that came down the hill road stopped at Orra first. She did not open the sacks. She hummed one low note against the boards of each cart and listened to what came back. A cart of grain hummed dull. A cart of bells rang. A cart with a false bottom hummed twice. She cost the town a bowl of soup a day. When she nodded, the cart rolled on, and only then did the porters wake. They split its load among the doors, ten men to the east, four to the cellars, one to the counting room. Before her nod, nobody moved. A drover once tried to drive past her straight to the hall. The porters stood up all at once, unloaded his cart onto the bridge, and argued until dark over whose door each sack belonged to. By the time they stopped, the grain had gone to rot and their wages had eaten the price of it.

Orra began every hum the same way, with three notes her grandmother had taught her, and she changed only the last one. A visitor asked why she didn't make up a fresh tune for each cart. She said the first three notes were already sitting in the boards. Each morning the bridge remembered them, and she only had to lean on what it remembered. On her best days she sang two hundred carts and hardly lifted her chest.

At festival time the Long Hall held a dance. One choir stood at the north wall and one at the south, and they sang the same song toward each other. Where the two songs met, the floor went still in certain places, and on those places the old women set their stools and did not move all night. Everywhere else the floor rocked and the dancers spun across it. Over the years the dancers changed. Children grew up into the spinning and old men left it. The still places never moved. A child could point to a board and say, my great-aunt sat here, and be right.

One year the south choir got into a quarrel and walked out. The north choir kept singing, louder, to make up for it. The still places came loose. The floor rolled under the old women's stools and slid them toward the door. Nobody could stand anywhere. The dancers said it felt like walking on a river. When the south choir came back sheepish and took up the song again, the stools found their boards one by one.

The year after that, a stranger stamped out of time in the middle of the floor. He came from the salt country, where people dance alone. He wasn't trying to do harm. In his country that was how you showed you were happy. The song didn't get quieter around him. It came apart. The north choir lost the south. The still places scattered like dropped beads. One old woman fell. The music didn't fade out. It came apart in knots, all over the room, all at once, and it took until dawn to tie it back.

Afterward the town told the story three ways.

The hall-keepers said the stranger had been rude, and that the song had shown its strength by coming back.

Orra's grandmother, who had sat at the gate before her, said the song was good but the door had been wrong. Somebody should have hummed at him on the bridge and listened to what he was made of before he reached the floor. She didn't blame the stranger. She blamed the gate.

In the salt country, where the stranger went home, they said that in Vell the floor itself tells you where to stand, and a man who moves on his own is called a disaster. They told it as a warning to their children, and they laughed while they told it.

That third story never crossed the bridge back to Vell. No cart carried it, and Orra never hummed it, because it never came down the hill road. It went the other way.

Orra still sits at the gate. She still starts with the three old notes. Sometimes, between carts, she turns around on her stool and hums one low note toward the hall instead. Then she waits a long time to hear what comes back.

---KEY---
1. A small, cheap check at the entrance weighs every load before the big, expensive crew behind it does anything (Orra humming each cart while the porters wait).
2. Work only fans out to the many after the gate has passed it; going around the gate wastes everything (the drover who skipped her and lost his grain to the porters' arguing).
3. Starting every time from the same fixed opening lets the stored part do almost all the work (Orra's three unchanging notes that the bridge "remembers").
4. A standing pattern needs two opposing waves, and with only one the fixed points come loose (the south choir walking out and the stools sliding).
5. The members keep moving and changing while the fixed points of the pattern stay exactly where they are (dancers grow up and leave but the old women's boards never move).
6. When a standing pattern is disturbed it doesn't weaken gradually; it comes apart everywhere at once (the salt-country stranger's stamping).
7. The fix belongs at the gate, not on the floor: a disturbance should be weighed before it enters the pattern (the grandmother's reading that blames the door, not the dancer).
8. The reading the house never hears is the one told by the people who left; the gate only listens to what comes in (the salt-country story that never crossed back, and Orra humming toward the hall).
