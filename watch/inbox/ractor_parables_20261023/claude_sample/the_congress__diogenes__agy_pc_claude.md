# The Lantern at the Grain Gate

I came into the mill town at noon with my lantern lit, as I do. A dog followed me for the bread in my sack, and that dog was honest. That made two of us.

A bell rang in the square, and the people who happened to be standing nearest the hall went up the steps. Nobody had been chosen the day before. Nobody had been told to come. Whoever answered the bell sat down, and that was enough.

There were three benches. On the first sat the keepers of the town's fires: one for the bakery oven, one for the forge, one for the kiln, one for the well-wheel, and so on. Each had soot on their hands from a fire they actually tended. On the second bench sat ten tongues, one from each craft: the weavers, the tanners, the rope-makers, and seven more. Each argued the way their trade had taught them to think. On the third bench sat five people in painted faces. One had coins painted on the cheeks, one had a road, one had a hammer, one had an hourglass, and the last had bells. Behind every person on every bench crouched a child holding a pebble. When anyone said something the child could show was false, the pebble struck the floorboards and the speaker had to stop and prove it or sit down.

I watched the bell-faced one take the most pebbles and laugh the loudest. He was the only one there who didn't pretend to be wise, so I liked him.

At dusk they washed off the paint, wiped off the soot, and went home. In the morning the bell rang again and different hands climbed the steps. I asked the woman with the hammer face, the same one who had argued so well the evening before, what the town had agreed. She looked at me as though I were a stranger, and to her I was one. "Ask the wall," she said.

The wall ran the length of the square, whitewashed, taller than a man. One old woman held the only brush. She painted the day's agreements in black, line under line, and nobody else ever touched the wall. Anyone could stand there and read it: children, beggars, me with my lantern. When a thing on it proved wrong, she didn't paint over it. She wrote underneath: *The line above was mistaken; this is what holds now.* A boy once came at night with a wet rag and scrubbed a line half away. In the morning she didn't repaint it. She wrote below: *Here a boy scrubbed.* The smear stayed, and people looked at it longer than at anything else on the wall.

The steward of the town came to her while I was there. He wore a good coat and wanted a fresh start. "The wall is ugly with corrections," he said. "Whitewash it and we will begin clean."

She kept painting. "Whitewash it," she said, "and nothing was ever agreed in this town. The mill will turn tomorrow on no word at all."

He left. She didn't watch him go.

Then a man came up the river road with a measuring rod and a cold face, sent by the towns downstream. The steward hurried to meet him with wine and a speech. The man walked past the wine. He didn't ask the steward anything, or the five painted faces, or the keepers of the fires. He walked the whole wall with one finger on the paint, reading. Where the wall said a thing was so, he went and looked.

One line said: *The granary keeps more than fifteen measures of floor swept bare at all times.* He went to the granary and paced the empty boards with his rod. Sixteen measures. He chalked the number on the granary door, went back, and read the next line.

The steward followed him all afternoon, saying, "You see we keep good order. You see how well I manage." The man with the rod never answered him once.

At evening I found the steward on the hall steps with his good coat creased. I held my lantern up to his face, though the sun hadn't gone down yet.

"What are you looking for?" he asked.

"I found it. You've built a town where no one has to trust you. The benches forget, the wall remembers, the children throw stones at the wise, and the man from downstream reads paint instead of faces. And you're still standing at the gate asking him to say you did well."

"Shouldn't he?"

"The granary had sixteen measures. Your coat had nothing to do with it. Take off the coat. Go sweep the seventeenth."

He laughed a little, the way a man laughs when he's been caught. He didn't take off the coat. But the next morning, when the bell rang, he climbed the steps with soot on his hands and no one called him steward. The dog went with him. I took that as a good sign.
