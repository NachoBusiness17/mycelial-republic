# The Copper Rings

When a matter came to the guild, the porter tied a tangle in the great cord that hung the length of the hall, and the matter was not finished until the tangle was cut out and swept. That was the whole of the law, and nobody remembered who had tied the first.

The guild had burned once. The fire took the shelves, the deeds, and the street plans, and what the fire missed, the magistrates' wagons carried away to be pulped. One grandmother stayed through both, and afterward she could walk the town blindfolded. She recited the streets each morning to the apprentices — here was the fountain, here is the fountain — and her voice was the only record that had outlived its paper.

The guild hired Marthe to draw the town anew. She counted thirty-three wells and twenty-one streets still running between them, and the streets were so few that the wells fell into twenty neighborhoods, most of them a lone well with no street at all. She engraved the wells as dots and the streets as lines on a copper plate, and when she counted the ways a walker might leave a well and come back to it without ever turning around, the count came to eight.

The guild kept a rite older than any plate. To judge a circle you walked it, a pole on your shoulder, the pole's shadow held on one fixed side of you, no turning at the crossings. When you came back to your starting stone you looked at the pole: what the shadow had gained, the circle had paid you. If the shadow lay exactly where it began, the circle was old ground — the town had long since absorbed it, and it would teach nothing further. And if there was no walking it at all, if the street broke and the far side could be reached only by stepping off the plate, then the break itself was the matter, and the matter was for the guild to close with brick.

The rite stood on the copper too. Marthe had drawn her own table among the wells — dot thirty-three — and one of the eight circles ran through the map-room, around the table on which the plate lay. Whoever judged that circle would carry the paper while walking around the paper.

No one had walked any of them. The circles had been counted, engraved, and gilded at the rim; the apprentices recited them at supper; and the book of readings lay unopened, because no mason, no magistrate, no merchant had ever asked what a walked circle pays.

The town admired the copper. Pilgrims came up from the coast to see it, eight perfect rings on a green plate, and they stepped over the bricked alley and around the broken bridge to reach it, and the wells they drank at on the way were dry.

Marthe took the pole and went out at first light. By dusk she had this: six of the eight circles were broken — a bridge out, an alley bricked shut, a street that stopped dead at the cistern and resumed beyond it. Of the rest, one ran only around the map-room table, and the last passed through a courtyard locked since the fire. Not one circle was whole in the town; not one shadow had been turned; the plate paid nothing it had not drawn for itself.

She laid the pole on the magistrates' table. They wanted the copper hung higher, lit by two more lamps; the pilgrims were expected at harvest. Marthe asked for bricklayers. A drawn circle, she said, is either a promise of a street or a lie about one; the town held eight of them outstanding — six payable in brick, two payable in scraping.

The guild argued through the autumn. The grandmother died in the first frost, reciting to the end, and the apprentices finally wrote her streets down, badly, from memory of her memory — a copy of a copy, though it still named wet stones the copper had made dry.

In the spring the apprentices went out with poles and stakes. Where a circle broke they planted a stake with a red rag, so the gap could be seen from a distance instead of admired up close. Where a circle ran only around the map-room, Marthe scraped its dot from the plate and moved the table to the margin, noted like any other object that happens to sit in a room. A circle would be walked, or it would not be drawn. The cord in the hall took new tangles daily; the cut ends were swept weekly; and what the sweep missed, the apprentices could still recite.
