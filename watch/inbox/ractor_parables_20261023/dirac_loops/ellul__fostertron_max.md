# The String Hall

Every trouble in the coast town was carried to the string hall before anyone dared judge it. The keeper tied what he heard: a peg driven for each thing named, a string stretched for every "is tied to," until the grievance stood on the long board as a small tangle a person could walk through with her fingers. The fishers' quarrel over the wrecked weir became nine pegs and eleven strings. The widow's boundary became four pegs and three. Nothing was settled until it had been tied.

Anke was the walker. She began at one peg and followed strings only, never stepping off them, until the path either died under her or delivered her back at the peg she had left. Only arrival paid. And not every arrival: a ring that could be assembled out of rings already walked earned nothing, however long the night; a ring stood for a stone only when it stood on its own strings. Then she held out her hand, and the hall gave her a stone, and what the walking returned was cut into it.

The shape of the return was fixed by custom older than the boards. A ring with every string in place, beginning meeting end, paid a smooth stone, and smooth meant already held: the thing enclosed owed nothing and taught nothing, the town owned it the way it owns a wall. A ring that fell one string short of closing paid a notched stone, and the notch sat exactly where the missing string belonged. The apprentices lived on notches. A gap with a notch pointing at it was the only gap they closed, because a gap no one can point to is not a gap at all, only weather.

At the equinox the keeper counted the hall, because the hall had always been counted. That year the board carried thirty-three pegs and twenty-one strings. It fell into twenty islands, with no string crossing between them. Eight rings stood on their own strings — eight, no more, no matter how the night was used. Six of the eight were broken, each one notched. None was whole. No one living had seen a smooth stone.

The newest strings were the hall's own. Three winters back, the keeper had driven a peg for the count of pegs and a peg for the count of strings, and strung them to the board and back again. Since then, Anke passed among records of her own walking as she walked; her rounds were pegged while she made them, and the equinox count counted the counting too. She asked him once who had wanted this. No one had wanted it. It had been there to be done, and what is there to be done in a hall of string gets done. She asked what the count of the count would serve. He showed her the shelf.

The shelf ran the north wall, and on it lay every stone the hall had ever issued, in dated rows, back past the memory of anyone. No one came to the shelf. The fishers had long since mended the weir themselves, with green wood, and it held; the widow had hired her own surveyor. The stones went to the shelf because stones went to the shelf. And no one had ever walked behind Anke to see whether her stones said true — the count was trusted because it was a count, and the method kept its own books about its own books.

The first hall had burned in the clearing year. The provost ordered every paper of the method into the fire, and the paper obeyed. But the old mother had been a girl in that hall, and each winter she recited the whole walking-song to the apprentices, word for word, and the second hall was raised on her mouth alone. What the fire cleared, the recitation carried.

One evening, with the equinox stones in her apron, Anke asked the keeper — not unkindly, which was worse — what all of it made inevitable, and who had chosen that. He had rows; he had no answer. So she did one small thing and one small refusal. She carried the six notched stones down the hill to the harbor and the boundary, and let the stringers work where a torn ring hurt somebody. And she declined to shelve the stones of the count-of-the-count; when they were cut, she left them on the board, where anyone could watch them going nowhere. The keeper kept counting. She did not fight him.

What she did not doubt was the walk. A ring walked honestly returns what it returns, and a notch is a true report of an absence. In the spring after her descent to the harbor, one strung gap closed the widow's ring, and the hall cut the first smooth stone in living memory. Anke carried it past the shelf, past the hall, to the widow's door — already held, owed nothing — and the widow set it in her garden wall. The old mother died that spring with the apprentices reciting the song back to her, word for word, and the hall burned nothing.
