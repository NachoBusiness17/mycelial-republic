# The String Room

Odile came up the river to the dyers' hall with clean hands and a small slate. She had audited for thirty years and took the work the way other people took cures: alone, early, on an empty stomach. The string room sat at the back, behind the vats, and down its center ran a long board of black oak on which the hall kept its knots.

Every petitioner who sat one day with the hall about a question tied one knot to the board — one knot for one sitting, no more — and when the question was answered, or the petitioner gave up and went home, the knot stayed. A man who had come three times about his brother's will had left three knots in a row, a small account of his patience. Nothing on the board was ever untied. Where two knots asked after the same matter, the warden of strings ran a thread of dyed wool between them.

When threads led out of a knot and, followed hand over hand, came back to it, the route was called a circuit, and the hall walked it formally, once. The walker carried the hazel staff with the white mark at its head and turned it a quarter-turn in her palms at every knot she passed, as the rule-post required. Back at her start, she read where the mark pointed — the door, the ceiling, her own shoulder — and that direction was the circuit's answer, cut into the tablet by the door. "A circuit that walks whole is a settled thing," the warden said, tapping the stone. "Its reading is here already, and always was. It is the broken ones that are owed." He unrolled the walkers' map, where six circuits were drawn in ink, each with a red bead at an empty span, where the route wanted a thread that had never been strung. Those spans were the hall's debt: wool spun, span strung, walk finished, so the circuit could be asked what it carried.

Odile took the numbers herself. Thirty-three knots. Twenty-one threads. The threads had not made the board one country but twenty small thickets, knots strung to near neighbors with provinces of bare oak between. She used the reckoning her mother had taught her at the loom: take the threads, take away the knots, give back the thickets; the remainder is the number of circuits hiding in the frame. Twenty-one, less thirty-three, plus twenty. She turned the slate so the warden could see the eight. His map named six. Two circuits somewhere on that board had never been drawn by anyone; the warden could not point to them and did not argue, because the arithmetic was the arithmetic. And of the six the map did name, every one carried its red bead; not one circuit on the whole board walked unbroken.

One knot near the rule-post was the warden's own, tied the day he took his office, and its question was the board itself: whether the keeping was well done. Its circuit ran from that knot to the rule-post and back and through no other question. In the tablet's margin, beside that circuit, a later hand had cut: the mending of this one is known to me, and the showing of it would not fit this edge. "Then the tablet says only that it is claimed," Odile said. "Not that it is done." The warden dusted the edge with his sleeve and said nothing.

She asked to see the second walks, the re-walkings by which the first readings had been checked. There were none. In the hall's whole history no circuit had been walked twice; no one had ever followed behind a walker to see whether the staff turned truly. She asked who came for the answers — which merchants, which petitioners had ever sent for a reading. No one ever had. The readings lay cut in the stone, were dusted on feast days, and that was everything that had ever been done with them.

At supper the walkers recited. There had been an older hall before the fire, where the tannery stood, and an older board, and all of it had burned — knots, threads, the tablet of that day. But the walkers of that time had made a habit of saying one circuit aloud each evening, knot by knot, turn by turn, and what is held in mouths does not burn. They said it still, every evening over the stew, though not one thread of it existed anywhere but in the saying. Odile listened to the whole of it, twice.

Before she left she took the staff and walked the nearest of the six until her hands found the red bead and the empty air beyond it. She stood at the break, the staff at its quarter-turn, and the warden studied his boots. "When I come again," she said, "have one of the six walked whole, and let your apprentice do the same with another. We will lay the two readings side by side with no names on either, and let strangers say which circuit gave which. Bring me the staff, not the story." Then, because she had sat one day in the hall about one matter, she tied a single knot of her own and strung it to the warden's knot, her question and his being neighbors now, and went down the river with her slate.
