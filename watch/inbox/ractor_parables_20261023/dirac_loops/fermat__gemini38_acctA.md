# The Perimeter of Beaumont

Gilles, appointed assessor by the magistrate of Beaumont, rode into the lower parish carrying a brass astrolabe, a surveyor's chain, and a snarled bundle of cords that the syndic had brought to the court. The cordage was thick with cross-weaves, twisted hitches, and overlapping loops, each tangle representing a dispute between neighbors who could no longer settle where one pasture ended and the next began. To the lawyers in town, these snarls were merely grievances; to Gilles, who had spent thirty years assessing royal estates along the Garonne, every tangle was a geometric question that could be resolved only by pacing its bounds across the earth.

The law of the survey was ancient and unyielding. A man could not judge the estate from the courthouse bench; he was obliged to walk each circuit from marker to marker until the path returned upon itself. When a surveyor completed such a round, his sighting needle never pointed to the exact mark from which he had set out; the subtle drift between the first bearing and the last was the restitution of the walk. That difference was neither an error nor a fault in the chain, but the true yield of the circuit, measuring the hidden rise and fall of the terrain.

Where the chain returned cleanly to the iron pin at its origin, closing the perimeter without an inch of daylight or missing turf, the ground was declared invariant. Such closed perimeters belonged to the established bedrock of the parish; they were settled facts, requiring neither fresh decree nor the magistrate’s seal. But where the line ran from stone to stone and stopped short before reaching the starting post, leaving an open expanse of weeds, the circuit remained open. An open perimeter was an unfulfilled obligation, a vacancy in the commonwealth that revealed precisely where the gang of village laborers must be summoned to clear ditches and pile fieldstones.

For three days Gilles walked the damp pastures with his iron links clinking against the rock. When he had tallied his pins and plotted his linen chart, the geometry of the parish appeared in cold arithmetic. There were thirty-three boundary stones standing upright in the loam, connected by only twenty-one measured spans of chain. These stones and lines were scattered across twenty separated parcels, isolated from one another by marshland and thorny waste. From the chapel belfry, looking down upon the posts with a brass circle, he distinguished eight independent rings wound among the stones. He walked all eight in the afternoon heat. Six of the rings hung broken, trailing into empty brambles where no line had been carried to the starting pin; of closed, completed circuits, he found not one.

In a stone shelter by the parish well, Gilles discovered the pine chest left by the surveyor who had labored there the previous decade. Inside rested an oak board pierced with brass pins and strung with fine scarlet thread. Gilles traced the threads with his finger, seeking where the measurements touched the life of the valley. Every scarlet thread began at one brass pin only to end at another pin on the same board, and each notation inscribed in the margin referred solely to another coordinate on the same surface. He opened the accompanying notebook: no provincial auditor had ever set his signet upon the margins to verify the measurements, and the masons building the mill downstream had never once requested the figures to guide their foundations. The apparatus observed only its own pins, declaring its own completeness while touching nothing beyond its frame.

That evening, Gilles sat by the hearth of the sheepcote with an old carter whose eyes were dimmed by cataracts. Gilles remarked that the ancient boundaries must have been thrown into confusion twenty winters ago, when the chancellery caught fire and every parchment deed was burned to gray flakes. The old man smiled into his cider. Without hesitation, he began to chant a rhythmic verse that named every granite marker, the angle of the elder hedge, and the curve of the dry ditch from the sheepfold to the ridge. His father had taught him the lines at the sheep-shearing, and his grandfather before that, during the great frost. The written sheets had vanished in the fire, but the true bounds had survived intact in the memory of the elders, handed down from mouth to mouth across the years.

Gilles waited until dawn to close his chest. He rolled his linen chart, leaving the six open circuits marked in bright vermilion so the syndic might see where the village laborers had yet to set their hands, neighboring the truth rather than boasting that they possessed it.
