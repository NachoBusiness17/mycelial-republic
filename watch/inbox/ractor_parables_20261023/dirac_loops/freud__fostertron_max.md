# The Six Missing Spans

I reached the fen town in a spring of rains. My trade is quiet work: I sit with people and listen to what their hands do twice, so I stayed.

Petitioners came to the toll-house each morning carrying snarls of cord, some small as a fist, some heavy as a sleeping cat, and not one could be pulled straight. The elders took each snarl, felt it all over, and never tugged an end. That was the first thing I learned without being told. A trouble is a ball, their hands said; you enter it, you do not unwind it. Follow where it leads, and if the following returns you to the place you entered, the ball has made a round, and a round can be read.

The town kept its knowing out in the water. Each settled thing — this ford drowns oxen in March, that roof takes the south wind — stood as a stone, and where one knowing leaned on another, a plank joined the two. Walkers crossed the planks with straight wands on their shoulders. To walk a round was to leave a stone and arrive, plank after plank, at the stone you left; at arrival the wand was read, how it lay at departure against how it lay at return, and the wand's word was the round's worth, notched and said aloud.

A whole round, so walked, was finished. The eldest walker answered it — held — cut the word into the first stone, and no one walked it again except to show a child what finished means. Whole rounds rest.

What did not rest were the six. Six rounds wanted their last span: the planks ran, the stones stood ready, and between one stone and its return the water lay open. A walker came to that shore, stood, and went home with the wand unread. The custom was older than the planks: when a round wants its span, the bell calls all hands, and the span is laid by everyone together, never by one pair. The bell had not been rung in years. Instead, every spring, the walkers walked the six broken rounds, stood at the same six shores, said the same half of a word — the syllable cut off at the same shore, walker after walker, year after year — and went home. I wrote the syllable down. My trade is with such syllables.

The counter kept the tally in a stilt-house at the fen's rim. Her book said thirty-three stones, twenty-one planks, and twenty of the stones standing alone in the water, joined to nothing — twenty small islands of knowing that touched none of the rest. I rowed to one and read a whole certainty, perfectly said, useless for standing alone. Her book said more: eight rounds in the fen that no joining of shorter walks could counterfeit, six wanting their span, none whole. Nothing in the town had yet come all the way home.

I asked who walks behind the first walker to read the wand a second time. She looked at me as though I had asked who sings behind the singer; no second feet had ever checked the tally. I asked who had ever come from outside to be answered by a round — the ford-keeper guesses his floods by his knees, and nobody has brought him a word. The book answers no one, she agreed, and I let it lie.

Her house stood on the last stone, and the last stone said only: here stands the counting house. When she counts the stones she counts her own floor. She built the book from a song her grandmother sang — the one thing of the older house that crossed the burning year, when the town's papers were raked into the fen and lit, everything written lost, everything sung kept.

I did not scold; I never do. I asked my one question, the one I ask every town: what keeps returning, and what does it ask for? She was quiet a long while. Then she took the bell rope, and I took the other end, and we rang it — in that town two honest hands are all hands — and the two of us, the smallest crowd the custom allows, laid one green plank across the nearest missing span.

The eldest walker went out at dawn, walked the round whole, and the wand came home as it left. Held, she said, and cut it, and the feet that had returned to that shore every spring simply went there no more; they went to the next water. By nightfall the grandmothers sang the new line, by morning the children sang it wrong and were corrected, and the correction crossed the water the way everything kept in that town crosses it — by being said again.
