# The Round Walks

Salt Harrow stood in its shallows: thirty-three stones, twenty-one planks of causeway, no plank longer than a shout. The stones fell into twenty companies. Within a company you could pass from stone to stone with dry boots; between companies there was only water, and the ferrymen had their living.

There were eight ways to leave a stone, keep to the planks, and come home without retracing a step. The woman who laid the first planks had left one teaching about those eight rounds — or the ferrymen said she had. Her book had gone to the pulper in the year the town weighed its papers and sold them, so what remained was the ferry-stove telling, and the kitchen telling, which differed, and the telling given to children, which was plainer than either. But all the versions carried the same center: a full circle pays a fare. A walker who completes a round arrives home speaking a word she never carried out. It is not written on the planks and not dropped by gulls. The walking mints it.

Oren kept the tally. When a fare matched a word already cut into the home stone, he called the round settled — the circle had brought back only what the town already held. When a fare named a thing with no place to stand — a crossing never laid, a stone never set — he called the round owing. An owing round was not the walker's fault and not the walker's work. What the fare pointed at belonged to the town.

At midwinter he read the count aloud on the ferry steps: thirty-three stones, twenty-one planks, twenty companies, eight rounds; six of the rounds owing, and not one settled. Then he walked back in, because the thirty-third stone was his own doorsill. The plan chalked on his wall put the tally room at the middle of the water, and the room the plan hung in was one of the things the plan counted.

No round had ever been walked twice. The first fare went into the book, and the book moved on; whether a second pair of feet would be paid the same word, no page could say, for no one had gone back to see. And once written, the fares went up to the loft over the ferry shed and stayed there. No farmer asked after them, no ferryman, no schoolmaster. The loft was opened after rains, to see if the roof held, and for no other errand, and it always held.

In spring a seed-trader came through, waiting on the ice to clear. She walked the eastern round with Oren one grey morning, stone to plank to stone, and at the last plank the fare arrived, and the word was elm-crossing. Nothing on the water answered to it. No plank was elm; no gap in the causeways wanted one, so far as anyone had measured; the word stood on nothing and pointed anyway.

At the home stone the word was heard three times. Oren wrote it down and said it was a fare misdelivered — the plank-layer had meant some other word, and the circle had carried it badly. Bree, who rowed mail between the companies, heard a commission: a crossing that wanted laying. She asked Oren which two stones it would join, and took out her pencil, and Oren had no answer ready. The trader said the circle paid nothing at all. The word was the walker's own breath, she said, gone out of her at the first step and come home wearing a coat; the town had laid its planks to hear itself, and named the echo a fare.

Oren wrote all three hearings beneath the word, because he wrote everything, and carried the book up to the loft, and the loft kept it as it kept the rest.

The trader left on the first ferry after the ice broke. She had walked one round and heard one word heard three ways, and she carried the story inland, where it was heard a fourth.

That night at the stove the ferrymen told the plank-layer again, each telling a little different from the last, and every telling found hearers, and above the shed the rain came down on the loft roof, and the fares lay dry beneath it.
