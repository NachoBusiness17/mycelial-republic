# The Cellar of Circles

Every trouble that came to the house was tied once, knotted, and hung from its own peg in the cellar beams, and when one trouble was found to lean on another, the keeper stretched a cord between their pegs. Over the years the beams gathered thirty-three pegs and twenty-one cords, and the web hung mostly apart from itself: single pegs, joined pairs, twenty clusters that never touched, and only in a few dark places, where the shape of the house's griefs curved back toward their beginnings, did cord cross cord.

The grandmother had taught that a hanging trouble is not understood by looking. On her evenings the apprentice was sent down with the lamp and the rule of the hand: lay fingers on a cord and go where it goes, peg to peg, until the run either breaks or returns. When a run did return — when the apprentice came back to the very peg she had left — she did not report the cords. She reported the turning: which way her body faced now, against which way she had set out. That turning was the only thing the walk made, and it was carried up the stairs like water, carefully, and spilled nowhere.

On the far beam hung the iron rings from the grandmother's grandmother, cast whole in the forge. Walking an iron ring always brought a walker back facing exactly as she had set out, empty-handed, and the house said of those rings that they were owned. No one was sent to walk them anymore; there was nothing in them to bring up the stairs.

The web's other circles were not whole. A run of cord would go so far, and the shape of it would plainly want to curve home, and then the cord would end at a peg with nothing continuing, a missing span of dark air between one hook and the hook that would complete the return. Over each such gap the household had nailed a board, and the boards were kept clean. Visitors were brought down and shown the gaps as though they were windows, and in recent years the household had begun to call them the house's openness, and visitors went away envying it. The grandmother had said the gaps were the house being asked to lay the next length. No length had ever been laid.

One winter the apprentice counted the cellar honestly, following every crossing with her lamp. The tangle held eight circles — she traced the possibilities with her fingers until no ninth could be found — while the keeper's book of counts named six, every one of them broken, and not one whole ring anywhere in the house. She said eight in the kitchen. The keeper corrected her to six, gently, as one corrects a child, and no one walked behind her to see; the six were received the way weather is received.

In the corner of the cellar stood the finding hoop the keeper had built in a hungry year, a ring of wood with cords drawn across it, made because the work needed a thing that could show where circles hid. A wet evening, the apprentice traced every cord on the hoop, and every run of it led around the hoop and back into the hoop, so that what it found, always, was itself. It had never once been matched against a walked circle in the beams. She said so at table: it finds itself, and we have been calling that finding. The keeper's face did something small and wrong, and the apprentice was not sent down for a week, and the hoop was oiled.

Every turning she had ever carried up had been said aloud and dropped into the clay bowl by the door, as the custom required. The bowl had never been emptied. No cook had ever taken a word from it; nothing baked in the house had ever been salted with one. Beside the bowl, against the wall, stood a small chair that the grandmother said was meant for whoever would taste the turnings and name the spoiled ones. The chair had never been sat in. It wore a cloth over it, like furniture in a dead room.

When the apprentice asked how the walking had come through the great clearing — the year the shelves were carried out and every written pattern burned — the grandmother laughed in a way that was mostly not laughing. Paper burns, she said. The walking had lived that year in the mouths of the kneading women, hummed over the dough at dawn, and it came back up out of the ashes a little bent and still true. What the house had stored, it lost; what it had recited, it kept.

In the spring the apprentice went down alone, walked one broken circle as far as the last peg, and stood before the board nailed over the missing span. She took the board down and set it against the wall, and measured the dark air with a cord of her own, and cut the length, and left it lying on the floor beneath the gap — laid nothing, fixed nothing. Then she went up and took the cloth off the small chair and set the bowl in front of it. In the morning the household found the open gap, the cut cord, the bare chair, and did not know what any of it was asking, and the keeper came down last, and counted, and stopped at seven, and did not say the eighth.
