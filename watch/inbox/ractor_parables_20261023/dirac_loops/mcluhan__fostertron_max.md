# The Rounds of the Fen

Ines was given her reed at the toll-house door on the morning she joined the walkers, beneath the charter cut into the lintel: ONE TOWN, EVERY PLANK TRUE. The reed was straight as a heron's leg. The tallyman scratched its shadow on the doorpost before she left, so its lean could be read against the scratch when she came back. That was the whole of her teaching: the reed, the scratch, and the rounds.

The fen held thirty-three cairns. Twenty-one planks crossed the water between them, and the planks did not make one town but twenty — twenty islets, thirteen of them single cairns standing in the reeds that no plank had ever touched. A patient eye could trace eight rounds among the planks: eight ways to leave a cairn and come back to it without retracing a step. Everything the fen asked of anybody was worked in this shape. When a fisherman brought a trouble to the toll-house, he brought it as a snarl of cord, and no one pulled ends. They looked for where the cord crossed itself, and a walker walked that crossing, reed on her palms, out over the planks and back.

Then the reading. If the reed came back with its shadow still lying along the scratch, the tallyman shut his mouth and wrote nothing; that round was already the town's, worn in like a doorstep. If the reed had turned — a finger's width, a hand's width — the turning was the catch of the day. He copied the lean into the tallies, and the lean pointed, as everyone knew, at a crossing the town had never laid.

Ines walked six rounds that first autumn. Six reeds came back turned. Not one came back true. They hung on the toll-house wall in a row, and men came to look at them the way they looked at weather.

The horn governed everything. It hung on its own stand inside the door, older than the planks, and no walker left or returned in silence — one note going out, one coming in, the second flatter than the first by exactly the lean of the reed. The tallyman kept the horn in the tallies too, entered among the cairns as if it were a stone, and he kept the tallies themselves in the tallies, page by page, each page numbered and that number written down. Ines noticed that the pages about the horn, and the pages about the pages, now ran longer than the pages about the planks. She said so once. The tallyman said the book was a cairn like any other, and kept writing.

When a reed came back turned, no second walker was sent out to walk the same round and see whether the lean repeated. And no mason ever opened the tallies. The horn called the count at dusk; the town came down to the water to hear the number and went home. In living memory the note had summoned counters, and counters only. The six missing planks stayed missing.

The walkers themselves were the guild's true work, though no one said so. A man who carried a reed for a season could not afterward cross a room without pacing its edges. They read a table, a net, an argument, a marriage; anywhere the cord crossed itself, they itched to walk it. The old ones sat by the fire with their fingers moving on their knees, tracing rounds in the air of a fen they no longer crossed.

The first walker was the grandmother of all of it, and the fire had taken even her name. In the fever year the toll-house burned to the waterline and every paper went to ash — the maps, the tallies, the charter itself, copied four times and burned four times. But her rounds did not burn. The walkers had chanted them at the fires each night, turn by turn, plank by plank, and the chant walked out of the flames in forty mouths. What the town held on paper it lost; what it held in one another it kept. The first walker's name survived only inside the chant, at the turning of the third round, where the voices still carried it.

On the last night of autumn Ines did not strike the horn. She stood at the fire and chanted the six rounds that had turned her reed — cairn to cairn, the missing crossings spoken flat where the planks should have been — the way the first walker's rounds were kept. The chant moved among the walkers like water finding level. By first light, bundles of cut reed stood at the first missing crossing, and by noon there were men at the second, laying plank, and nobody had called them. The horn stayed on its hook. In the toll-house the tallyman opened the tallies to a fresh page, wrote the date, put the pen down, and went out to the water.
