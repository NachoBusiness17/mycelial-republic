# The Eight Rounds

The town stood on thirty-three mounds scattered through a fog lagoon, joined here and there by twenty-one bridges, so that the whole place made twenty neighborhoods that never touched, and no one living had seen it all at once. The fog was old there. When strangers asked when it would lift, the townsfolk said fog was fog, the way water is wet, and went on with their oars.

Every trouble in that country went up to the counting-house on the ninth mound. Whatever a household brought — a boundary quarrel, a drowned ox, a disputed bride-price — the warden on duty tied it into the office cord, one twist apiece, and the cord hung by the door and grew heavier every year. Beside the threshold lay a flat stone with a fan of grooves cut by the founders, and a child who asked about it was told only not to trip.

The town's other habit was the rounds. A round left the counting-house door, crossed bridges, and came home to the same door without crossing any bridge twice. The walker carried the ash pole with a red eye painted on one face, and set out with the eye toward the door; at homecoming the eye had wandered — toward the kiln, the heron rock, the open water — and wherever it had wandered was written in the office book as that walk's answer. A stroll that returned by its own outgoing path was not a round, and nothing was written. A round whose eye came to rest along one of the stone's grooves was called settled, and settled rounds were not walked again.

The ferrymen sang that there were eight rounds and no more: whatever walk a clever child invented came apart, watched long enough, into those eight or several of them strung end to end, and no ninth had ever been found. They had sung the rounds since before any paper. In the burning year every chart in town had gone to the kilns by order from over the water, and the chart now hanging in the office had been drawn afterward by Petra's grandmother, from the singing.

That season two wardens kept the office and shared one skiff: Jonas, the elder, who was never long in any room, and Petra, the younger, who could not pass a drawing without a pencil.

On the seventh mound stood the dead light-tower, the only thing that rose above the fog. One still morning Jonas climbed it and saw whole what no one below had seen: the mounds in their scatterings, the threads of the bridges, and — wherever the chart promised a span — the black breaches where no plank had ever been laid, the water running straight through the ink. He came down dazzled, and for a day could not tell rope from rail, and learned his own home bridges again with his feet.

Petra walked the rounds against the chart. Six of the eight were broken, each somewhere missing a span, and at every breach she had to turn about, and a pole that is turned about keeps its eye where it started, so she wrote nothing. The custom was plain: a warden chalked the breach, and apprentices owed planks before the next fog; none had come in three seasons, and the chalk had gone grey. The last two rounds were whole, but no one had walked them since the burning. And no round in the book had ever been written twice, no walker having followed another to see whether the eye wandered the same way twice; the answers piled page over page, no barge ever called to carry them off, and the fisherfolk steered by the heron rock and the smell of the kiln.

At fog's end the reeve crossed the longest bridge to shut the book. Zero settled, he said; a losing count; the office could stop pretending. Jonas asked him to climb the tower first, and Petra climbed behind, and the three of them stood in the clear light above the fog and marked, together, where the ink promised what the water refused.

Then Petra did not argue the count; she drew. She re-inked the six breaches as spans awaiting hands, a date chalked beside each. She set two fresh eye-marks on the two whole rounds, one walker to go first and a second to follow. She drew the office threshold honestly as a crossing, the counting-house being itself one of the thirty-three, its doings part of the country it counted. And into the cord she tied one thin twist for the counting of the counting-house. The reeve watched her pencil the whole way down and said, at the door, that the book could stay open another year.

By first frost planks were going down across the widest breach, and the book held one round written twice, the two answers sitting a hand apart on the page. The fog stayed. The planks went down anyway.
