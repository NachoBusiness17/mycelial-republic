# The Cavern Course

Ren descended the stone steps into the quarry where the watchmen kept their testing floor. The air smelled of scraped limestone and stale tallow lamps. Before him lay a wide basin where generations of runners trained to cross the dark, guided only by flickering shadows on the rock face. The proctor sat on a pine bench by the gate with iron shears across his knees. When Ren stood before him, the proctor offered no chart or greeting. He reached into a bin and handed Ren a bundle of braided cord, knotted so tightly into a hard fist that both ends were buried in the core. That tangled hemp was the task for his watch.

Ren did not rush toward the dark as the novices had done. Instead, he walked the outer terrace circling the pit to take the measure of the chamber before stepping upon it. Across the basin, thirty-three stone pillars stood upright in the gravel. Between these pillars, twenty-one ropes were stretched taut, pegged into iron rings. The lines formed no single web across the floor; they were clustered into twenty separate islands of stone, each divided from the next by wide gutters of loose shale. A traveler on one side could not reach the other without descending into the dust.

Ren searched the spans for routes that looped back upon themselves. Following the lines from post to post, he found eight independent circuits where a traveler could make a full round and return to the starting pillar. Yet when he reached the anchors, six of those eight circuits hung broken in mid-air. The ropes stopped inches short of the eyelets, their frayed ends dangling over ditches where the company had never laid the closing span. In the entire yard, not one of the eight circuits was closed.

To test what the lines held, Ren unhooked a brass plumb-bob from his belt and rested it in a bowl of dark oil. He carried the bowl along one of the eight ringways, stepping across three taut lines and jumping the ragged gap where the rope ended, returning to the first post. When he leveled the bowl, the suspended thread did not settle over the scribe mark. It hung tilted three degrees toward the outer wall. Had the circuit been closed, the plumb would have rested true upon zero, confirming that the foundation was fixed. But because the loop had an open mouth, the transit returned a shift in the wire, a debt of unfinished work.

At the center of the quarry stood a timber platform holding a heavy brass sighting tube, which the older runners claimed showed the true horizon of the upper world. Ren climbed the rungs and looked through the glass. The barrel did not point toward the roof vents or the sky. It had been swung backward on its gimbal and clamped directly against its own mounting flange, so that anyone looking into the lens saw only the polished face of its own brass collar. The instrument watched its own frame, reporting nothing beyond the screw that bound it.

Ren walked the perimeter to the northern landing, where the manifests claimed wagons arrived to take cargo brought across the ropes. The timber ramp was swept clean, but no teamster waited at the gate, and the boards ended against a blank wall of uncut granite. Along the ropes themselves, no ballast had ever been hung to test the cordage, and no notch marked a proof strain. The lines were strung, but no one had ever tested their strength, and no receiver stood at the far end to take what they carried.

Ren returned to the bench and asked the proctor why the school drilled men upon paths that led nowhere. The old man shook his head. He told Ren that the original drawings and slates had been thrown into the lime kiln three winters ago when the masters purged the citadel's archive. Nothing written had survived the fire. Yet the proctor tilted his head back and hummed an old rhyme, reciting the number and order of the thirty-three pillars from memory, word for word as his grandfather had sung it before the burning.

Ren laid the knotted cord on the pine bench beside the shears. The proctor asked whether he would enter the simulation and run the broken course until exhaustion took him, as the runners had always done. Ren looked back at the wall where shadows danced against the lime. He said that walking out of a rigged room leaves the machinery running for the next prisoner, while running an unproven test only worships the proctor's error. He took up the iron shears, cut the pins that locked the sighting tube against its bracket, and turned the brass barrel away from itself, aiming it straight at the flue where daylight entered the cave.
