# The Tokens No One Wants

In the fen country, every hard question is a tangle of ways, and the custom is older than the moot hall: no tangle is answered by looking at it. You take boots and a staff and you walk. Some of the ways curl back on themselves, so that a walker who steps off a boundary stone at first light comes to that same stone by dusk with the dust of the whole round on her boots. The round pays as it is walked. At the stone she presses a clay token, and the face that rises is the day's turning — how far the road swung her before it brought her home.

Where a round is whole — every plank sound, every bridge in — the face never differs. Generation after generation mints the same one, and a single token of each such round hangs nailed above the moot-hall door, put there before any living walker was born. Walking a whole round is pleasant and teaches nothing. But where a plank has rotted or the water has taken a bridge, the walker comes home empty-handed and wet to the knee, and the custom is plain about that too: the missing stretch is the errand. Masons are fed where the way is absent. The fen does not pity a wet walker; it hands him timber.

The roadwright was a dry man named Ove, who counted before he walked. One season he set it all down: thirty-three boundary stones, twenty-one stretches of sound way, and the ways hanging in twenty clusters that never touch — half the stones stand alone in the sedge, a meeting of ways with nothing meeting. From the arithmetic alone he could say the fen held eight rounds. He sent walkers out. Six of the eight could be attempted at all, and of the six, not one was whole. The lintel of the moot hall got no new nail.

Ove's counting-house stood where the ways curl. Whichever gallery a man took through his premises, he came out by the door he went in, and the eighth round ran through Ove's own gate. Below his garden the ford was out, crossed on a single private plank laid for one man's legs. No mason had ever been fed there. The trade of rounds sat on a round, and kept its own break the way a man keeps a limp he has stopped mentioning.

What each party wanted was no secret. The masons wanted named breaks, because a named break was wages. The walkers wanted the closing of what they had begun; a broken round itched in them like a song stopped a bar short, and they would walk for the itch alone, paid or not — and Ove's trade rode that itch, naming six breaks and keeping the walkers moving without ever buying anything from them. Ove wanted his eight believed. The town wanted herring, salt, and a dry road to market, and no one standing in that road had once asked the price of a token. The shelf of them grew, mud-colored, each with its turning, taken on faith — for there was no assayer in the fen either; nobody had ever walked a round twice to see whether the face held.

In the grandmother Greet's time the water rose and took every paper in the fen — the maps, the tallies, the first walker's own name. What survived survived at the wool-carding, where the old women recited the stretches until the words were smooth as pebbles. What was said aloud could not drown. The first walker herself is only a voice at the wool now, and the fen trusts her anyway; Ove's count agrees with her recitation plank for plank.

One evening at the wool a child asked what the tokens were for, and none of the grandmothers answered, and the silence was the kind that settles over things nobody has ever wanted. Ove heard it and did not answer either. But on the next market day he set the six break-names in a row on the sill where the herring-buyers pass, and stood a little apart to see whether any hand would reach. The buyers bought herring. The walkers walked. And below Ove's own garden the ford lay broken still — the one missing stretch in all the fen that had never once been said aloud at the wool.
