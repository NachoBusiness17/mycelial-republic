# The Unwatered Valley

The upland parish never began its seasonal council with speeches or statutes, but with an entanglement. Whenever the year brought a dispute between the sheep-walks and the haywards, the bailiffs refused to weigh arguments or precedents. Instead, they took a heavy snarl of tarred hemp from the storehouse and laid it upon the mounting-block at the crossroads. Until the disputants sat together in the dust and teased apart that snarl strand by strand, no court opened and no stall was unlocked. The elders knew that every human contention is an intricate snarl of unacknowledged appetites, and that to address the legal quarrel before loosening the tangle merely pulls the cord tighter until it snaps.

Master Corin rode the high crests where the parish was charted. He was an elderly warden who had spent forty years observing the motives of men, learning that communities are never governed by logical syllogisms or official drapery, but by the crude grain of what people actually desire. Across the moors, the valley had erected thirty-three granite cairns to mark its territory, knit together by twenty-one beaten footpaths. Yet the people had drifted into estrangement; twenty detached clusters of stone cottages crouched among the gorse, each small hamlet suspicious of its neighbors and refusing to share its wells. Across the whole expanse, a surveyor could find only eight independent ringways that began at a post and offered to return upon their steps without retracing a track.

By the oldest custom of the hills, an independent circuit was the only journey that yielded a true reckoning. When a shepherd set forth from a boundary stone and rode a full ringway back to his departure post, he returned with a carved wooden tally handed to him at the furthest crossing, a realized measure that discharged his clan’s seasonal obligation. Where a ringway was continuous and unbroken, it confirmed an ancient bedrock, proving that the communal way was already embedded so deeply in the habits of the valley that no authority needed to defend it. But where a track dissolved into marsh, the breach was an unfinished hollow that called forth the common company of laborers, who were summoned with mattocks and barrows to complete the path.

Corin followed the eight ringways one after another across the windy heath. The town leaders had boasted that the ancient bedrock stood firm, but his eyes sought the turf. Six of those eight circuits broke off abruptly into impassable briars, dry gullies, and stone-quarries abandoned long ago. In the whole valley, not one circuit was complete; zero tracks returned upon their origins. The bedrock of settled habit was entirely absent, and six wide breaches lay bare to the weather, neglected by the very clans who claimed the land.

He turned his pony down to the timber pavilion where the water-guild held its hall. In the center stood an intricate tower of polished brass and walnut, humming with weights and escapements. The chief clerk paced beside it, recording its oscillations with solemn pride. Yet when Corin looked behind the wooden plinth, he found that the driving arbor connected to nothing beyond the case. The brass teeth turned an idler wheel, which turned a flywheel that drove a dial marking the friction of the machine itself. No rod extended through the stone wall to the millrace; no chain lifted a weir-gate in the creek. The contrivance was absorbed entirely in itself, expending all its motion solely to register its own mechanical fatigue.

"Has any town inspector ever set a water-level or a plumb against your balance to prove that it keeps true measure?" Corin asked.

The clerk answered that the guild had no need of external trial or proving, for the machine was coherent within its own frame.

"And down by the wash-houses," Corin asked, pointing through the shutters toward the dry stone basins, "does any woman bring a jug to the spigot, or does any dyer wait for your channel?"

The clerk frowned, replying that the tower existed for pure demonstration, far above the common thirst of the village. Corin stepped outside. An apparatus that rides upon no human hunger, he thought, is only an idle toy; when its keepers tire of winding it, the grease will dry and no one will miss its turning.

At the edge of the common, an old shepherd sat beside a turf fire with three apprentices, shaping ash-poles for hurdles. Corin warmed his hands and asked how the valley remembered the true boundary of its first founder, since the Great Fire forty winters back had burned every deed-box and parchment roll in the church vault to grey soot.

The old man smiled into his beard. "The paper burned because paper has no blood," he said. "The fire ate the chests, but it could not reach the chimney-corner. My grandfather sang the founder’s journey to me when I was a boy at the sheepfold, and I have sung it to these lads every winter while we sat with the lambing ewes. What lives in the throat of an old man will survive when every slate in the town hall has crumbled to dust."
