# The Verse on Six Roads

Alder wrote songs the way other men of the river country wrote contracts, because paper mildewed in the wet seasons and tunes did not. Each year at the same tide he sealed a new verse, one copy for each of the six barges, and the six left together up the six canals toward the six cities where his words would be sung before the ice. The song went out. Nothing else ever went with it.

The verse of his last year was short. Its first two lines climbed the same six notes, step for step, note against note, and settled together on a low G. Then all six singers took that one G at once and held it as long as breath allowed. After it the page kept silence for one full measure, and when sound came back it came scattered, one note then another across the whole width of the voice, like seed thrown wide. The end-words of the third line and the fifth chimed, and no others did. The cities found it plain and sang it all winter.

What Alder never sent was the page in the drawer of his desk. The key to that drawer hung on a cord against his skin and went nowhere that he did not. The page was written small and gave each figure its burden: the six notes walked twice and landing as one meant the six hulls and their one cargo, the same words on every deck; the G held sixfold meant the thing that must not differ from itself, whatever else went under; the measure of silence meant the lock at the head of the canals, where the six waters narrowed to one and every hull waited, and where the gates opened only when the water in the chamber stood flat, neither rising nor rocking; the scatter that followed meant the letting-go, the fan of wakes on the far side; the chiming pair meant the two winters a verse stayed good, and no more. The last line of the page was about the verse itself: this song is the shape of its own reading. He never copied the page, never spoke a word of it, and refused flatly the one collector who offered a horse for it. "Six hulls, one song," he told the man. "A page would be a seventh."

He died in spring. The cord and the drawer passed to his niece Iva, who had tuned his instruments all her life and asked him nothing.

By then the verse no longer needed him. The singing houses of the delta had improved it — a brighter turn at the close, an extra echo in the second line — and the improved kind traveled back up the canals, so that the six cities now learned the song from the delta's copies rather than from Alder's barges. The annotators of the delta printed a commentary that gave every figure its meaning: the sixfold tone was the memory of a drowned king, the silence was his tomb, the scatter was his inheritance. The book was detailed, and the detail gave it credit. Pilgrims came on the first night of ice to hear the six choirs of the delta take the low G together, six processions converging by water, and if you asked any of them who had first said to gather, no one could tell you; it had simply always been the custom, from three winters ago.

Iva went down once, with the cord under her coat. In the booksellers' row a man offered her the original, meaning the delta edition, finely stitched and twice the length. From the back of the hall she listened: the two lines still climbed their six steps, note against note; the G still fell sixfold; the silence still held before the scatter; the third and fifth still chimed and nothing else did. Everything the page in her drawer said was still true of what six cities sang, and none of them had ever seen the page. She bought the commentary for a small coin, rode home, opened the drawer, and added one line below the last: the copies keep the shape by themselves now. Then she locked it, wore the key, and sent nothing.
