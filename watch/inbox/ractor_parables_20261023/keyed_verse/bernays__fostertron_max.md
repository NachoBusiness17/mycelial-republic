# The Sixfold Song

The cove kept its harbor behind a door of rock, and the door opened for no key. It opened at a certain hour for a song, and the song was sung from six towers standing along the cliff, each tower within earshot of the next, all of them within earshot of the water.

Every morning the keeper, an old man named Ivor, copied the day's song from his slate onto six cards. He copied the words first, then the notes above them, and he copied each card from the slate itself and never from another card, so that no slip could travel from tower to tower. A young singer named Corin, who had the finest voice in the cove, asked leave to add a small turn at the close of the second line — only a turn, the kind that made the fish-wives look up. Ivor struck it out and handed back the plain note. "Sing to the gulls if you want to be wept at," he said. "The door is not an audience."

The six cards went out at dawn, one to each tower, carried by six runners who never swapped. The singers sang at the same hour, and the song had a shape everyone knew by heart and almost no one knew the meaning of. Twice in the middle it walked the same six pitches, step by step, and both walks came down together on one low note. When the six towers reached that close as one — one note from six throats, no voice early, none late — the sound did not pass along the water; it stood on it, leaning on itself the way a wave in a tub leans on its own reflection and climbs. That standing tone was the only thing the rock listened to. It swung.

Then a silence, exactly as long as one held breath. Then the bells, released, scattered — each tower taking its own line up and out and away from the shared note — and the ships came through and fanned across the open water toward every point of the compass.

The rule the children learned first, because they could sing it before they could say it: the door answers six as one. Corin proved the other half one windless evening by singing alone from his tower, better than the six had ever sung it, and the door sat in the cliff like any other stone.

In Ivor's room above the fish-hall lay a thin book, and the book never left the room. No runner carried it; no tower held it overnight; no copy of it had ever been made to travel. When a singer named Sela begged to know what she was singing for, Ivor read to her from the book but gave her nothing to take. Where the line walks its six steps twice, he read, that is the six of you, each given the very same; where you come down together on the low note, that is the thing that must not change; where you fall silent and then scatter, that is the door and what comes after it; and the song itself, learned whole and sung true, is the lesson that teaches its own singing. Sela climbed back to her tower with the words in her ears and nothing in her hands, and by the top step some of the words had gone soft, which was also part of the design. The towers held the song. Only the room held the meaning.

In the autumn a man came to the cove with a wagon painted gold and a voice like warm oil. He said the old song was grey and the cove deserved better, and he taught a new close in the square by candlelight, free to anyone. He kept the sixfold walk — he was careful to keep that — but where the six notes should have come down together he set a triumphant rise, his own invention, easier to cheer. Believing the new close, people felt the door owed them an opening, and the feeling was so pleasant that they sang it to their children. The man in the gold coat put a rope around his candlelit square and began charging at the rope.

On the fog-day, with the fleet outside and the fish running, the square sang the gold close with real love. Three towers, taught by their mothers, sang it too. Three sang the old card. Over the water the two closes beat against each other and died, and the rock did not move, and the man at the rope kept the coins. The people had paid for the feeling of the door opening, which he could supply nightly, and not for the door, which he could not.

The cove went back to the cards after that, and the fog lifted on its own terms. Corin still sang his turn at the close, but only to the gulls, on the far side of the cliff where the water could not hear him. And when visitors asked Ivor why the rock trusted six ordinary voices and not the finest one in the cove, he answered as he understood it: the rock has no ear for quality. It opens for nothing but agreement.
