# The Song Given to the Roads

Sela made one song in her life, and she made it the way good bread is made: for strangers to eat. At the crossroads in spring she handed it to six singers, one copy each, and told them to sing it in every bathhouse and tavern down their own road, to let it be copied, mis-sung, worn smooth by foreign mouths. The more hands it passes through, she said, the better it works. The six took their copies and went six ways, and each one, as porters do, mistook the load for a title.

Sewn into the cuff of her coat she kept a small grey card, crowded with her shorthand: half-lines of the song down one side, plain words down the other, written by one person for one person. That she gave to no one. Not to the singers, not to the landlord who fed her through a winter, not to the rich man who offered a house for it — she asked him whether he also bought ears at the market, since he clearly shopped for organs he lacked. The song went everywhere; the card went nowhere. That is the whole architecture of the trick, and it is not a trick, only patience: a thief in a port town once copied the entire song off a drunk singer, every note and every rhyme, and walked away owning a pretty noise he could not spend.

When the six came back in autumn they met at the same crossroads, and their copies carried the same furniture. In the second line, and again in the fifth, six notes climbed in the same order twice — identically, twice — and both times the phrase walked down and landed on one low tone and stopped dead on it. A child who had trailed the singers asked why the same walk ends in the same place twice. The proudest of the six said it was grief for the drowned, and invented a drowning, with names in it. Sela, on the fence, said nothing. She set six pebbles in a row on the flat stone, then laid one hand over all of them at once. That was her entire commentary.

Then she asked them to sing the low line together, the line where one tone is struck six times — the same tone, six times, every voice on it at once. The youngest, clever in the throat, curled the last strike into a little ornament, the way clever throats do when plainness frightens them. Sela flattened her hand in the air, and he sang it plain. A tone that every mouth holds together belongs to no mouth; make it pretty on one road and plain on another, and the song becomes a liar with six heads.

Up the hill stands a mill with a habit: its door rises only for a level tone — a note held steady, neither climbing nor sagging, unwavering as long as it takes to walk through. Five singers threw their trembles at it and it stayed shut. The sixth held one tone flat and still, like water that has stopped pretending to be a river, and the door rose. And in the song itself, just after that figure, comes the part the child loved: a silence exactly one breath long, then notes flung wide — high and low at once, no two near each other. Sela kept her palm flat over the pebbles through the silence, then swept all six off the stone in a single splash. After a door that admits only what stands still, everything scatters.

A man came through at noon carrying a lit lantern and asked each singer, in full daylight, what the song meant. He received six confident answers, all different, and nodded as one nods at six counterfeit coins from six proud mints. Porters, he said, bragging of porcelain they have agreed to call bread. The song only ever asked you for your feet.

Her last winter, Sela took an apprentice and never told her a single meaning. Learn the shapes, she said; the shapes keep their word. By spring the girl could hear a strange song once and name the road it had walked. They say the last entry on the card is the song itself — a rhyme about a rhyme, the ballad that carries its own grammar in its bones. Where the card went after, no one can say. It was never seen again, which is exactly what never giving a thing away looks like from the outside.

And you, the one holding the cuff — mark what you are still asking the world for. You give the song to every road all day, then stand at the threshold of every ear, waiting to be admired as its only reader. You could stop asking today. The card is not your title; it is your chore. Give the song away, keep the ear, and go home.
