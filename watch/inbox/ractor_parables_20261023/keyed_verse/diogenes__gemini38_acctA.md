# The Cistern at Noon

You are still asking the world for an embroidered certificate before you trust your own eyes. You want the choir to carry a wooden sign around their necks explaining what each breath is supposed to accomplish, so that you never have to stand in the sun and do the honest labor of listening. Stop asking the world to flatter your doubts; a dog does not ask for a pedigree before taking bread from your hand.

Six boys stood along the limestone rim of the dry reservoir, each stationed before his own sluice-gate. The midday heat beat down upon the courtyard, where city officials were busy trading debt-scrolls and reciting genealogies. An elder in a coarse wool mantle, carrying an unlit clay lamp in broad daylight, walked the rim. He carried six slips of rough scrap paper torn from a single sheet. Upon each slip was inscribed an identical eight-line song, setting down the meter and cadence of a plain tune. He handed one slip to each boy. When the third boy asked what the verses were intended to prove, the elder touched the boy’s collarbone with the cold spout of his lamp. "Sing what is scratched there," the elder said. "Your throat is not a courtroom; do not ask it to debate what it has only to sound."

Beneath his mantle, pressed against his ribs, the elder kept a slab of dark slate marked with charcoal ciphers. On that slate, every rhyme and every interval of the song was paired with the physical workings of the stone channels—how the reservoir would fill, when the oak timbers would bite, and how the runoff would divide. The boys glanced toward his cloak, wanting to see the slate and know the design beforehand, but the elder never drew it out. He refused to copy a single mark onto their slips, and he did not permit any hand to touch it. "If I give you the cipher," he told them plainly, "you will spend the afternoon arguing over the charcoal instead of keeping your rhythm. The slate stays against my ribs."

The boys smoothed their papers against their palms. Across the first two lines of the song, six identical pitches rose in steady succession, climbing note for note without straying, until every voice met on the fourth bell's middle tone. Each of the six boys read the exact same phrases from his paper; no boy was given a higher flourish or a lower turn than his neighbor. They took the same dust into their lungs and formed the same syllables, so that six separate sluice-mouths received the exact same measure of sound.

Then came the true test. At the close of the second line, all six voices struck that middle bell-tone together. It was not a sweet harmony designed to soothe a parlor; it was a bare, unvarnished unison, six throats locked onto a single pitch. While that tone rang against the stones, the water in the intake channel did not stir, the heavy oak pins stayed in their sockets, and the balance beam hung motionless. Nothing was permitted to shift while that common tone filled the basin. A city inspector in dyed sandals called up from the road, shouting that one boy ought to sing an ornamental fifth above the rest to show civic refinement, but the boys held the unison flat and hard, shaming his pretense with an undivided note.

Then, at the mark in the verse, the six boys shut their mouths at once. A total rest fell upon the stones. For four full counts of a quiet pulse, no throat hummed, no foot shifted, and not an echo lingered along the limestone. The basin became as still as an unswung hammer. It was the gate of stillness, where all momentum gathers and tests whether the quiet holds true before any stream may pass forward.

Only when the fourth count of silence was complete did the song break wide. From the hush, their voices burst into a climbing arpeggio that leaped across five full octaves. One voice soared toward the battlements, another plunged into the deep gravel of the drainage trench, and the rest scattered through the wide intervals between, like water kicked from an uncurbed trough. Having held the quiet gate together, their single line fanned outward into separate paths, tripping the wooden catches and releasing the reservoir into six different channels across the terrace.

The inspector climbed the stairs, out of breath and brandishing a sheaf of stamped parchment, demanding to know where the rules of the reservoir were registered so his clerks could certify them. The elder set his unlit lamp upon the rim, sat down with his bare legs dangling over the stone, and dipped his feet into the rushing current. "There is no register," the elder said, rinsing the road dust from his heels. "The song did the work, and the water is in the trough. If you still doubt it, take off your shoes and feel whether you are wet."
