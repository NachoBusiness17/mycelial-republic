# The Held Note

The woman who wrote the song lived above the flood meadow, and she wrote it the year the river rose twice. She copied it out six times, and the six copies were exact: the same marks, the same words, the same dark line at the top. She did not choose the best voices in the valley. She chose six singers — in the flood town, the mill town, the salt town, the orchard town, the town under the cliff, and the town at the ford — and what she chose them for was that they would sing what was written and add nothing.

A courier carried the pages down the valley, one to each town, and the pages agreed with one another completely. In the second stanza and again in the fifth, the same six quick notes came down the same steps and landed on the same low tone. Every line ended in a word that chimed with another word farther down. In the middle of the song, one note carried a mark that meant: hold, to the end of the bell. And near the end there was a measured silence, after which — the mark said — each singer was to leave the common tone and take a note of her own, and no two were to take the same.

When the flood bell rang at noon, the wind took it up the valley, and the six singers began together, each in her own town. A traveler on the ridge that day heard one song coming from six directions. Through all the stanzas it stayed one song. Then the long note came — the same low tone the six steps had landed on — and it was one pitch everywhere, held while the bell's hum rose, leaned, and finally stood still. In the quiet after it, the six voices left the note one after another, each onto a different tone, the low town deepest, the orchard town highest, and from the ridge the one song came apart into six and went on as six.

On the woman's desk lay a book no larger than a hand. Beside the six quick notes she had written: six towns, one page. Beside the long note: what does not move. Beside the measured silence and the parting of the voices: the crossing, then the fields. Some entries sat beside pairs of chiming words instead of notes; beside the last two words of the song she had written: a song like this one.

The courier asked, once, whether the book should ride down with the pages, so that the towns could read what they were singing. She wrapped the six copies in oilcloth and told him the pages traveled light. He did not ask again, and the book never crossed her threshold.

The singer in the orchard town, who was young, copied her page against the rule and wrote a small guess in the margin beneath the long note. A clean page came back up the valley with the next post, and nothing else with it. She sang from the new page, kept the old one folded small in her sleeve, and sang nothing that was not written.

One winter the woman struck out the words beside the long note and set different words in their place. The six pages did not change. At noon the singers held the same pitch at the same hour as every noon before; the note was the note, what the book said beside it was new, and no one in the valley was told.

She died in the spring the water took the lowest field. The book passed to her granddaughter, who read it through once, sent nothing to anyone, and put it in the drawer with the spectacles. The singers sang at noon as they had been taught. The song kept its shape without her, exact in six towns, and nothing in it asked to be understood.

In the sixth year a traveler stood on the ridge at the appointed hour and heard the long note come from six directions at once, one pitch over the whole valley while the bell's hum stood. Afterward he came down into the flood town and asked the singer there what the note meant. She held it to the very end of the bell before she answered, and then she said: it is the same everywhere. That was all she had been given, and she gave it.
