# The Choral Siphon

In the valley of the dry limestone, the watermaster did not govern his conduits with winches or leaden counterweights. Metal corroded in the sulfurous spray, and workmen were prone to fatigue, distraction, and grief. Instead, he hollowed a vaulted chamber beneath the municipal reservoir and divided its floor into six identical stone trenches. Each trench was thirty paces long, cut to the same width from mountain rock, and washed clear of grit every dawn.

An overseer named Arnaud sat in a stone gallery above them. Every morning, six youths were brought down from the hospice. They had not been instructed in the mechanics of siphons, nor had they ever seen the copper discs that held back the upper basin. Arnaud handed each youth a leaf of paper inscribed with four stanzas of verse. It was a rhythmic chant honoring the patron of dry wells. The singers believed they were offering prayer for the harvest. They did not know that syllable lengths, consonants, and rhyme placements had been calculated to vibrate bronze reeds mortised into the floor.

Behind the gallery bench, locked in a pearwood box fastened with iron studs, lay a narrow roll of calfskin. It had never been duplicated and was never brought down to the trenches. Arnaud permitted no one to inspect it. On its surface, two parallel columns had been inked: the left listed the rhymes and rising intervals of the chant; the right listed the movements of the intake valves, the release of the copper discs, and the allowable drift of the reservoir head. The youths received only the verses. The calfskin stayed hidden, for technique demands that the human instrument remain unconscious of the apparatus it animates, lest understanding breed hesitation.

When the bell struck seven, Arnaud raised his staff. In their six separate trenches, separated by wet ashlar partitions so that none could see his neighbor, the six youths began to recite. They sounded the opening lines on six identical rising pitches. Because the text in each trench was identical in meter and phrasing, the sound advanced through the corridors at the exact same velocity, pressing against the air columns without variance. From his perch, Arnaud watched the indicator pins slide in their grooves. The six separate paths performed identical work under an identical compulsion, uniform and blind.

At the second stanza, the verse forced the voices inward, converging upon the fourth note above middle C. In each trench, the pitch was held without ornament, clean and unyielding. Six pairs of lungs produced one solitary, unwavering sound. In the convergence gallery where the trenches met, the air did not churn. Because the pitch was uniform across all six conduits, pressure reached an equilibrium that held the balance beam motionless in the dark. It was the single unbending constant: a shared state that had to be maintained without discrepancy across every trench before the stone would yield.

That sustained tone struck the throat of the siphon, where the draft met the backwash of the underground lake. There, two equal waves of acoustic force collided in opposition, creating a stationary crest of motionless air between the stone jambs. It hovered without advancing or retreating, an impenetrable barrier formed purely of balanced vibration. The water pressed against the copper threshold, but the frozen crest held the trip-latches jammed in their mountings. For three measures, the entire works remained suspended by the tension of opposing energies, locked in a stillness that simulated absolute rest.

The sudden surge of wide intervals rushed through the chamber, scattering the gathered pressure across twelve diverging spillways at once.

The reservoir discharged its flood into the stone canals with flawless economy. The fields received their portion; the vineyards were watered; the municipal registers recorded another morning of optimal distribution. Arnaud closed the pearwood box and watched the singers climb the stone steps into the gray morning, their coats damp with condensation. They smiled because the harmony had felt noble inside the vault. They asked Arnaud whether their devotion had been acceptable to heaven.

Arnaud looked at them with cold, exhausted eyes. He did not tell them that their piety was merely the trigger for a siphon, that their devotion had been swallowed by an acoustic calculation, or that tomorrow, when the reservoir deepened, their verses would be rewritten to make them breathe more rapidly.
