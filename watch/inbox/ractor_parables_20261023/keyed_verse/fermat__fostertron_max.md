# The Song and the Sheet That Stayed

In the year the river took the low bridge, the old master of the singing house set down a verse of nine stanzas, and he would not let anyone call it old. At the head of every copy the ink-boy was ordered to write *made, not found*, and when a copyist once set *ancient air of the valleys* in that place, the master made him scrape the page and begin again. A thing might be sung falsely, he said, but not dated falsely.

He boasted of the verse in the margin of the fourth volume of the house book, in a hand smaller than the print: that it carried its whole meaning inside its own figures, and that the margin was too narrow to hold the proof. Then he folded a single sheet into that margin, and the sheet was the proof, and it did not leave the house again while he lived.

Six copies went out on one morning, one to each of the singing houses in the six valleys, carried by six riders who had all drunk from the same jug before leaving. Each rider carried the verse and nothing more — the notes, the words, nothing written under or between. Before they left, the east gate was tried against them. That gate lifted for no key a man could carry; it rose only for a knock given in step, six fists, one beat, and it had been known to stand shut all day against a ragged hundred. The six knocked as one, the gate rose, and on the far side the riders broke their line and spread over the hills, each to his own valley, and did not ride together again.

In the autumn the third valley wrote back: we sing it wrongly, send the meaning. The master sent another copy of the bare verse and nothing else, and when the valley begged a second time he sent the verse a third time, and the sheet stayed folded where it was.

I saw it once. He showed it to me for the length of one poured cup and no longer. The line-ends that chimed were paired there, and against each pair stood a few words in his small hand. Where the verse set the same six notes in two lines, one under the other, and both lines settled on the ground tone, the words said: the six, one morning, one letter. Where every voice was made to sound that ground tone at once — all the singers on the single pitch, neither more nor less — the words said: what holds. Where the singers fell quiet for the length of a heartbeat and then flung the notes wide, one to each string, the words said: after the door, so, and never before it.

On the feast of the frost he bade the six houses sing the verse back to him on one morning and write him their final tones. Five ended on the ground tone together. The sixth ended a step above it. He did not blame the valley. He went down instead: from the singing house to the copy on its stand, from the copy to the rider who had carried it, from the rider to the ferry, where the page had taken damp and a hurried crossing-keeper had re-inked one note higher, to better it. Step to the step before, all the way back to the one morning. The bare verse went out again; the sheet did not; and the next winter all six houses ended together.

A composer in the north town wrote that no such sheet existed and that the verse meant whatever a singer pleased. The master answered: take my verse, change one note in your house, sing it back, and I will name the note you changed. Letters crossed; he named the note from the endings alone; the north went quiet about meanings after that.

The master died in the spring. The fourth volume keeps the sheet, and I have not held it since; I can stand next to that meaning, but I do not own it. The verse still rides out every year, still bare, six copies on one morning, and the houses still end together on the ground tone. Anyone who wants the gloss must come with clean hands to the book, because in all these years the meaning has never once traveled with the song. And this is the strangeness of it: sung whole and read beside the sheet, the song tells the story of its own journey, figure by figure, and yet no rule is ever spoken aloud in it.
