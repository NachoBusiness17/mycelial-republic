# The Canon of the Fourth Bell

The provincial court had long recognized that men will quarrel over any decree written in plain words, finding ambiguities in the phrasing and exceptions in the margins. The Magistrate therefore preferred the discipline of numbers, where a truth is neither debated nor excused, but simply found to hold or fail. In the octagonal hall of the lower tribunal, six vaulted cells were carved into the stone perimeter, separated from each other by heavy ashlar pillars so that a man seated in one could neither see his neighbor nor hear his breath.

To each of these six cells, the usher carried an identical sheet of verse. It was a hymn of four rhyming stanzas, written in the standard notation of the cathedral, inked cleanly with iron gall. Not a syllable differed from the first sheet to the sixth; the commas were placed with identical spacing, and the dry seal at the foot of each page bore the same weight. Each clerk was charged only with singing what was set before him, exactly as it was set.

Inside the cuff of his robe, the Magistrate kept a narrow strip of parchment. Upon it he had drawn a small table of concordances, aligning the rhymes, the caesuras, and the intervals of the hymn with the silent dispositions of the court. This parchment had not been copied, nor had the usher been permitted to touch it. It remained beneath the Magistrate’s hand, kept strictly from the six cells, for a key ceases to govern the lock the moment it is distributed among the wards.

The hymn was so composed that throughout its second couplet, six successive syllables sounded upon the exact same pitch, rising without alteration until they resolved together upon the tone of the fourth bell—the middle G of the chapel organ. Because the sheets were indistinguishable, each clerk began from that identical foundation. When they struck that fourth tone, no singer bent the pitch to suit his natural range, nor did any linger behind the measure.

In the center of the hall, their six voices joined under the dome. A sound divided into parts will usually beat against itself, the crests and hollows jostling the air like water in a narrow basin. But here the six voices struck the fourth bell in true unison. The acoustic pulse disappeared; the air grew level, dense, and still. From the center of the vault hung a silk thread weighted with a lead pellet; during their unison, the pellet did not tremble by a hair’s breadth, resting in the center of its ring as if suspended in ice.

At the entrance to the lower archive stood a balanced bronze grille, mounted upon copper reeds that answered only to resonance. So long as any voice wavered, the alternating pressure kept the locking pins engaged. But under the stationary wave where the sound held motionless without flutter or rebound, the air pressed against the reeds with uniform weight. The counterpoise sank, the pins slid into their sockets, and the heavy grille lifted into the arch without a sound.

On the parchment, immediately following that resolve, stood an empty measure marked with a double cross—a sudden and absolute rest. The six clerks ceased together on the instant. For the duration of three heartbeats, the hall fell into complete silence, while the lifted grille settled into its stops. Only then, from the stillness, did the score release them into a wide, ascending arpeggio that leaped across three full octaves. No longer gathered into one tone, the voices branched outward in rapid, leaping intervals, each clerk singing his allotted flourish into his own gallery to carry the finished judgment to the outer courts.

The canon of the chapter house, listening from the balustrade, leaned forward to commend the clerks for so sweet a melody, praising the neat turns of the rhyme and the gentle cadences of the harvest theme. He asked the Magistrate whether such music might be copied for the choir at Toulouse. The Magistrate folded his hands into his sleeves and said that the composition was complete as it stood, possessing neither need of an addition nor room in its margins for another voice. When the canon turned away, the Magistrate walked alone into the archive through the open archway, his hand resting upon the folded slip in his coat, while the silence in the vault closed behind him.
