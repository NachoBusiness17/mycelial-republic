# The Drawer That Sang Nothing

Each autumn the examining house issued one song to six stands. The stands stood in a row along the wall of the practice hall, each with its own lamp, each facing its own corner, and every morning the registrar laid a fresh sheet on every one of them — six sheets, struck from a single plate, so that no singer could claim a different page. On the sheets was a verse: rhymed lines with a melody stitched above them, and the singers were instructed to sing what was written and nothing else. What the verse meant was not a question the house put to them, and they had long ago learned not to put it to the house.

The meaning lived in another room, in the registrar's drawer, on one slip of paper ruled into two columns. Down the left column went the figures of the song — each rhyme, each turn of the melody — and down the right, the thing each figure stood for. The drawer was locked. The slip was never copied, never posted, never carried out to any stand. A young singer once asked to see it, and the registrar told her, not unkindly, that the house does not explain its examinations to the examined, and that a proof explained is a proof spoiled.

Yet the house examined without pause. Two readers sat behind the stands with slates, marking every deviation. Each night the night-clerk collected the slates; the door-warden countersigned the clerk's tally; and twice a year the warden's own log was gone through by the registrar, in a room whose door the singers could see from their stands. The line of gazes closed into a ring, and no one inside it stood beyond being looked at.

The verse itself was plain about one thing only. Two of its lines were built alike: in each, the same six pitches, one after another, stepped down and came to rest on a single G — and that G was taken by all six voices at once, together, on a sign. The readers listened for it the way a magistrate listens for the name in a will. If the six did not land as one — one early, one flat, one holding past the others — the morning was void, and nothing sung before or after it counted as anything. That unison was the house's one article of faith, and the singers held it as tenants hold a wall.

After the shared G came a silence, a full measure in which no one breathed on purpose, and then a wide chord: the six flung apart, high and low, each taking its own interval for the first time in the song. Here, and only here, difference was no fault. The slates stayed quiet through the scatter; the readers had been taught that the silence was a threshold, that past the threshold the six were meant to part, and that a parting attempted before the silence was the same as no parting at all.

One winter an outside ear came to visit — an examiner from a distant house, with leave to ask the forbidden questions. She asked the registrar what the verse meant, and the registrar, to everyone's surprise including her own, unlocked the drawer. Beside the two built-alike lines the slip said: the six stands are fed from one plate. Beside the sixfold G: this must hold, or nothing after it is evidence. Beside the silence before the wide chord: past this they may differ. The outside ear read the columns twice and understood that the song was not a song. It was a drawing of the room — the stands, the plate, the threshold, the scatter — folded into notes and rhymes so that it could travel as music and be denied as a drawing.

She saw, too, how the room was cut. The singers had the verse and could not read it; the registrar had the reading and could not sing it. Whoever held the slip named what the unison meant; whoever stood at a stand supplied only the sound that proved it held. And when the outside ear asked what became of singers who read the slip, the registrar brought out the file. Years back, one had copied two lines of the right-hand column onto his cuff and sung his knowledge into the G. It landed perfectly — and perfectly wrong — and he was sent to a house that keeps no drawer.

The drawer was locked again. On her way out, the outside ear passed the six stands, where a new verse was being struck from a new plate: the same shared G, and, in a place of honor, a longer silence before a wider scatter. Behind the stands the slates filled; behind the slates the clerk; behind the clerk the warden; behind the warden the registrar, whose own drawer the record shows was audited that same season by the examining house — the same one, the file says, that had sent the outside ear to ask.
