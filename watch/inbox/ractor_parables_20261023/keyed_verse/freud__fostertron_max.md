# What the Valley Repeated

Every year, on the first cold evening, the messenger went down the river with six folded sheets in his coat, and at each of the six villages he left one. The sheets were copied by the schoolmaster and checked line against line, so that no singer held a single note more than any other. The singers never met: a ferryman, a midwife, a goatherd, a carter, a weaver, a schoolboy. At dusk each stood at the edge of a village and sang what was written, from the first mark to the last, changing nothing.

I had come up the river for my own rest and stayed for the valley's. My trade is listening; the carter could not sleep, and he slept better in the third week of my stay, which he attributed to my draughts and I did not. It was through him that I borrowed a sheet.

Two lines near the middle of it wore the same six notes, note for note, like twins walking, and both lines stepped down at the end onto one plain tone, neither high nor low, the color of fog. Near the close there was a small square of silence, drawn like a held breath, and after it the notes flung wide, from the cellar to the roof of the voice, a dozen sounds in one throw, spreading the way seed spreads. The words rhymed in pairs, always in pairs, and turned year upon year on the same few sounds. I asked the weaver, who had sung longer than anyone, what the fog tone meant. She said it meant it was evening. I asked what the twin six notes meant. She said they meant the singing was nearly over. I asked why the wide throw came after a silence, and she looked at me as one looks at a man who has asked why rain follows cloud.

On a still evening I rowed the length of the river and heard all six villages arrive at the fog tone in the same moment — each in its own country voice, and the tone itself the same in every mouth, the same, the old men said, as long as there had been sheets. Long ago a clever carter had curled the tone into something finer. No one reproached him. The next year his sheet came back with the tone plain and a small cross beside it, and he sang it plain the rest of his life; he told me the note had simply come back to him, the way a name comes back to you at a door.

I noticed that the tone did not stay inside the singing. The midwife hummed it over her kettle without knowing that she hummed. The ferryman's oar knocked it out against the current. Children who could not yet read clapped it in the road — four small claps and the fog note, four small claps and the fog note. Whatever the sheets were carrying, the valley could not stop producing it, though nobody could say what it was for.

At the head of the river lived the widow of the man who had written the sheets. In her drawer lay a thin grey book tied with a shoelace, and no messenger had ever carried it. When the elder of the third village asked her to send it down the water so that everyone might know what the songs meant, she put her hand flat upon it and said that the post was for the singing, the book was for her, and it had never gone out and would not begin with him. He went home offended, and on the next first-cold evening his village sang as loudly as any. Once a year, on the morning after the singing, the widow took the book down, read the sheet against it with her finger — and her finger, the messenger told me over wine, rested twice as long on the rhymes as on the notes — then made one line of ink in the margin of her chronicle and tied the lace again. He had carried sheets for twenty years and had never seen inside it, and had stopped wanting to.

The words of the sheets, each year, told some small story: a sower who waited for a wind before he threw; a mill-door that opened only between two bells; six sisters setting the same table in six houses. The farmers did not know they had learned anything. But they had taken, within living memory, to waiting for the wind before sowing, and to passing the mill only at the turn of the bell, and no one had taught them by word. It is done, they said, and shrugged.

The widow died in the spring. The valley feared the songs would die with her, but she had left the book to her granddaughter, with instructions the girl would repeat only in part: send the sheets, never the book; read the morning after; one line, no more. On the first cold evening the six villages sang, the fog tone held in six mouths at once, and up at the head of the river a lamp burned past midnight and a pen moved for the length of one line.
