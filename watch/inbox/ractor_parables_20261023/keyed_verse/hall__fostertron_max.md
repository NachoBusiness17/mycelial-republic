# The Book That Stayed Home

In the winter of the flood, Maren wrote one song and sent it out to six towns. She copied it six times in her own hand, and though the copies travelled by six different roads — the river road, the ridge road, the road under the pines — every page carried the same marks, down to the smallest comma of ink. Riders were stopped and searched at each boundary, in case the song was going out with anything tucked behind it. It never was. What each town received was the song, and only the song.

The song had a stubborn shape. Twice the melody climbed the same six tones, and twice it fell to the same low tone at the bottom, like a stone let down twice into one well; the two climbs even ended on the same rhyme, which everyone took for art. The mill town decided the six tones were six mules on one rope. The fishing town heard six oars in one boat. The hill town, having less water to think about, heard six brothers. Maren sent no note with the song and answered no letter about it, so each town kept the meaning it had made in the room where the song was first sung, and no town's meaning was any emptier for being homemade.

Near the middle came the bar no town argued about. Every line that had been wandering its own way came down onto one tone and held it, all voices together, six times over. When the choirs met at the spring fair and sang the song side by side, that was the only bar they did not rehearse; it arrived whole in every mouth. A choir that shook on it was asked to sing it again before anyone would trade with them. A choir that held it could change whatever else it pleased. The fair gave no prize for the best reading of the song. There was no such prize to give.

After the held tone, the song fell silent — one long rest, longer than seemed polite — and when the notes returned they scattered, climbing away from one another until no two were within reach.

The oldest people in the towns remembered what that winter had actually been. A single notice had to reach all six towns before the water did. It crossed the old arch at the ferry, where the ferrymen turned back any load that swung and passed only what hung steady on its frame, and on the far bank it was copied six times and given to the six roads. The last line of every copy — the line naming the hour the water would arrive — was forbidden to differ by so much as a breath. The song had been written the week after, and the towns had assumed it was about grief, or about the mules.

If Maren ever learned what the towns had made of her song, she left no sign of it. The drawer in her house held the winter's meanings and not one word about the mules.

Maren died in the third winter. A young woman came up from the capital to catalogue what the water had spared, and in the drawer under the north window she found a small book in Maren's hand that no rider had ever carried anywhere. It was not a diary. Beside each figure of the song it set a short plain note, the way a cook labels a jar; beside the long rest it said only, the arch. The young woman sat with the song and the book and read the winter back out of them, bar by bar. The two climbs and the two falls were the six roads and the one notice. The tone every voice held was the last line that could not differ. The scattering was the far bank, the copying, the many roads going their own ways. She could even tell which road had been sung first and which last. The song had been the record all along, complete without the book, and the book only said what its figures had stood for, for whoever came looking.

Before she left, the young woman copied the song six times in her own hand and sent it up to six new towns that were building on higher ground. The riders carried nothing else. The book went back into the drawer. That autumn the song was sung in six new rooms, and it became about six new things — a well, a wedding, a debt forgiven — none of which anyone had sent.
