# The Cellar Book

Every evening the six singers took up six sheets in six rooms along the cold corridor and sang the same song from the first bar to the last. The sheets were copied each month by one hand, and no singer ever held a paper different from any other. The old master had written the song before he died, and the house had sung it since, in the same order, at the same hour, without one variation the walls had ever heard.

Twice in the song came the figure the singers called the flocking: six notes of one height, set in two lines that ran side by side, both lines settling at last onto a single tone. That tone was the one thing in the house no one softened. A singer might hurry a phrase or weep through a rising passage — the house forgave all of it. But once, on a night of frost, the newest singer let the tone lean, only a finger's width, and the inner door between the corridor and the master's hall sat dead in its frame, and no lamp in the passage would light, and nothing in the house answered until he sang the six notes clean again and held the last of them, with every voice upon it, until the air in the doorway stopped trembling. Then the door swung of itself, as it always did, and the song went on.

Past that door the song fell silent for one beat — the held breath, the singers called it — and on the far side of the silence the chord broke open wide, each voice taking a different distant note, the six of them climbing away from one another like seed thrown from a single hand.

No singer could say what any of it meant. Asked, they laughed the way workers laugh at a question about a doorknob's weight. It opens the door. It is sung. The master wrote it so.

Below the singing rooms, down a stair most of the house had forgotten, lived the keeper, the one who copied the sheets. He had been the master's copyist while the master breathed, and he had kept one command past the grave. The master had written two things in his life. The song went up to the rooms. The other, a thin brown book, went nowhere; on its first page, in the master's own hand, stood the words: to no room. For thirty years the keeper copied the song six times a month, carried the brown book nowhere, and the house above him sang.

A woman came in the autumn to tune the old clavichord. She worked with the corridor doors open, and by the third evening she had stopped working to listen, because the shapes came round too deliberately to be decoration. The six-of-a-kind, twice. The single tone that stilled the air of a doorway. The beat of silence, and the wide breaking chord. She asked the newest singer what the flocking meant. He said he had never asked. She asked the keeper. He looked at her the way a man looks at a stair he has decided not to climb; then, because she did not look away, he took her down.

The brown book set each rhyme of the song against a meaning, in the master's cramped hand. Six of a kind, in two rows: the six rooms running one errand. The tone that must not bend: the thing that stays true in every mouth. The held breath before the scattering: the doorway, passed. And on the last page, against the song's closing round, a line she read three times: a telling that keeps its own rules folded inside it, unspoken.

She had gone down expecting a dead secret; she found the house's other half, awake and keeping time. The song had ruled the singers all the more surely for being nameless, and the buried book had loosened its grip on no one. She had watched men serve a dead father's voice before; the voice grows louder the longer the father is in the ground.

She carried one page as far as the top of the stair, and the keeper did not stop her. In the corridor she read it to the newest singer, who stood with his sheet in his hand. When he understood, he asked her to leave the passage. That evening the six rooms sang exactly as before — the six notes of one height, the tone held until the doorway stilled, the silence, the wide chord — and the newest singer varied nothing, in the voice of a man who has looked at something and closed the door on it slowly, from the inside.

The brown book stayed below, for the dead man's word still governed the paper. But that winter the keeper took a pen and added a second line beneath the first, in his own slower hand: — but not to no one.
