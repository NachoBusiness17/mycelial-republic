# The Song and the Slender Book

Maren came down from the pass in the dust season, when the singing house opened its six alcoves to the wall. She had left the town years before as a singer and had walked far enough to lose the habit of walls; the daylight had taken the edge from her sight, and she had paid it gladly, though it made her slow now in bright doorways, shading her eyes like a beggar.

The house was unchanged. Six alcoves faced a long stretch of pale plaster, and lamps behind brass grilles threw the song onto that wall in rows of small marks that climbed and settled, climbed and settled. Six singers sat with their backs to one another and sang the marks as they arrived. They never turned around. Maren asked the tall girl in the third alcove what the marks meant, and the girl laughed and said they meant today's song; she had sung them since she was seven, as her mother had, and it had never occurred to either of them to ask what the marks had been before they were thrown. When Maren asked whose wall it was, the girl said the house's, as if that settled it.

Behind the singing wall ran a corridor, and at its end hung a curtain stitched with the founder's motto: know the trial before you fight it. The town still told how the founder, in her youth, had been given the old trial of the house, the one no singer had ever passed, and had not passed it either. She had instead gone through its rules line by line, shown the judges every place it could not be won, and had the thing rewritten. The house had sung differently ever since. The tall girl told this story the way children tell of a flood.

Behind the curtain, the cantor kept a room with one table, one lamp, and a slender book bound in gray. The book never left the room. This was not guarded as a treasure is guarded; it was simply the arrangement, the way the river is under the bridge. No alcove had ever held it. No singer's hand had ever turned its pages. When the song went out at dusk, the book stayed, and the cantor followed the song with one finger, and where a figure came back on itself, the finger stopped and rested.

Maren asked to read the gray book. The cantor said no — not from cruelty; the book had never gone anywhere the song went, and its one rule was older than both of them. So Maren asked to sit and watch the finger instead. She sat a season of dusks, noted where the finger stopped, and said nothing.

The season's songs were plain. In the second line and again in the third, six marks climbed to the same height, and the six voices, fed from the same thrown shapes, sounded one above the other and came down together on a low round tone the oldest singers called the anchor. Each song was otherwise unlike every other — different in pace, in sadness, in length — but the anchor arrived the same every dusk, and all six struck it, and while they held it the marks on the wall stood still.

Near the end of each song the marks went dark for the length of a breath, an emptiness held on purpose, longer than any singer's habit, and when the voices returned they came scattered, flung wide, the high and the low at once, as though the song had gone through a narrow door and burst on the far side of it. The young singers loved that moment and threw their arms out when it came. They called it the burst. Not one of them asked why the silence had to come first, or what the scatter would become without it.

On the last evening the cantor closed the gray book and said, unasked, that the finger stops where the song speaks of this house — and would say no more. But Maren had watched a season of stops: the six marks at one height, the anchor struck by all, the held breath before the scatter, and the final page, which the finger touched last and longest, after the song was over.

The tall girl asked whether Maren had come back to sing. Maren said she had already been out; the daylight was hers, and it had shown her this room. She stayed by the lamp, between the wall and the book, facing neither, and did not leave.
