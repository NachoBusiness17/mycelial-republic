# The Six Copies

Before the fair, the song-maker wrote one song for six valleys. She copied it six times in her own hand and laid the sheets side by side on the long table, and they were so alike that the messengers could not tell which was which. Each sheet carried the same words and the same small marks above the words, showing which tone to hold and how long. With each copy she sent nothing else — no letter, no word of meaning. The last messenger waited at her door and asked whether there was not more to carry. She said the sheet was the whole errand, and he went up his road.

In the song, two lines were twins. Each climbed the same six steps of the scale and each came down on one plain tone in the middle, the sound a door makes when it settles into its frame. A singer might dress the rest of the song as her valley pleased — flatten this phrase, sharpen that one — but the twins were not hers. Every sheet in every valley carried them exactly, and both landed on the same settled tone, so that whoever heard the song twice heard the same arrival twice.

At midsummer the six singers came down to the fair and sang it together beneath the old arch. Where the twins ended, they held the settled tone, six voices on one sound, and held it. If a single singer leaned, the hum in the stones shivered like water and the song went no further; the arch gave them nothing until the tone stood still. So they held it, breath after breath, and when the hum at last stood — not rising, not falling, only standing — the arch let them through.

Then came one full measure in which nobody sang. The six stood in the quiet with their mouths shut, and when the quiet was spent they broke apart across the scale: one voice climbing high, one sinking low, the others threading between, note set far from note, six journeys widening out of the tone they had held. Whatever a valley changed elsewhere, it kept the quiet before the widening, because that was where the widening began.

In the song-maker's drawer lay a card folded once, in her own hand. On it she had written what each figure was for: at the top, what the whole song itself was — a lesson that could be learned only by being sung; beside the twins, what they carried; beside the tone six voices held, what it proved; beside the standing hum, what it opened; beside the quiet before the widening, what followed it; beside every pair of rhyming line-endings, the thought the pair had been chosen to hold. The card had never gone up any of the six roads.

Men came for the card the way they came for grain. A buyer from the city set a full purse on the table and said he wanted to be the one who knew. She sold him a copy of the song instead, and he found it bare: lovely, singable, and silent about itself, a song that belonged to anyone who learned it and told nothing to anyone. Some years the singers asked her to explain the twins, and she sent them fresh copies. One valley tried to better the twins so that its version would be the true one, and the other valleys did not follow; they kept the six steps and the settled tone as these had come.

She grew old, and the song traveled farther than her name. Every valley changed it somewhere — a word here, a bend there — but the twins held, and the held tone held, and the quiet before the widening held, so that a stranger who heard all six valleys in one summer could mark what never changed and guess that the unchanged parts were the errand. What the errand was, the stranger could not say, and the valleys could not say either, though they sang it.

When she died, they found the card in the drawer, folded once, unsent. It was read aloud in the square at the fair, standing where the arch could hear, and the six valleys learned at last what they had been carrying all those years. At the bottom of the card, in small letters, she had written: sent up no road. They sang the song that evening exactly as before, and afterward the messengers' children asked for copies, and were given copies, and nothing else, as before.
