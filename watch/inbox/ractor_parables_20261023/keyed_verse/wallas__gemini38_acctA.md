# The Six Horns of the Spillway

The master of the weir understood that if you hand six young men a set of hydrostatic tables, they will neglect the culverts out of boredom or sheer perversity; if you threaten them with penalties, their attention will scatter the moment your back is turned. A youth does not hunger for numerical proportion; he hungers for noise, company, and the swing of his own ribs. So the master took six fresh slips of rag paper and copied upon each, in carbon ink, the identical twelve lines of a ballad concerning a pack-horse dying of thirst beside a frozen well. He carried them along the river wall where the water divided into six stone trenches, cut deep into the granite and walled apart so that a workman in one could neither see the face nor touch the hand of his neighbor. At each trench he found an apprentice resting on the gravel with a copper horn across his knees. To each he delivered one slip of paper, directing only that when the tower bell struck, the horn must follow the verses to their end.

Beneath his wool waistcoat, pressed against his ribs, the master kept a narrow strip of shaved sheepskin. It had never been copied, and he would have burned it before allowing it to pass into any of the six trenches. The apprentices supposed the paper in their hands was merely an ancient ballad of the dales, but the sheepskin in the master’s pocket recorded what the verse actually commanded. Upon that strip, each couplet, each vowel repetition, and every rise of the horn was paired to the mechanical action of the floodgates. The rhyming of the second and fourth lines governed the parallel opening of timber valves; the refrain measured the intake of silt. He withheld the parchment not out of malice, but from a psychologist's knowledge of men: people dispute an argument with their intellect, but they surrender to a tune with their blood. A diagram provokes defiance; a rhythm compels the throat.

When the red flag dropped from the tower, the six youths raised their horns. None wished to seem feeble before the companions whose tones carried over the stone partitions, and each grasped the verse with the sharp vanity of youth. In two successive stanzas, the melody called for six rising pitches, identical interval for interval, each youth blowing the same progression through his separate trench. The sound began as six disjointed blasts of brass, discordant with solitary pride. But the appetite for concord is deeper than the impulse to resist. As the phrase climbed through its second repetition, the six divergent tones drew toward one another, gathered by the physical discomfort of discord, until all six horns settled upon the exact same clear, unbending middle pitch.

The moment that single note rang in unbroken unison across all six trenches, the shudder in the weir abruptly ceased. The massive oak beam spanning the breast of the dam did not hold by virtue of statute; it remained locked only so long as that shared pitch remained entirely uniform. If even one horn wavered sharp or flat by a fraction, the timber groaned and water began to fret the lime mortar. But the apprentices, caught in the collective trance of shared breath, held the pitch motionless, each leaning his weight against the sound of his fellows. They did not know they were maintaining the one invariant condition that kept the valley dry; they knew only the animal pleasure of six chests emptying at the identical pace.

Downstream, where the six trenches converged before the narrow throat of the gorge, the current encountered this unbroken wall of acoustic resonance. The river did not surge forward, nor did it fall back. Caught between the vibration of the six matching horns and the granite abutments, the water reared into a stationary crest of pale foam. It hung suspended between the piers, a standing gate of water that neither advanced nor broke, humming with tremendous, poised weight. It was a barrier of pure balance, holding the reservoir at bay solely by the constancy of the air above it.

Then arrived the mark upon the parchment for which every singer waits with an eager throat: an abrupt, total rest. For two full pulse-beats, all six horns cut their sound clean. In that gap of absolute silence, the stationary crest collapsed, and as the apprentices drew breath to sound the following phrase—a broad, leaping arpeggio that sprang across three octaves—the pent-up river plunged through the gorge and divided instantaneously into dozens of foaming channels that fanned across the terrace fields, watering every orchard and mill-wheel in a single sweeping distribution.

When the shift ended, the apprentices climbed out of their trenches, shaking the spit from their horns and boasting of who had sustained the long note with the greatest vigor. The master walked past them without praise or reprimand, his fingers touching the pocket where the unread sheepskin rested against his coat. He had not compelled their labor; he had merely cut a channel along which their natural appetites were glad to run.
