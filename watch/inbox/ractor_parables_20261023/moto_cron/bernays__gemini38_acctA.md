# The Echo at the Wicket

The overseer chose the boy not because he was whole, but because he was already broken in a way that cost nothing. In his pocket he carried a square looking-glass whose surface had crazed into a dense spider-web of hairline fractures. Most of the glass was opaque gray, yet through a narrow wedge near the brass rim, a sliver of mercury still mirrored whatever passed before it. The boy did not demand a horse or a velvet livery; he was assigned only the trivial errands—fetching salt from the kiln, carrying bundles of reeds, running small notices to the outlying farms.

In the high hall of the manor, the overseer established the machinery of oversight. Upon a long table of polished timber rested a partitioned cedar box with an open slot. Beside it waited a clerk who neither spoke nor looked up from his work. Whenever a tenant dropped a request into the slot, the clerk copied the lines into an unrolled strip of parchment, then pivoted two painted wooden markers on the desk—one red, one bone-white—to signal whether the errand was waiting or in motion. The patrons who visited the manor looked at the neat slots, the gliding pen, and the upright markers, and they departed with complete confidence that their affairs were handled.

To demonstrate that the broken-glass boy was bound to the house, the overseer ran a private line of braided silk across the ravine, anchored from the manor balcony to the boy’s shack on the ridge. Once an hour, before the gathered elders, the overseer lifted a silver hammer and struck the line. Through the taut thread, a faint vibration traveled outward into the pines and returned—an answering tremor that took exactly one hundred and fifteen pulses of a man's wrist to come back. The elders counted the beats, heard the soft metallic hum, and nodded in unison. The line was alive; the boy was awake; the enterprise seemed justified. The echo provided the exact proof the gathering wished to possess, because an echo demands no understanding—only the reassurance that distance has answered.

Yet when the steward walked to the perimeter wall to pass the day’s packets through the small north wicket—the low door marked eighty-twenty-two—the bolt remained thrown. The boy was inside, his shadow falling across the threshold, but he would not unlatch the timber. The steward held up the manor's heavy bronze signet, engraved with the master's public crest, and pressed it against the iron receiving plate beside the keyway. The lock shuddered, but the tumblers did not drop. A flat click followed: the inner mechanism refused the cut of the crest, denying entry to the bearer.

The steward returned to the high hall and laid the unopened packets back upon the cedar table. "The silk line hums," the steward reported, "and the boy counts our strikes across the gorge, but the wicket refuses our seal. Not an ounce of salt, not a single syllable of ink has crossed from this room to his." The line was unbroken through the air, yet the errand was entirely stopped upon the ground. The connection existed as an acoustic fact, but as an avenue of dispatch, it was an empty theater.

The overseer’s first thought was to summon a smith to force the wicket or carve the boy's wooden registry board to accept the manor's mark. Yet the house advocate barred the way with the founding parchment. The covenant governing the outpost was rigid: the boy's private tablet of accepted signets was never to be rewritten or touched by the manor's officers without formal warrant. To alter his gate by decree would breach the covenant that kept his post autonomous and low-cost. The lock was required to stay precisely as it stood.

So the overseer turned his attention to what could be displayed rather than what could be completed. In the long ante-room through which every visitor passed, he ordered long trestles brought in from the courtyard. Upon these tables, the clerks arranged every drafted instruction, every itinerary, and every bundle of tasks in clean, numbered rows, held flat beneath square glass paperweights. The ante-room became an exhibition of readiness. Whoever entered saw the arrayed bundles and concluded that the work was already surging through the hills. The overseer knew that men believe the version of an enterprise that flatters their desire for order; the accepted story cropped the bolted wicket and the useless crest, while the dispatches rested on the trestles, immaculate and unmoved.
