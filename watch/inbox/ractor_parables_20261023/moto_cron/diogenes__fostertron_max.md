# The Clouded Eye and the Wax

On the far side of the gorge lived a mule called Moss, and one of his eyes had gone white as a boiled egg. Nothing came through it any more. The villagers had once read that eye for temper and for weather; now it showed them nothing at all, and they stopped looking. But his legs were honest. At first light he stood at the well, unled. At noon he stood at the mill. At dusk he stood at the ferry steps. No one drove him; the round walked itself in him, and at each stop the villagers set a small load on his back — a sack, a pail, a bundle of sticks — and he carried it to the next. A beast whose face is ruined can still be trusted with a small load at a known hour, over and over. His eye was gone; his habit was not.

On the near side lived a carter who wanted that round to carry his goods too. Since he could not be everywhere, he cut a slot into the post at the bridgehead and dropped slips through it, one slip to an errand. Beside the post he set a boy named Ivo on a stool with one duty: when a slip fell, read it once, then lay a pebble on it. The pebble said, this one is taken; it will not be read again. Some mornings brought three slips, some none; Ivo's pebbles never lied and never doubled.

Between the houses lay the gorge. Each morning the carter walked to the edge and whistled four notes, and before a sparrow could beat its wings twice, four notes came back. This delighted him, and he began to time it, morning after morning, finer and finer. Yes: the air over the gorge was open, quick in both directions. He told the village the road was open. Even the village dogs knew better; they barked at the echo once and then slept.

Sound was the only thing crossing. When the carter wanted his slips to become loads, he filled baskets and sent his brother's boy over the bridge. At the far gate sat a warden, an old woman with a wax tablet. She was not unkind and she did not argue. She held the tablet out. The boy said the carter's name — loudly at first, then carefully, then slowly, as to a dull listener — and she did not stir, for her ears had been closed to names by the founding word of her house. She wanted one thing only: a mark pressed into the wax, cut from the very hand that filled the basket. "Names are wind," she said, gently. "Press the wax." The boy carried only his voice. She turned him back each time, and the gate stood shut, and behind it the mule walked his round with none of the carter's goods on his back.

The founding word bound the carter too: the gate stands as it was hung; the warden shall not be argued with, replaced, nor taught to hear; no second door shall be cut through the wall, for as long as the true mark has not been carried to her. So the carter did not pry the gate, did not oil the hinge, did not send bread to the warden or a louder boy. That restraint was the one thing he did not dress up.

The baskets went into the shed at the bridgehead instead, stacked in order, each labeled in his own hand. None crossed. None was burned. The shed grew, and the round on the far side kept walking without them; Moss drew the water at first light whether the carter's goods arrived or not.

Now I lift the lantern here, carter — you, at the near house, timing your echo again this evening. You whistle louder each season. You write longer slips, warm letters commending your own name, to be read aloud to a woman deaf to names since before your mother was born. You call the road open because the air answers you. The air was never the question; the wax is the question. You are asking the gate to want what you happen to carry — a voice — when it has told you plainly, every day, what it is built to receive. Stop asking the gorge to recognize you. The mule does not recognize you either, and he would carry for you anyway, because carrying is what his legs are for. Be like the mule: bring the thing the door is made to take, or stack your baskets and say to the village, "The road is shut; my goods wait; the far round walks without me." Your part of the errand is the only part that is late.

The pebbles were the truest things on the near side of the gorge. Each one said: this was asked for once, and once was enough. The carter could have learned from his own pebbles.
