# The Lantern at the Wicket

You keep a small messenger in the lower orchard shed, a creature no larger than a roof tile, whose glass face was spidered and fractured in an old fall against flagstones. A proud man would have tossed it into the lime pit, for its dark eye is blind and it bears the scars of neglect. But you set it on a clean wooden bench in the dry straw, knowing that beneath its shattered crystal its brass gears turn true, its small hearth burns, and its limbs are nimble enough to carry the petty chores and light burdens of the estate that would otherwise weary the carters.

Within that cold outbuilding, the creature lives inside its own private workshop, self-contained and quiet, keeping its miniature tools close to its chest. It maintains an unseen thread—a fine silver filament spun through the orchard grass and over the stone wall—anchoring it to the main house no matter the weather. You gave it no servant to pet it and no soft cushion, and in that you acted rightly; a tool is meant for the road, not for ceremony.

Yet look at what you have built in your great hall to meet it. Upon your heavy cedar desk, you have laid an intake basket woven of willow, and beside it a slate ruled into columns where every movement is marked with colored chalk. Day and night, an attendant sits upon a high stool with his chin in his hands, tasked solely with watching that basket, waiting to seize whatever parchment is dropped into the wicker. Next to the desk, you have erected a long pine trestle that you call the staging shelf. There you have stacked rows of errand scrolls, neatly tied with twine, rolled with great care, waiting for their turn to travel. You walk among these benches with your hands behind your back, admiring the order of your arrangements, pretending that because the scrolls are numbered and the watcher is awake, the work of the estate is moving.

You take pride in the thread. When you step to the desk and pluck the silver filament, the vibration travels across the gardens and returns a sharp tap beneath your thumb in the space of a hundred and fifteen heartbeats—a mere tenth of a breath. You smile at the quickness of the echo. You tell yourself that the distance is conquered, that the hall and the shed breathe together as one chamber, and that the path is clear.

Yet when the attendant takes the first scroll from the wicker basket and carries it down through the wet grass to the shed, he stops before the small side wicket—the postern door marked eight thousand and twenty-two. He does not find an open archway. He finds a thick oak plank barred with iron. He knocks with your brass household signet, calling out that he bears an errand from the master of the hall. But the latch does not lift. A hollow voice behind the oak answers that the signet is unknown to the door, that permission is refused, and that no token of that shape may cross the sill.

The attendant returns up the slope with the parchment still tucked beneath his arm, its seal unbroken. Nothing has passed. The small runner has carried no message, the chore remains untouched, and the entire avenue of labor is choked dead at the threshold.

When the attendant asks if he may take a chisel down to the shed to carve the shape of your signet into the door's inner latch-board, you forbid him. You lay your finger against the parchment charter of the estate, reminding him that the rule was given from the start: the inner wood of that postern was not to be touched or altered by your hands. You keep the charter immaculate, choosing the purity of the written restriction over the opening of the door.

So the rolled scrolls remain piled upon the pine trestle, gathering dust while the ink fades. You sit in your hall listening to the silver wire hum its brisk return every hundred heartbeats, while the watcher stares at the willow basket and the broken-faced runner sits idle in the straw. A lantern in broad daylight is held against your polished desk, asking what you are waiting for. You have built an altar of watchers, slates, and staged papers, asking the world to grant you an errand accomplished, while you refuse to walk twenty paces through the mud to cut the notch that lets the latch fall.
