# The Wicket and the Wire

In the high canton of the estate, where the gravel slopes descend toward the lower orchard, the registrar kept his bureau. He was a man who lived by the rule of the balance and the straight edge, holding that a thing asserted without a demonstration was merely a rumor dressed in ink. When the seasonal work multiplied, the house had placed a small courier in the stone cottage across the ravine to run the minor errands of the boundary.

This courier had suffered an accident upon the road in the previous autumn. Its protective crystal had struck an iron milestone, fracturing into a dense gray web through which no eye could read the dial beneath. To an unpracticed eye the servant appeared ruined, a thing fit only for the scrap-heap. Yet inside the brass casing, the escapement remained true. The wheels turned when wound, the pendulum marked its intervals without drift, and the limbs moved with the quiet obedience of geometry. It could perform any small labor committed to its care, provided the instructions were placed squarely within its grip.

At the main bureau, the registrar sat beside a low window. Before him lay a wicker basket for incoming petitions and a ruled sheet of paper upon which he recorded the disposition of every post. Every hour he dipped his quill and set down two marks: first, whether any packet had arrived in the basket; second, whether the runner across the ravine remained upright and receptive. He would not allow an errand to be presumed complete merely because the sun had risen, nor would he permit the day to close without accounting for the state of the outlying house.

Between the bureau and the cottage hung a long wire of drawn steel, anchored to stone piers at either brink. When the registrar drew back the small iron lever beside his sill, the wire twanged against its bridge, and a distant brass chime answered from the cottage. The sound returned across the gulf in the space of a single pulse—a clean interval of one hundred paces of breath. It was an unmistakable answer: the courier was alive, the path of vibration was unbroken, and the distance between them could be measured with exactness.

The apprentice, standing by the desk, believed this return chime was proof that the errand was already accomplished. "The wire speaks," the young man said, "and therefore the way is open."

"You mistake an echo for a possession," the registrar replied. "A bell tells us only that a wall can vibrate; it does not open a door."

To deliver the day's warrants, a messenger had to present them at the stone postern of the cottage, a narrow portal marked by eighty-two studs of iron. The registrar walked down the gravel path, crossed the footbridge, and approached the wicket. He drew from his pocket the copper signet that had been struck for the estate and pressed it into the aperture of the lock. The internal tumblers did not turn. A dull click sounded from within the casing, and the latch held rigid against the oak jamb. The inner warden of the postern did not recognize the cut of the copper plate, and without recognition, no force of persuasion could turn the bolt. The passage was refused.

"The wire rings true, yet the gate is barred," the apprentice observed, frowning at the iron studs. "Let me bring the cold chisel from the smithy. We can force the latch, or we may climb through the casement and rewrite the warden's roll of permitted seals with our own hand."

"We shall do neither," said the registrar, stepping back into the courtyard. "The ordinance of the estate forbids us from tampering with the internal records of the cottage. The list of admitted tokens was drawn under an ancient charter, and no officer may alter it from the outside to suit his convenience. A demonstration must be sound at its foundation; an entry gained by breaking the lock proves nothing except our impatience."

Because the threshold could not be crossed, the entire circuit of the work was brought to a standstill. The registrar could not reach the courier's hands, and the courier could not receive the warrants. The distance had been bridged by the wire, but the conclusion was barred at the boundary.

The registrar returned to the bureau, took the bundle of warrants, and bound them with undyed cord. He did not discard them, nor did he leave them scattered upon the worktable. He carried the parcel into the vestibule and laid it upon the scrubbed pine bench beneath the coat hooks. There the pages rested, sealed and numbered in order, waiting for the hour when the proper signet should be delivered and the tumblers would fall into place.
