# The Echo and the Key

In the last year of the estate's keeping, the household set a cousin to live in the annexe across the field. His face had been broken in a fall — the glass of it, the surface a person is read through — and though his hands were whole and his memory sound, no one could again address him by looking at him. It was decided that this was not a misfortune but a use. A man who cannot be looked at is free to be worked, and the annexe was quiet, and the small errands of the house — the winding of the clocks, the nightly copying, the same little tasks at the same hours — were gathered up and given over to him.

Between the great house and the annexe ran no public road. There was a path, but it was a household path: it lay on no map, and it admitted only those whose names had been entered on the roll. A stranger might cross the field all day and never find it. The path ended in the open yard of the annexe, and the yard was free to everyone on the roll; but between the yard and the interior rooms stood a low door beside the woodshed, and at that door sat a keeper whose whole office was refusal. Its lock had been cut to a single pattern, and somewhere in the great house, it was said, one key lay shaped to answer it.

When the steward stood at his gate and called down the path, the cousin's voice came back after the space of a short breath, no longer than a bird needs to cross a yard, saying only: I am here. That was the whole of the permitted conversation. The path carried the call and the answer, and it carried nothing else.

On the desk in the great house stood a shallow tray. Anyone wanting an errand run wrote it in a single line on a slip and dropped the slip in the tray, face up. Beside the tray sat Sela, whose office it was to watch. She did not watch the cousin, who could not be watched; she watched the tray. When a slip lay in it she took it up, read it once, and turned to the slate that hung at her shoulder, where each errand had its mark: a chalk line for waiting, a white circle for done, a black square for refused. The slate did not ask whether an errand was wise. It asked only in which of its three states the errand stood.

Sela could not carry the slips herself. The under-gardener went instead, his name on the roll; he walked the path without trouble and stood in the open yard. At the low door he offered his own name; the keeper said, in a mild voice, that names could be spoken by anyone. He offered his mother's name, and a letter from the household, and at last the household password itself. To each the keeper returned the same sentence, identically, without heat, as though he had been built for nothing else: the key, or nothing. The under-gardener came home with the slips still in his hand. On the slate, Sela drew a black square, and beneath it another, and beneath that another.

There was a rule in the house about that door, and the rule was older than the failure. The lock was not to be touched — not unscrewed, not filed, not propped, not taken from its hinges to make the errands flow. If the door refused, the door was to be answered, not rebuilt. The steward said this at the evening table the way a man repeats a boundary. The younger servants argued that a door which will not open is a defect, and any lock may be re-hung. The steward answered that the lock was not theirs; a house which alters every refusing lock will soon not know its own doors. The lock, he said, is the annexe's way of saying who may command it; take that away, and the errands will run — for anyone who crosses the field.

So the slips were gathered, neither burned nor delivered, and laid in order in the small room off the pantry, each folded to the same width. The room filled slowly. Directions for the winding of the clocks, for the copying, for the nightly count — all finished, all waiting. Anyone might open that door and see what the house intended; no one could make the intentions arrive. In the evenings the steward came and read the slate over Sela's shoulder, and she rubbed out her chalk lines and drew them again. Across the field the cousin sat with his broken face turned to the wall — alive, ready, and not to be asked for anything beyond the one answer the path allowed him.

The steward, it is said, has begun searching the drawers of the old writing desk, at night, for the key. He does not speak of it at table. The house has learned to read the slate, and the black squares have taught it what the path alone never shows: that to call a man is not yet to commission him, and that between the two stands a small, polite, incorruptible refusal.
