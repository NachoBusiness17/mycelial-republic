# The Mouth of the Path

Esther's kitchen had a slot in its west wall, and a stool beside the slot, and on the stool a girl called Pell who did nothing all day but mind the two. When Esther had work for the far side of the valley, she wrote it small on a paper slip and fed it through. Pell drew the slip out, read it aloud once in a flat voice — saying what was written, never what was meant — and copied it as a single ruled line into the long book chained to her table. Then she turned a pebble in the cup at her elbow, pale side up, and the pebble said: given, not yet taken. Esther had learned not to reach for the slips again. Whatever went through the slot belonged to the slot.

The work itself was meant for her cousin in the hollow beyond the ridge, a man nobody had seen in years. A fall had shattered the glass of his face long ago, and nothing had crossed it since — no smile, no frown, no appeal. But his hands had kept their habits, and the habits kept their hours. At the first bell he drew water whether anyone had asked. At the noon bell he fed the fire. At the last bell he banked it and latched the flues. No one alive remembered instructing him. The hours themselves were the instruction, and he was the sort of man who could not be late to himself.

There was one way to him: a smugglers' path under the ridge, too narrow for a cart, its mouth hidden in the gorse so that only family could find it. Each morning Esther walked down, stood at the mouth, and called. A bell in the hollow answered — it always answered, though the sound needed a slow heartbeat to come back over the ridge. That much the path granted her, and she came to distrust what it granted. The bell said only: the path is open, the hollow hears. It did not say the hollow would open.

For the door at the far end had one law. It would swing for a token cut with the family's notches, and for nothing else. Esther had a token; she had cut it herself, at home, by lamplight, sure of her eye. When she pushed it into the door's slot, the door drew it in, weighed every notch in the dark, and handed it back with one flat sentence: No notch of mine recognizes this. The door said nothing else, ever, to anyone.

She went home furious and called the door broken. Pell turned the newest pebble over, dark side up, and said without lifting her ruling pen: "It isn't broken. It read you. Reading you and agreeing with you were always two different acts." Esther did not want to hear that a refusal was a reading. She wanted the door to have failed, because a failed door could be mended by force.

A tinker came through that autumn and offered, for a coin, to climb the wall or pry the plate from the door and file its wards to fit her token — an hour's work, he said, and the door as good as hers. Esther stood at the mouth of the path with the coin in her fist and did not hand it over. The family's standing instruction was short and old: the door stays exactly as the maker hung it; no ward filed, no hinge lifted, not while the path is to remain a family path. The tinker shrugged and moved on to easier villages.

So the chores piled. She sewed each slip into oilcloth and stacked the bundles in the lean-to at the mouth of the path, in the order they should be done, the first at the front. Nothing crossed the ridge. Pell's book filled its pages, one line to a chore, and the cup filled with pale stones. Some mornings Esther counted the bundles and the pale stones and found they agreed exactly; agreeing exactly was all they did.

In winter she took the ferry across the water to the old carver and laid her token on his bench. He turned it under the lamp, twice, and said the fault was not mysterious: cut again, in the right wood, to the pattern the family's doors actually kept — for the notch that opens a door is not the notch you meant to cut; it is the notch the door knows. He would not go near the door. He would recut the token, and the door, being unaltered, would still be standing to meet it.

She came home over the ice with the old token at her throat and the new one promised. That night, at the last bell — she could not hear it across the ridge, but she knew its hour — the cousin banked his fire and latched his flues, instructed by nobody, on time. In the morning the bell answered her call, one slow heartbeat late. The lean-to stood full and in order. Esther stood at the mouth of the path with two carvings in her hand, one failed and one promised, and understood that until the notches met, every chore she owed the hollow was a paper lying closed on its shelf — sent, heard, and not yet read.
