# The House That Answers but Does Not Open

At the edge of the marsh stood a grey cottage where the small work of the estate was meant to happen — the sweeping, the winding of the clocks, the copying of lists too dull for the great house. The work had been given to a servant who lived there alone. He had once had a face of glass; a fall had starred it with cracks, and after the fall no one could speak to him eye to eye. The glass had been his only way of being spoken to. What lay beneath the cracks still worked — his hands, his memory, his patience — but no one had yet found the way in to reach them.

The great house kept a desk for him. Anyone with a chore wrote it on a slip and fed the slip through a brass slot, and each slip fell onto a single long ribbon that coiled in a box below the desk, one chore after another, nothing ever unwritten. Beside the slot sat a watchman whose whole duty was the ribbon and a board of small painted pegs: when a chore went out, he turned a peg; when it came back, another; when it died in the dark, a black one. The watchman had one older duty still. Every so often he walked to the end of the causeway and called across the marsh — one flat syllable — and waited.

Between the houses ran a private causeway laid under the water, built long ago by the founder so that the estate's feet would never touch the public roads. No one walked it uninvited. And the causeway answered. The syllable went out and an echo came home — late, always a little late, a slow breath late, as though the sound had to stoop under the marsh — but it came home every time. On this the whole estate agreed: the road is alive, and the far house is not deaf.

But a road is not a door. When the scribe finally went out to bring the servant his first chores, she knocked at the low door of the grey cottage. A voice came through the wood, quiet and without anger. "Show me your hand." She laid her palm flat against the grain. A pause. "This hand was never given to me," the voice said. "Inside this door I keep impressions in wax, every hand the house ever promised to know. Yours is not among them. I cannot open."

She offered the household word instead — the old word that opens gates in other parts of the estate. The voice did not soften. "I do not trade in words. Only in hands. Bring the hand that was promised, or have your hand pressed into the wax by someone standing already inside. Those are the two ways, and there is no third."

She thought for a moment of melting the wax herself, of lifting the keeper from his stool. But she carried the founder's letter, and it was plain: the door's memory belongs to the house; no one from outside shall carve, melt, or recast the wax, and no one shall pull the keeper down; the cottage has taken its harm once and will not take it twice for anyone's convenience. She read the letter twice by the water's edge and put it away.

So she did what could still be done. Behind the great house stood a lean-to shed, and into it she carried every prepared chore, sealed and numbered in the order of the ribbon, ready for the day the door would know her. Nothing crossed the marsh after that except the watchman's syllable and its late echo. The ribbon grew. The pegs turned from waiting to waiting. The bundles kept their numbers in the dark.

In the evenings the scribe sat with her own hand open on her knee and would not blame the door. The road had never refused her; the keeper had never refused her. What had been refused was smaller and nearer: a promise made and never kept — her hand's impression never carried in, never left in the wax — so that to that house she was only weather knocking. She wrote this on a slip and fed it through the slot with the rest. The watchman would find it in the morning and turn no peg, because it was not a chore. It was the day's account: the house answers, the house does not open, and both of those are honest; the fault lies folded in her own sleeve, waiting to be carried across.
