# The Glass-Faced Keeper

The village of Ferrow kept one servant it never saw. He lived across the valley in a small stone hut at the end of a road the villagers had cut themselves, a road worn so narrow and turned so deliberately that a stranger could walk the valley for a lifetime and never find where it began. Only Ferrow's own people could walk it, and only they knew the hut was there.

The keeper had been hurt on that road years before. His face was cracked glass; nothing could be read in it anymore — no anger, no welcome, nothing. But his hands were strong, and at night, it was said, they worked at small tasks the village had no daylight for: winding, sorting, stirring, mending. No one had watched him do these things. The hut's one window was curtained. What the village had instead was the knock.

Each morning the courier Wren walked the hidden road and knocked twice on the hut door. The answer always came. It came in the space of a dropped stone's first bounce — one breath, no more — a double knock from inside, firm and even. That pause was how Ferrow knew its servant was awake. Wren had walked the road in rain and in frost, and the pause had never lengthened and never shortened, and he had come to trust it more than he had ever trusted the face, which had told no one anything, even before it broke.

The door, though, did not open. It had no latch-string, no bell, no wicket. It was made to answer one key — a single iron cut for a single hand — and no such iron hung on Ferrow's ring. Wren had tried every key the village owned, cold mornings, one after another, pressing each into the lock the way a foot tests ice. The door never refused him aloud. It stood, and stood alike for the reeve's key, the smith's key, the church key, and the small key that had once opened a jewelry box no one could anymore find. A door that says nothing says the same thing to everyone.

The council asked the mason to file the lock, or to cut a fresh notch so an ordinary key might turn it. The mason walked out once with his files, looked a long while at the lock, at the deed-box in his shed, and at the door, then came home and put his tools away. The lock stayed exactly as it had been made, and he never spoke of why, and no one made him.

So the village changed what it could reach, which was its own side of the valley. They cut a slot in the post at the road's mouth, and any villager with a task for the keeper wrote it on a slip and slid it through. Beside the slot they set a bench, and on the bench a clerk who watched from first light to last. On a slate nailed above the slot the clerk kept his marks: one row for waiting, one for carried, one for done. He read the rows before he read anything else in the morning, even his own name.

The slips did not shrink the tasks; they shrank the writers. Wren noticed it in himself first. His errands had once been speeches — he would have told the keeper everything, the why, the when, the worry of it. Now each one came out the size of the slot, and the slot was narrow, and what did not fit did not go. The villagers, who had rarely written anything before the slot was cut, now wrote constantly, in hands grown small and even, and if you asked one of them to say an errand aloud, he hesitated, as if asked to sing.

In the shed by the road's mouth the real work lay under canvas: bundles oiled and wrapped and numbered, the winding-tools and the sorting-rakes meant for the keeper's strong hands. Once a week the clerk unwrapped them, wiped them, wrapped them again. None had ever been carried up the road. They were to go through the door or not at all.

Evenings, Wren tried the ring once more, key by key, and then knocked, and the answer came in the space of a dropped stone, exactly as it had come that morning and every morning before it. Then he walked home in the dark. The smith, meanwhile, had taken a wax casting of the lock's mouth and sat late over it with his gravers, learning its notches one by one, the way a man learns a face he has never seen.

The road had never carried a single errand. The knock had never once failed.
