# The Dry Niche Under the Last Arch

Alda kept the tray by the quay-house door. Any neighbor might drop a folded paper in it — a chore that wanted doing again and again at set hours: the bell rung at six, the clock wound at nine, the steps salted before the first frost. Beside the tray sat the tally-man. Each paper that arrived got a chalk mark on his board, and the board was meant to turn from white marks to red ones as the chores were carried off and done.

The chores were not done in town. They were done in a squat tower at the end of a long causeway, and the causeway was on no map of the coast, for it showed itself only to those whose names had been read onto the roll. Inside the tower, they said, was a workshop the size of a cupboard, and in the workshop a keeper whose single lamp had a cracked pane, fogged and veined, so that nothing showed through it either way. The keeper worked by touch and by the town bells. The little works went round inside whether or not the glass ever cleared.

When Alda walked out she called at the third stone, and the tower called back. The answer came late — she could count ten heartbeats before it arrived, whoever called, every time — but it came every time, and by this she knew the stones were whole under her feet.

At the tower's foot stood a door with a narrow slot, and the slot had been taught, on the day the door was hung, to know certain tokens of pale wood by the pattern cut into them. Alda had carved her token as well as she could guess. However she turned it in the slot, the door gave back one flat sentence, the same every night: Not to you.

Before she had ever set out, the one who owned the tower had given her a single instruction: the door's iron is not yours to change. Teach the slot nothing from across the water. Pry nothing. So she left the iron exactly as it stood, night after night, though her arms ached to be useful.

What she could do was prepare. She wrote out every chore fair, tied each bundle with string, the whole winter's work of the tower, ready to carry in a single trip. And since the door would not have her, she laid the bundles in the dry niche under the last arch of the causeway, where the rain could not reach — as close to the tower as she was permitted, and no closer.

Back in the quay-house, the younger clerks would hear the far call come back across the water and write on the wall slate, The tower is with us tonight. The tally-man rubbed the words out and wrote: The road answers. The door has not spoken. Most of them had never stood at the door itself. To them the echo and the welcome were one sound, because they had only ever heard the one.

Two voices rode home with Alda in the evenings. One said: a door that will not learn you is not worth your winters; walk away and let the tower keep its own hours. The other said: put your shoulder to it, break the fogged pane, climb in, and hang a door of your own making. She gave each its hearing and neither its way. The first voice had never looked at the door, and neither had the second; both were guessing at a thing that stood in plain sight one street from her own bed. She would not knock louder tomorrow merely because she had knocked tonight — the door was indifferent to loudness, and her knuckles were not. When she moved, it would be after standing at the slot in daylight, reading what the door had been taught, and changing only what was hers to change.

On the evening slate she kept two lines and only two: The road answers, slow and whole. The door knows us not. Between those two lines all the winter's work lay waiting in the niche, dry and patient, while the tray by the quay-house door grew tall with papers and the tally-man's chalk stayed one color.

And far out past the last arch, behind the blind pane, the lamp kept its hours all the same — lit at six, out at nine, faithful, unseen.
