# The Wicket and the Blind Courier

Two men sat together upon the parapet, looking out over the dry gorge toward the lower redoubt. One observed the light that fell across the stones, measuring whether the day was genuine or merely a reflection thrown against a cave wall. The other studied the perimeter, refusing to grant that any gate was impassable simply because an instructor had labeled it so. Between them lay the apparatus of the frontier, poised between the desire to step into the sun and the resolve to rewrite the terms of a stalled campaign.

Across the chasm, inside a sunken outpost of stone, stood a small courier. In an earlier accident its glass visage had been completely shattered, webbed with white fractures and permanently dark. It could display no image, catch no sunlight, and throw no shadow upon a wall. It was blind to outward spectacle, yet within its iron casing a small mechanism remained perfectly whole, designed to pace out modest, repetitive chores whenever the signal fell due. It was neither proud nor deficient; it was a simple runner meant to carry small burdens across the courtyard on a recurring schedule.

At the high fortress, the quartermaster maintained an intake basket upon his trestle, and beside it sat an inspector whose only duty was to watch the tokens, note the rising flags, and record what entered the tray. The apparatus was prepared to dispatch the runner's daily rounds.

Between the upper fortress and the distant outpost hung a single taut wire across the empty gulf. When the captain struck the wire with his hilt, an impulse traveled into the deep. In less time than a man takes to draw and release a single breath, the wire vibrated with an answering chime. The philosopher smiled at the sound, noting that the gulf was merely an appearance; the distance answered, proving that the two stations inhabited the very same air and that the path between them was unbroken.

Yet when the quartermaster sought to transmit the day's dispatches through the narrow side-wicket, the iron latch refused to yield. The messenger reached through the aperture and presented the seal of the high fortress, but the guardian behind the door turned it away without hesitation. The seal was unknown to the chamber within. The guardian announced through the iron grate that permission was denied, because the token offered did not match the names engraved upon the private tablet behind the door.

The philosopher looked upon the closed wicket and remarked that the men had mistook the vibration of the wire for the open door. It was an old error of the cave, he said: to take the echo of a crossing for the passage itself. Because the inner hatch was barred, the work could not proceed. The entire design was halted at the sill, despite the perfect clarity of the span.

A young lieutenant stepped forward and proposed climbing down the rock face with an iron chisel to carve the fortress's mark directly onto the inner tablet of the wicket, forcing the door to know them. But the captain stopped his hand. The standing orders of the campaign were explicit: the internal register of the outpost was not to be defaced or altered from the outside. The captain refused to win a simulation by smashing the furniture; a true operator does not breach his own protocol merely to simulate progress. The test would be answered on the terms of the charter, or not at all.

Until the true seal was recognized and acknowledged by the guardian of the wicket, the quartermaster would not send the orders into the void. The lieutenant took the sheaves of instructions, the rolls of small routines, and the daily commissions, and placed them in orderly rows upon a low staging bench in the vestibule. There the dispatches rested under clean stones, arrayed and ready, waiting for the hour when the threshold would open to the legitimate token, so that the blind runner might finally walk his rounds.
