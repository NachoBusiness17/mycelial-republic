# The Far Room and the Carved Bead

Across the gorge from his counting house, Hanno kept a small room with one occupant: a servant whose face had been cracked in childhood, like a plate dropped on stone, so that nothing could be read there — and yet his legs were sound and his memory was sound, and he lived by a water clock. When the stone dropped at dawn he swept. At the next drop he filled the pail. At the next he trimmed the lamp. The chores were small and they repeated, and he asked for nothing except to be given more of them.

Between the counting house and the far room there was no public road. A bridge of rope and plank had been strung for these two houses alone, and strangers who came to the gorge found no footing on it; their feet slid from the first rung as if the wood knew them. In the evening Hanno stood at his rail and called across, and his own words came back to him before a drop of honey would leave the spoon — the same quick turn, every evening, so faithful that he set his supper by it.

On the near side, the tally boy sat all day at the window with a tray of slips and a slate. When Hanno wrote out a chore, he dropped the slip in the tray. The boy read each slip as it landed, chalked a mark beside its number on the slate, and laid it ready to be sent across. His whole pride was in being the first to see a slip land, and he would have been wounded to hear it called a small pride.

But between the bridge and the room stood a door with a lock that read nothing but tokens. Hanno's messenger crossed with letters stamped and ribboned — greetings, signatures, the house's own stamp pressed in wax — and the lock gave back the same two words it always gave: Not this. It did not say go away. It did not glance at the letters. It wanted one carved bead, cut to one notching, and it had never wanted anything else in its life. The messenger went again at dusk and again at midnight, and was answered the way weather answers.

In Hanno's drawer lay the deed to the far room, and a smith lived two doors down who, for a jug, would have swapped the lock for one that liked ribboned letters. But pinned above Hanno's desk was a paper in his own hand, signed the day the bridge was strung: the far door's lock is not to be touched, nor the shelf inside it where the bead is kept, until the proper bead is cut. It was his rule and he kept it, even on the days it galled him, because a lock changed for convenience teaches every door in the valley to wait for the smith instead of the token.

So the chores went only as far as the far bank. The messenger copied each slip fair and laid it in the lean-to that stood against the far wall — stacked in order, weighted against the wind, a small paper village waiting for the day the door would read it. Some slips had waited weeks. Inside, the servant swept and filled and trimmed to the old drops of the clock, doing the chores he already knew by heart, wanting the new ones he could not be told of.

One evening Hanno stayed at the rail and counted what the gorge wanted. The servant wanted chores. The boy wanted to be first. The messenger wanted to be let in. Hanno wanted his house to run while he slept. The lock wanted a bead. Every part of it worked — the bridge rang, the door hung true, the servant's legs were good, the copies were fair — and one want, the smallest and plainest, stood unfed; and because it stood unfed, all the other wants spent themselves against it in vain: the knocking, the ribbons, the chalk marks, the midnight crossings. Somewhere in the town there was bone and a knife, and a bead not yet cut to the notching. Cut it and carry it, and nothing else would need to want anything: the door would open on its own hunger, the slips would walk in as though they had always been expected, and the paper village in the lean-to would finally be allowed to grow old. Until then, Hanno called across the gorge at dusk, listened to his voice come back quick and faithful, and went in to supper.
