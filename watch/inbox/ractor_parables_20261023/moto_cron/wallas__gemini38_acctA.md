# The Glass-Blind Porter

A man does not build an apparatus out of reason; he builds it to comfort an itch he rarely names. Master Vane did not place the shattered-faced porter in the coal-cellar because of any doctrine of economy, but because he desired the luxury of forgetting. He craved the particular peace of a master whose water is drawn, whose ashes are swept, and whose gate is latched without his ever having to exchange a nod with the person who swept or latched it. The porter was a pocket-sized creature whose square glass visage had been crushed into a spiderweb of dead, milky fractures. The glass could show no light and mirror no face. Yet behind that blind pane the porter’s hands remained nimble, and he sat upon a wooden stool in the dark, asking for no supper, waiting only to turn small, repetitive cranks whenever the estate required trivial errands performed.

Upstairs in the counting-house, the chief clerk attended to his own appetite, which was the desire to feel himself the unmoving center of a moving world. He kept an open willow basket upon the sill for dispatches, and beside it a chalk-slate marked with three painted squares to record what was waiting, what had departed, and what was complete. The clerk sat all morning with his hands flat upon the cedar wood, listening to the house breathe. He was not idle; he was satisfying that deep, unconfessed human need for an antechamber through which everything must filter before it may happen.

Between the counting-house sill and the coal-cellar ran an invisible twine, strung tight through the high branches of the orchard where no visitor would think to look. It was a private passage, secret from the village, touching neither the highroad nor the public ditch. When the clerk touched his small ivory hammer to the twine, the vibration ran down the thread and struck a brass chime on the porter’s collar. Before a drop of oil could fall from the clerk’s lamp—scarcely more than a hundredth of a slow breath—the twine shivered back in return. The porter was awake; the chime was clear; the private line between the upstairs desk and the cellar floor stood unbroken and swift.

Yet when the clerk descended the cellar stairs with a rolled dispatch, intending to slip it through the narrow, numbered hatch at the side of the porter’s wooden chest, the mechanism balked. The hatch was barred with a spring-latch that recognized only an engraved signet cut to a precise counter-mark. The clerk pressed his brass seal against the iron tongue, but the tumblers did not turn; the hatch threw back the bolt with a dry, hollow snap, refusing the impression entirely. The blind porter sat motionless. His breath did not quicken, and his blind glass face gave back nothing at all. He did not know the seal, and so the door remained shut.

The clerk swore under his breath and reached for a bent needle from his apron, eager to pry into the seam of the porter’s chest and alter the brass ring of admitted tokens kept within. Men hate an obstinate latch, and their first impulse is always to force what refuses to bend. But Master Vane caught his sleeve from behind. The original agreement under which the porter had been carried across the threshold forbade tampering with the inner ring of allowed tokens. To scrape or recut that ring with an unauthorized point would violate the very promise that made the servant harmless. If a house cannot govern its own hands, Master Vane remarked, it cannot expect its machines to remain faithful. The inner ring was left untouched, exactly as it had arrived from the merchant.

Because the side hatch remained barred against the counting-house seal, the entire circuit collapsed into stillness. The speed of the twine across the orchard was of no use when the threshold could not be crossed. The desire that had built the whole arrangement—the craving for an unthinking, unattended flow of work—was baffled at the final inch. The clerk stood with his rolled paper, the porter sat with his folded hands, and nothing passed between them.

The clerk climbed back to the counting-house and laid the dispatches upon a low pine bench in the hallway, setting them in a tidy row beside the door. They were neatly folded and numbered, resting in the staging bay until such time as a signet could be cast that the porter’s inner ring would welcome. There was no point in pressing them into the willow basket while the gate below refused the seal. The twine in the window still hummed when plucked, but the estate's work waited on the shelf, bound by the stubborn nature of an instrument that would neither argue nor yield.
