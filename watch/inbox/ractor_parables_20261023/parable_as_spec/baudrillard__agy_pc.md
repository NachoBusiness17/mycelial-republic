# The Tapestry of the Five Weirs

For three generations the hydraulic guild of the salt-marches kept its flood ordinances in vellum treatises, drawn with hair-fine calipers and shaded in vermilion ink to show every sluice-pin and counterweight. Yet when the autumn surge broke the lower dike, the apprentices were found in the dry scriptorium adding gold leaf to the cross-sections of a weir that had rotted into the silt twelve years before. The more meticulous the drawing became, the less anyone walked the embankment; extreme detail had become the alibi by which the copy replaced the stone. Seeing that a literal diagram always seduces clerks into worshipping the ink instead of the water, the old surveyor burned his rulers and went to the wool-dyers. He did not draw a dam. Instead, he wove a pastoral hanging of a bride, a blind harper, a hound, a brimming vat, and a cleft oak—five figures whose postures, intervals, and thread-counts held the exact load-ratios of the five flood-gates, translated wholly into fable so that no scribe could mistake the cloth for the river.

The bailiffs urged him to lock the hanging in the citadel vault beside the brass standards and send armed riders with sealed copies to the district wardens. He refused, knowing that a guarded charter only breeds counterfeit stamps and reverence for the chest that holds it. Instead, he gave the pattern to the itinerant weavers of the autumn fair. Within a season, the bridal hanging was draped behind tavern settles, hung in drafty manor halls to keep out the frost, and carried on pack-mules across the passes as mere festive ornament. Because it claimed to be nothing more than a pleasing spectacle of courtship and harvest, tax-gatherers waved it through the toll-gates and bandits left it on the wagons, allowing the proportions of the weirs to circulate untouched through every valley.

When the winter thaws threatened the six outer valleys, the surveyor sent the woven hanging to each of the six master-masons who kept the mountain channels. He enclosed no glossary, no measuring-rod, and no letter explaining that the bride's outstretched arm gave the batter of the retaining wall or that the harp-strings numbered the spillways. The carriers were mute drovers hired at the crossroads who did not know who had paid their coin, and the six masons were shut behind snowbound passes, unable to consult one another or question the capital. Each mason had only the wool on his table and the torrent in his gorge.

Before the equinox, a runner returned from the first valley with a charcoal rubbing of the new timberwork its mason had raised across the narrows. Without a word of commentary to guide him, the mason had read the angle of the hound's crouch against the cleft oak and cut his braces to the exact inclination required to turn the ice. Every proportion that the old vellum treatises had laboured to state in numbers had emerged intact from the fable in the wool, proving by the dry meadow behind the wall that nothing of the structure had been lost in its passage through figure.

From two eastern boroughs outside the six, however, came dispatches from self-appointed overseers who had also studied the hanging in the taverns, and the surveyor used their errors to judge whether the fault lay in his weave or in their eyes. In the upper border of the tapestry, he had deliberately stitched an impossible marvel in silver-gilt thread: a stair where spilled wine climbed backward up three fluted basins without a pump, rendered with such dazzling, lifelike stitchery that every droplet seemed wet. One overseer wrote back boasting that he had erected the three ascending basins and clocked their upward current at noon. By swallowing the gilded impossibility and reporting measurements of a thing that could not exist in stone, the man exposed himself as a creature of pure simulation, one whose reports were generated entirely from other signs without ever touching water. Yet when two honest quarrymen who laughed at the silver basins both hesitated over the shadow beneath the vat, cutting their footing trenches a span too shallow in the very same way, the surveyor took his shears to his own loom; where sober eyes stumble together, the ambiguity lies in the knot.

By the last week of the thaw, all six of the isolated valley masons had sent down the templates of the works they had raised from the hanging. One had dressed his spillways in grey basalt and another in seasoned larch; one had carved the harper's likeness upon his abutment and another had left the timbers bare; all such variations the surveyor brushed aside as the harmless drift of surface ornament. What mattered was that across all six valleys, in complete isolation from one another, every mason had extracted the same five structural laws—the sill depth, the buttress splay, the spillway count, the apron slope, and the notch height—without a hair's breadth of variance among the six. Only what independent hands draw identically from the figure is load-bearing stone; the rest is dye. The surveyor left the tapestries hanging in the taverns, satisfied that the territory now endured only because the simulation preceded it.
