# The Plates

Mara had kept the river out of Low Field for thirty years, and she knew the whole of it: cut the oak at the waning moon; set nine posts in the old clay; peg with rowan, never pine; and never trust a dyke that a man stands on to be admired. She wrote it out once for the council and watched them argue the page line by line until the words meant three things and none of them held water. A page, she decided, is where a rule goes to be negotiated, and the flood does not negotiate.

So she went to the kiln. She painted plates. On one, an oak falling under a thin moon; on another, nine posts marching into a riverbed; on a third, a bundle of rowan pegs bound with red thread; on a fourth, a proud landowner gesturing at a finished dyke with the water already over its crest. No words on any of them, no mark of her name. When the firing was done she carried crates of them to the fairs and gave them away to anyone who said the blue was good. A woman asked her once what the plates meant. "For the stew," Mara said, "or for the wall," and would not say more. Traders took them up the river and down, and in a hundred houses they hung over stoves, valued the way plates are valued, for the glaze and for nothing else.

The water rose in the seventh spring. It took the fourth valley first, then the sixth, and by summer travelers were bringing Mara word of what had been built in her name — though not her name, since no name was on the plates. In six valleys that had never traded with one another, that had no bridge between them but the river itself, the people had taken the plates down from over the stoves when the flood came, and every one of the six had built the same thing. Oak cut at the thin moon. Nine posts. Rowan pegs. Not a pine peg among them, and no man left standing on the finished work to be praised. Five things, returned to her six times, identical, by mouths that did not know each other. She sat by the cold kiln and understood that there had never been a decree; the council's page had drowned years before. The only law the river had ever obeyed was glazed blue and hung over stoves. The picture of the thing had become the thing's memory of itself.

The miller's town was the seventh valley, and it built on seven posts. The miller swore the plate commanded seven, and the town behind him swore it too. Mara traveled up. She asked, as if idly, what else the plates said. The miller's wife went to the stove and pointed to the sky of the second plate, where swallows cross above the posts. "Seven swallows," she said. "Seven posts. The picture counts them for you." Mara had not counted the swallows when she painted them. They were sky, thrown in to fill the glaze, and fired into every crate on purpose. Whatever they said in this valley, the valley had brought there itself. Had these people looked at the swallows and shrugged — had the sky said nothing to them, and the posts still come up seven — she would have gone home and broken her own plates, for the fault would have been in the firing. But the sky spoke whole sentences here. She drank the miller's beer and said none of this, and left their seven posts standing, because a dyke with seven posts is still a dyke.

After that she kept a slate by the kiln, and of every traveler she asked one question: what do the plates say where you come from. The answers that came back the same from every mouth — nine, thin moon, rowan — she underlined twice, and when a plate broke she fired those images again without change, exactly, the way one sets a bone. The answers that wandered, the oak become ash in one cold valley, its dyke standing anyway, she let wander. When she died there was no true text anywhere to find, and nobody went looking. But officials came down from the capital to study the river towns' uniform code, and they copied the dykes into their statute books, certain they were transcribing the old law of the land. They were transcribing a dinner service.
