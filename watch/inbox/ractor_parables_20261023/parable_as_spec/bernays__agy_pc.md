# The Five Copper Kettles

Kessler had spent forty winters staging festival processions for the salt barons before he sickened of his own trade and retired to a balcony above the wool market. In his youth he had made whole provinces buy bitter brine by dressing it as a bride's dowry; now he refused to manufacture belief for any patron, watching the square only to ask who profited when a crowd believed a spectacle, and what that belief moved their hands to do. When Maren, chief smelter of the upper foundry, brought him a vellum scroll of five winter quenching steps for frost-choked furnaces, Maren complained that although the decree hung on every post, the stokers never read past the preamble. Kessler noted that only chancery scribes profited from solemn proclamations, paid by the line to copy what no tired workman would remember. With shears he cropped the titles, warnings, and oaths, weaving the five bare timings into a three-act puppet farce about a jealous apothecary, a blacksmith's wife, and five copper kettles—knowing the version a crowd willingly accepts always crops the author's vanity.

Maren offered to hire bailiffs to herd the crews into the guildhall and force them to watch. Kessler asked who gained from compulsory benches: only bailiffs collecting fines at the turnstile, while every stoker folded his arms and despised the lesson. Instead, Kessler sold the play-book for three pence to travelling showmen by the cider vats. Because the farce mocked a greedy alderman and dunked the apothecary in a barrel, apprentices paid pennies to see it twice, tavern wives sang the kettle chorus while scouring pots, and rival booths copied the script by tallow light. Within a fortnight the comedy travelled the valley without warrant or guard, carried solely by the public's appetite for a laugh.

Maren wanted to ride ahead to the six mountain smelteries and explain that the puppet show held the winter safety sequence. Kessler forbade a single whisper: a foreman who knew he was being examined would flatter his superior by nodding along while changing nothing on the floor. Instead, a peddler left the chapbook and five copper kettles at each of the six snowbound gorges, which shared no couriers, with a festival notice offering a silver crown for the liveliest village staging on Saint Jude's Eve. None of the six crews knew Maren was involved or that the farce concerned smelting.

To make the farce work on stage, the players had to unstop the first kettle at the third chime, draw the grate pin before the wife entered with the bellows, and pour cold tallow only after the embers turned grey—otherwise the booth's wooden lattice jammed and killed the jest. When midwinter frost locked the waterwheels a week later, Maren watched the night shift at the first gorge in secret. Without orders, the stokers called out the apothecary's cues and executed all five quenching steps to the second. Because unbriefed workmen had rebuilt the exact working sequence from the play-book alone, Kessler knew nothing essential had been lost.

Earlier, during two suburb trials, the staging had failed in two revealing ways. At the tanners' yard, three separate crews pulled the brass damper before spreading sand; rereading his draft, Kessler found he had given both the wife and the apothecary a cue about cold wind in the same scene, proving his own phrasing was blurred. At the coal-wharf, however, a single contractor—who quietly profited selling replacement grates whenever a furnace cracked—claimed the farce taught that cold water belonged on red-hot plates. Because no other crew read the scene that way, the blunder exposed not a flaw in the script, but the contractor's own purse.

To catch such men without arguing over motives, Kessler had planted one deliberate absurdity in the second act: the apothecary boasted that stuffing pine shavings into the flue would turn soot into silver coin by dawn. Any crew that actually worked the props saw within ten lines that the boast set the apothecary's booth ablaze, and left the flue clear. When a newly hired river supervisor arrived at Kessler's balcony praising the play-book's wisdom and asking where to buy pine shavings for his chimneys, Kessler changed not a syllable; the bait had separated a flatterer from a workman who tested the piece.

When spring opened the passes, six prompt-books returned from the six isolated gorges. Kessler spread them across his workbench beside Maren. One gorge had dressed the apothecary in sheepskin; another cut the drinking song; a third added a comic terrier. Yet all six crews, without ever speaking to one another, had marked the exact same five kettle movements in the exact same order on their stage boards. What survived untouched across every separate staging was the solid timber of the work; the rest was only bright paint that let the valley believe the comedy was theirs, even as it moved their hands to save the furnaces.
