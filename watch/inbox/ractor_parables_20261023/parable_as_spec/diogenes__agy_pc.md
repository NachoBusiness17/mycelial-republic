# The Wrestler's Song at Noon

You grew tired of watching men bow to carved rules, old press-wright. For ten autumns your bronze plaques hung on the posts of the valley workshops, incised with every angle and notch needed to set an olive-beam so it would squeeze the cake dry without snapping the oak. Every autumn the foremen kissed the plaques, recited the figures like priests at a sacrifice, and then wedged their beams so crookedly that half the timber split before frost. So at dawn you took a hammer, smashed the bronze into scrap for the yard dogs to piss on, and made up a rough tavern song about Big Kreon the wrestler and the five holds by which he pinned five oiled suitors in the dust behind the butcher's stall.

Every grip, knee-brace, and hip-throw in those verses carried the exact span, fulcrum-depth, and counterweight drop of a true press-beam, without a single carpenter's number named aloud. You did not bind the song in a pouch or send a herald in a striped tunic to announce a master's lesson. You gave a loaf of barley bread and sour wine to a gap-toothed piper at the crossroads, whistled the tune thrice, and turned him loose toward the hills, where he traded the verses for dried figs alongside ditties about runaway goats so everyone took it for common street-music.

When the piper wandered into the hill-villages where the autumn olives lay heaped black in baskets, no runner went ahead to warn the carpenters that the old press-wright was testing their wits. No instructor stood beside the wine-vat to point out where a wrestler's elbow meant a mortise-shoulder or a suitor's ankle meant a stone wedge. The workmen heard the song cold while chewing salted lupins in the dust, without prompt or riddle-key.

In the first rocky yard above the ravine, young Jonas the carpenter listened to the piper once, wiped his nose on his sleeve, and walked straight to his timber-pile without asking a question. Before the shadows lengthened, he had notched his uprights, hung his counterweight stone, and pinned his great beam by the exact five holds of Big Kreon's match. When the baskets were stacked under the plank and the windlass turned, the dark oil ran out in a thick braid and the oak did not groan once, proving the tune had carried your whole measure intact.

Two ridges over, two other builders heard the same piper and ruined their presses before sundown, each for a different reason. Blunt-fingered Lucas set his fulcrum too high because you had slurred the second stanza about the wrestler's shoulder-lock so loosely that an honest ear could take the measure from either collarbone or chin—and when the beam kicked back, the fault was yours for singing with mush in your mouth. Next door, sleek Henrik, who wore a dyed wool collar in the sawdust, spurned the plain verse about sinking the heel-post into raw clay and propped his frame on polished marble rollers because he assumed a master's song must mean something grander than dirt; when his press slid into the ditch, his own swollen pride had blinded him.

To tell a muddy verse of yours from a workman who only apes wisdom for status, you had baked one rank lie into the fourth stanza: a line claiming Big Kreon greased his palms with mutton fat before grabbing the fourth suitor's wrists so his grip would hold tighter. Scarred Clara the smith spat on the ground when she heard that line, laughed in the piper's face, and rubbed dry wood-ash into her timber-pins so they would bite; whereas nodding Perrin, who always agreed with whatever sounded deep so the elders would call him clever, smeared grease over his iron pins and smiled until his beam shot loose and flattened his shed.

By the sixth evening, autumn storms had washed out the footpaths between the six hill-villages so no messenger could pass from yard to yard. Across all six isolated villages, the crews sang the wrestler's song and built their presses alone. One crew carved a scowling wrestler on the windlass-drum, another painted red rings around the capstan, and a third hung the piper's reed-flute from the rafters; yet all six crews, without trading a word, cut the exact same five joints at the same five measures to bear the crushing weight, showing which timbers truly held the press together and which were mere ornament.

Today at high noon, when the sun was bright enough to count the fleas on a hound's back and all six villages were pouring clear oil from those same five holds, the barefoot man who sleeps in the cracked wine-cask walked into your yard swinging a lit horn lantern. He jerked his thumb toward the six ridges, where your six foremen were still bellowing the wrestler's chorus across the valley—pretending they sang only for the love of rough timber, while every man of them kept one eye cocked toward your roof like a kennel-dog waiting to see who would get his ears scratched first for catching your tune. Then the barefoot man held his lantern against your knuckles where you sat in the daylight paring a fresh stick to hide a new riddle inside a winter tale, and told you to stop begging the world for one more proof that strangers can read your mind without your speaking plain, blow out the wick, and come eat raw onions in the sun.
