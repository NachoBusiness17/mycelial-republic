# The Wright Who Stopped Writing

In the year the river took two bridges in one spring, the old wright of the ford gave up paper. For thirty years he had sent his measures and schedules to the towns, and every man who read them found a different bridge inside, and the men with money had the pages rewritten until they described whatever was already being built. The river dined on every version. The wright watched it eat and said nothing, which is also a kind of teaching.

His boy grieved for the papers. "Put the guild's wax on them," the boy said. "Then they'll have to read them your way." The wright had already tried the wax. He had once walked the guild hall at noon with his lamp lit, and when they asked what he was searching for, he said: a bridge that resembles its papers. They laughed him out. He kept the lamp.

That winter he made a song. It told of a woman hauling water across the ice in a hard year, three jars lashed on a small sled. Everything a bridge needed was in what she did. She crossed where the current slowed and the reeds stood up straight. She struck the ice with her pole at every third step, and where the knock came back dull she turned aside and crossed elsewhere. She spaced her three jars the width of a hand apart, so each rode its own boards. She laid her boards end over end and never let a joint hang in the air. She crossed in the hard of winter and never once at the thaw. There were no figures in it and no commands. Only the woman, the pole, the jars, the ice — and a child could sing it.

He gave it away. No name on it, no mark, no wax. The fiddlers took it up because it sang well, and it went over passes in winter that his papers had never crossed in summer, and towns learned it from one another without ever hearing of a wright. The boy wanted to ride with it and explain it. "No," said the wright. "If you stand at their shoulders, you'll never know what the song can do by itself. Send it out naked, and keep your mouth shut afterward too. Explanation is a coat you put on it so it will look like your papers."

In spring he walked the river road, lamp lit at noon, and the ferrymen had heard the joke by now and laughed before he could make it. Six towns on six rivers, none of which knew his face or each other's, had each raised a bridge out of the song. He visited every one and said not one word about it. He only looked at what they had built.

Four things stood identical everywhere, close as a hand: the piers set in slow water, the footing struck with a pole and answered before it was trusted, the trusses spaced like the jars a hand apart, the joints carried and never left hanging in air. Whatever all six had raised alike, he wrote on his heart as true, and afterward he let no singer change a syllable of those lines, and he explained them to no one, ever, again.

Two towns had botched the fifth thing — they had set stone in the flood month. Now, was it the song, or was it the town? He had hidden one lie in the song on purpose, the way a man leaves a loose plank he wants stepped on: a late verse where the woman crosses once at the thaw and the ice holds her, because her heart is pure. One crooked town had sung that verse in earnest and believed it — trusted pure hearts to hold a footing in flood. From them he learned nothing about the song; ears like those would mishear any song ever sung. The other town had laughed the lie clean out of its singing — children's nonsense, they called it — and had still carried stones into the flood month, because the true verse said only "in the hard of winter," and their valley's winter sometimes lies and ends in February. That was his own fault. He took the verse home and cut it plainer, and the next year no town mistook it.

By the second spring, six bridges stood on six rivers, alike to the span of a hand, and the song had crossed the mountains and lost nothing on the way. The boy came to him again with the wax. "The world should know whose it is," he said. "You're still asking them to say they understood you," said the wright. "Stop asking. You have six bridges that understood you. That is the whole of it, and you could stop wanting more today, and the lamp would find nothing missing."
