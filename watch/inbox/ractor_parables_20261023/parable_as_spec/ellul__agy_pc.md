# The Six Foundries of the Valley

For thirty winters the bell-founders of the grey valley had written down their methods, and every scroll of regulation had given birth to another scroll. When a casting cracked in the frost, the guild appointed measurers to weigh the charcoal; when the measurers disagreed, the council appointed tally-men to watch the measurers; soon the bronze was poured not when the furnace glowed white, but when the third horn blew from the counting-shed. The founders no longer listened to the metal. They served the schedule, because the schedule had made itself the only cure for the errors the schedule created.

Anselm, who had cast the great bronze for the river abbey before the tally-men were born, refused to add another clause to the manuals, and he refused to smash the furnaces in anger. When the time came to set down the five essential measures of winter casting, he wrote no treatise at all. Instead, he composed an autumn stage-play about a patient widow, a boastful miller, and three brothers who rebuilt a hearth before the snow. Every proportion of the craft lived inside the story's turns: the balance of tin and copper hid in the widow's dowry, the thickness of the outer clay in the brothers' dike, the venting of the mold in the chimney-draft, the banking of dry peat in the long night watch, and the hour of striking the core in the thaw at dawn.

He did not lock the script in an oak chest or hand it to sworn couriers who demanded passes at every parish bridge. He gave the verses to a company of strolling players at the sheep-fair. The players cared nothing for furnaces or guild decrees; they loved the cadence of the widow's replies and the comic swagger of the miller. Because the work delighted the crowd as a play, drovers and weavers sang its choruses along the high road, and the story passed freely into every village without a bailiff inspecting its pages.

When the first frost hardened the ruts, the players travelled to the six isolated foundries scattered across the hills. Anselm had forbidden them to speak his name, to offer a single word of commentary, or to hint that the verses concerned the casting of bronze. In each yard, after the players packed their masks, an unnamed carter left ingots of copper and tin beside the kiln with a token asking only that the master cast a bell from what the evening had shown. None of the six masters knew that the others were being tried, and none had an instructor to question.

Earlier that autumn, Anselm had tried an unfinished version of the play upon two northern smithies. Both smiths had drawn the vents too narrow at the very point where the second brother bolted the barn latch. Because two strangers working leagues apart had stumbled at the identical verse, Anselm knew the shadow lay in his own phrasing, and he mended the scene. Yet when a proud founder in the east ruined his casting by stripping the peat while the bronze was still red, whereas the others waited until morning, that solitary mistake exposed the man's own hunger for speed rather than any flaw in the play.

To separate a hearer's blindness from a poet's vagueness beyond all doubt, Anselm had woven a snare into the first act. In the tavern scene, the boastful miller swore that dousing a red-hot mold with icy well-water saved three days of waiting and doubled his silver. Any artisan who respected his material knew cold water would shatter hot clay and metal alike, yet a mind conquered by haste would seize the shortcut. Whoever reached for the well-bucket convicted his own judgment, leaving the verse blameless.

Behind the abbey stood the granite pillar where the guild carved its enduring customs. Whenever a single master claimed to have found a new secret in the play, the mason left his chisel in his belt. Only when separate foundries, working with ridges between them and no messengers on the road, drew the very same practice from the scenes and cast their bronze alike did the mason cut that measure into the pillar to bear the weight of future winters.

On the twenty-third day of the tenth month, all six hill foundries brought their finished bells down to the river ford. Every one of the six bells rang with the same clear pitch, proving that the unglossed play had carried the whole craft into metal without losing a grain of its measure, and that all six workshops had drawn forth every one of the five proportions without a single fault or a single bucket of well-water. Seeing the six flawless castings, Anselm's young deputy eagerly unrolled a parchment proposing a permanent bureau of twelve dramatists and twenty circuit-judges to grade the valley's hearths every new moon. Anselm took the parchment and laid it upon the brazier until it curled to grey dust, leaving only the six bells and a song that needed no priesthood.
