# The Song at the Weir

The mill stood at the narrows where the river dropped, and the town had grown around the mill's hours rather than around its own. Bells were set by the wheel; bread was eaten when the stones turned; children learned to sleep against the beating of the water as if it were weather. Nobody had voted for the hours. The old miller, Bram, kept the working to himself for forty years and refused to the end to post it. "The wheel cannot read," he said, "and paper would only teach the town to obey another turning thing." When he died in the first frost, the working lived in one person: his apprentice, Ilsa.

The town came to her door the day after the burial and asked for the rules, nailed to the millpost where rules belong. She had watched the town for years and knew what a posted rule did there: it was read once, admired, and then obeyed the way the wheel was obeyed, without thought, until the obedience was older than the reason for it. She said she would think, and closed the door.

She knew the working the way a hand knows a pocket: drop the sluice before touching the stones; dress the stone only while it is cold; never fill the hopper past the third mark while the wheel runs full; rest the sacks a day before they are weighed; open the weir gate at dusk and never at dawn. Five things, small as that. She could have said them in a breath. Instead she took them apart and hid them in a tale about two sisters who inherit their mother's jars — which jar is opened first, which is filled and how far, which is left alone to cool, which is given away at evening — and she set the tale to a tune and sold it at the market for a penny, as amusement, and told no buyer what it carried. The song went out with the boats. It was sung in taverns for its chorus. It traveled as pleasure travels, which is farther than instruction.

In spring a stranger came up from the river, sent by no one, asking for a season's work. Ilsa put the sluice bar in his hands without a word. He dropped the sluice before he touched the stones, in the order the elder sister opens her jars, and at dusk, not dawn, he walked to the weir gate. He had never seen the mill. He did not know he had been taught. When the day's work came out right, Ilsa understood that the song had given away the whole working and lost nothing of it on the road between her mouth and his hands.

A second traveler, later, worked it wrong: he filled the hopper past the mark while the wheel ran full, the stones choked, and he swore at the mill. Watching him, Ilsa could not tell whether the fault lay in her verse about the third jar or in the man's ears. She could not ask him; a man asked about his own hearing answers like a man asked about his honesty. So she made a new verse, sweet and easy to keep, in which the tale's miller tips every jar in at once — the one thing the real mill cannot bear — and she let it go down the river with the rest. The ones who came afterward singing the new verse proudly, as though it were the best part, showed her what was broken in them and not in the song. The ones who came frowning, saying the verse sat ill with its neighbors, showed her that the song was sound; and where a true verse made even careful singers frown, she took that verse home and rewrote it.

All that season she kept one practice. When a singer arrived, she took him alone — never two together, never after talk — and asked him to give the tale back as he had it. Six came, one by one, from six directions. All six gave back the five things, whole and in order, without knowing they were giving anything back. And where all six, who had never met, sang a passage word for word the same, she marked it in her mind as one the mill could not live without; where their versions wandered, she let the wandering be.

The wheel went on turning, and the town went on admiring it, because it worked. Ilsa did not admire it. She had buried the mill's five necessities in a penny song, and the song was already farther down the river than the wheel's shadow would ever reach, sung by strangers to strangers who would never see the water. The rules stayed nailed to nothing.
