# The Bride of the Ford

A friend at the capital has written to ask how the bridges of the salt province come to outlive their builders, though no two builders ever studied under one master. I will answer with a story, and I warn him that my page is narrow.

In Anselme's youth the bridge law lay on a vellum roll as long as a barge. A bridge at the willow town fell in its third winter, and at the inquest it came out that two masons, the same line of the roll before them, had raised two different arches from it, each man certain the words were his. Anselme read the finding twice and said, at supper, that what men argue over they ruin, and what men love they carry.

There was one bridge whose whole law he held in his head: where the footing goes, how the pier stands, how the arch is closed, what is given to the flood, how the slow are brought across. Of this he wrote not a line. He made a ballad, the Bride of the Ford: a wedding party come to a river in spate, a fiddler who trusts the bright water, and a bride who knows better. She leads her people to the bank where willows stitch the gravel with their roots; she sets the fiddler's trestle with its feet spread wide upon the bar. The ring goes over the water from hand to hand overhead, and neither rain nor river takes it. She unbars the mill-gate with her own hands and lets the flood go through, and the song has hard words for whoever bars it again. She ties the leading-rope low, at the grandmother's hand and not at her own. Every practice of the craft is in the ballad, but wearing wedding clothes.

He taught it to two singers and let it go. It had a flood in it, and a wedding, and a fiddler saved at the last verse; there was nothing in it to obey. Within a year it was sung in taverns, painted on a saltseller's wall, and turned by children at the rope in the yard. No one was ever ordered to carry it, and so everyone carried it.

Then Anselme died, and his daughter kept his book. When the gravel town begged for a bridge and had no master, she sent neither drawing nor mason, only a girl who knew the ballad, told to say that her patron liked the song and would have the bridge as the song would build it. The builders laughed at the fancy, hummed as they worked, and raised what the bride knew, knowing no more than she did. The spring flood came down; the bridge kept its feet; and the daughter wrote in her book that the song had lost nothing in the singing.

In the third town a mason made the arch flat, because he liked it low, and it cracked in the first autumn. She did not send for the mason, and she did not touch the pen to the ballad. She put one question in the margin of her book: the verse, or the ear?

So she did a sly thing. She had the ballad copied out with one verse changed, in which the bride, coming last to the water, spreads her white cloak upon it and walks over it as over marble. It is the sweetest verse in the copy, and it is a lie no craft ever taught. To the next town she sent that copy, and watched what rose out of the ground. There a mason laid rushes on the running water and called it footing, and smiled at his cleverness; she marked his name and wrote him nothing more: an ear that will build a sweet lie will build anything, and the fault had found its house. In the town after, a young woman laughed at the cloak verse and would have nothing to do with it; she built the rest as the bride had done, and went wrong only in the placing of the flood's door. Then at last the daughter knew where the pen belonged: not in any ear, but in the verse.

Year on year she went on so, spring after spring, giving the song to strangers who had never met one another, with a page in her book for every town. A quiet thing showed itself. There were verses that every stranger built the same way, apart and unhinted; those she cut into the guild-stone at the crossroads, and they are the province's law to this day. Verses each town built after its own fashion she let the singers forget, until the ballad wore smooth as a stair and carried only what all ears heard alike. What her father meant is nowhere written; the stone says only what everyone heard.

The Bride of the Ford was the first song tried so, and is still the plainest. It went to six towns, none knowing that any other had built; six times the same bridge rose, and stood. In the margin beside its first page the daughter wrote what I copy now for my friend: the demonstration stands in six places at once, and wants no margin.

When you cross at the willow town, listen to the ferrymen. Ask them where the law is kept, and they will point at the bridge; ask again, and they will sing.
