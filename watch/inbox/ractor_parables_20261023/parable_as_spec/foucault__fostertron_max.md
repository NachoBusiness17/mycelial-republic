# The Wall in the Ravine

The hillside under the courthouse let go one night in March, and the wall of the courthouse went with it — down the ravine, into the dark below. The wall had carried five sentences, cut deep, and the town had walked past it for so long that no one could recite them. The falling stone finished the forgetting.

The clerk of verdicts, an old woman who had spent forty years copying judgments into folios, was told to cut the sentences again, on new stone above the market. She asked for a year, and because she was old — respected, perhaps, or merely old — she was given it.

She did not touch the chisel. She wrote a tale instead, about a beekeeper and a ferryman who trade a jar of dark honey for a single crossing, and about what each owes the other when the swarm comes home in winter. The five sentences are nowhere in it. She gave the tale to the fiddler at the inn, to the women who embroider kerchiefs, to the schoolmistress as a game of bees for the children, and to a painter for the tavern's back wall. She paid each from her own purse, asked each to change nothing, and told no one what the tale was for. Within a season the beekeeper was sung at weddings, children played the swarm with wooden spoons, and a kerchief crossed two counties in a peddler's pack. The tale belonged to everyone, which is to say that no one asked what it was.

Then she watched the strangers.

Grain merchants, refugees, a runaway monk: they came through knowing nothing of the fallen wall. They heard the tale the way one hears rain. When the town's old cases found them — a swarm settling on a disputed roof, a boat moored on the wrong bank, a jar entrusted and then mourned — the clerk sat at the back of the room and would not speak. She waited to see what the rain had done.

The first stranger fed the swarm before he fed himself. The second carried the jar back unopened. Through the season six strangers met the town's cases, one after another, none of them known to any other, and all six did the same five things — the five things, though they could not have known it, that the wall had required. In her private folio the clerk wrote one line: nothing was lost in the singing.

Not everyone read clean. The innkeeper's son had heard the tale a hundred nights and taken from it that the ferryman is owed everything; he began demanding tolls at the landing. The town wished to shame him in the market. The clerk stopped them. A wrong act, she said, is testimony — but of whom? Of the tale, or of the hearer? She would not sentence anyone before she knew which of the two stood accused.

For this she had laid her floors falsely. In the second verse the ferryman follows a glittering channel past the red buoy, and the tale marks the glitter as wrong water — plainly, twice, and again in the refrain. A hearer who sails the glitter in his own life has not been misled by the verse; he has brought his own river with him. But when the fiddler's own daughter took the tolls to be owed, the clerk did not summon the daughter. She went home and counted hearers. Where one stumbles past a clean verse, the crookedness rode in with the hearer. Where many stumble at the same stone, the stone stands accused, and that verse she rewrote for the fiddler's next season, quietly, so the weddings would not notice. The night she rewrote it, three neighbors stood in her doorway to watch her do it. She observed that she had made herself the one who could be watched, and let them stand.

At year's end she laid out what every hearer had done in every case — strangers, children, the dyer, the nun who never left her cell — each hearing given alone. Where all of them, hearing separately, did the same act, she trusted the singing as she had once trusted cut stone, and left that sentence where it was. Where they differed she did not trust it; of the differing there was only one, and that one sentence she cut at last into stone, small and set low, where only a kneeling reader could reach it.

In time no one could say where the law lived. Singers corrected one another across tables; a slipped verse was set right before the chorus came, and nobody called that obedience. The clerk grew older and stopped keeping the folio. On the last evening of her appointment she was at the landing, and a stranger handed her a jar to hold while he counted out his toll. She found herself giving the ferryman his share of the dark honey before the stranger's hand had closed on his coins — acting as the beekeeper's wife acts in the fourth verse — and afterwards she could not say whether the judgment had been sung into her or had been hers from the start. She noticed that she could not say it, and let that be the last line she did not write.
