# The Woodblock of the Five Hearths

Before the winter firing, the potter Venn burned his notebooks on the courtyard cobbles. His assistant begged him to write down the five proportions for laying a high-draft kiln so outlying villages could fire stoneware without cracking their vaults. Venn refused to draft a treatise or chalk a numbered diagram. Instead, he carved a pearwood block showing a country harvest supper: two bakers tilting a dough-trough at the pitch of a banked floor, a girl pouring cider through a narrow funnel into a wide jug, twins carrying a double yoke of firewood at even heights, a cooper binding a cask with three willow hoops, and five musicians standing beneath a curved ash arbor. Every measure of the kiln lived inside the postures of the feast, without a single numeral or rule cut into the wood.

Venn did not seal the prints in courier pouches or stamp them with a guild mark. He gave stacks of sheets to peddlers who sold them in distant market squares for a copper coin alongside almanacs and ballads for families to pin above their tables. Travelling as a festive picture, the sheet passed through tollgates and bailiffs without suspicion, entering cottages its maker would never visit. Watching the mules depart, the envoy Ansel reminded Venn that once the paper crossed the town ditch, it ceased to belong to the hand that carved it; its meaning would be remade at every table where strangers looked up from their bread.

When Venn's assistant saddled a pony to ride after the peddlers and explain to village bricklayers that the supper hid a builder's plan, Ansel barred the stable gate and emptied the boy's satchel of its notes. In every valley, the masons were to receive only the printed sheet—no preface, no key, no whisper of what Venn had wished them to build. If the woodcut could not guide an uncoached hand from the wall alone, then the carver had trusted his own voice rather than the cut of the wood.

Ansel rode out to sit on the benches where the print was received. In the first orchard village, an old bricklayer studied the supper scene over his broth, measured the tilt of the dough-trough against the curve of the ash arbor with his calipers, and walked to his clay-pit. Without a word from Venn's yard, he raised a kiln whose floor-pitch, throat-pinch, twin flues, triple girdle, and vaulted crown matched Venn's own work to a thumb's breadth. When the first firing cooled without a cracked pitcher, Ansel knew the woodblock had carried the whole craft across the hills intact.

In the next three valleys, the bricklayers built differently, and when the assistant called them mistaken, Ansel silenced him. Down in the windy salt-flats, a builder kept the tilted floor and narrow funnel, but replaced the high ash arbor with a low flat roof because sea-gales tore at tall vaults; she accepted the picture's foundation while bending its frame to local weather. Up in the timber gorge, a charcoal-burner read the rich feast as a landlord's boast over hungry laborers, laying his bricks in an open trench that rejected the enclosed hearth altogether. In a fourth village, a mason squinted at a blotched corner where Venn's gouge had slipped across the willow hoops and bound his kiln with two uneven straps that bulged under heat. Ansel recorded all three without ranking Venn's wish as truth or the charcoal-burner's refusal as error: every departure revealed either a blur in the carver's cut or the slant of the bench from which a viewer met the page.

To separate a flaw in the block from a habit in the viewer's eye, Ansel pointed to a snare carved near the edge of the supper: a hound sleeping on the live coals of a brazier while holding an unmelted tallow candle. Any builder who knew heat from experience saw that tallow runs in flame and dogs do not nap on embers, reading that corner as a warning against resting weight over fire. In the abbey town, however, a clerk who bowed to guild authority over his own eyes mortared his cross-beam straight through the firebox because the print showed the hound on the coals, and his arch fell at the first spark; yet three careful builders who dismissed the impossible candle still argued over the smudged willow hoops. By that hound on the embers, Ansel separated blind deference in the viewer from places where the knife had left the line uncertain.

After Venn recut the blurred willow hoops, fresh sheets were hung in six workshops separated by high ridges, whose builders shared no road or courier. Ansel visited all six benches in turn. Though each workshop built with local clay and spoke of the supper in its own valley's idiom, all six independent builders drew out the exact same five proportions from the silent print. What all six strangers recovered identically in isolation, Ansel marked as the true spine of the craft—the part that stood fast across every stranger's bench alike.
