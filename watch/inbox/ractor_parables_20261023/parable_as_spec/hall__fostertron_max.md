# The Picture of the Ford

Maren kept the ford where the high road crossed the gray water, and she knew the crossing the way other people know a face. Downstream the roads were watched. Papers that gave orders were opened at the tollhouses, argued with, stamped, and sometimes burned, and whatever reached the far villages arrived changed or not at all. But no toll-keeper ever unrolled a picture. A picture passed every gate untouched, because a picture looked like a thing that wanted nothing.

So one winter, while the water was too high to cross, Maren ground her colors and painted the ford. She painted the water lying flat under a sky just losing its stars, the hour when the current ran slowest. She painted the pale stones standing in a crooked line, and among them the dark round stones that looked easiest to step on — they rolled underfoot, and she painted them rounder than they were. She painted a rope made fast to the bank above the stones, so the water would push a fallen swimmer against it and not past it. She painted the heavy yellow-edged sky of the day after rain, when the air had dried but the water was still climbing. At the far bend she painted a knot of white foam, which rose only in the weeks when the crossing was true. Around everything she painted a border of ordinary flowers.

She told no one what the picture held. She gave copies to the wool carriers going down both roads, with nothing written on the backs, and kept one for her own wall, which she never explained to anyone.

Each spring her apprentice Ilse walked downstream to listen, and came home with a book of what the picture had said to people.

At the first tollhouse, the keeper had hung his copy crooked on the wall between two hunting scenes. It matched his shelf, he said, and he was right, and Ilse wrote that down.

In the second village a ferryman had propped his copy in the boathouse. He crossed at first light now, and had taught his sons to feel for the pale stones with their feet; but he still crossed under the yellow sky, because he liked to watch climbing water, and once it had cost him a winter's wool. He kept the picture and kept his own habit too, and Ilse wrote down both.

In the third village a midwife had held the picture up in the square and called it a fool's purchase, a thing that could teach nothing, and had fed it to her stove. Nothing came back from that village but the story of the burning, and Ilse wrote that down as well.

In the fourth village lived a carpenter who had asked a child to count what the ford in the picture held. The child counted five things. That summer the carpenter walked up the high road, found the ford, did the five things one after another, and crossed dry. When Ilse brought this home, Maren said, "Then it lost nothing on the road."

The fifth spring there was a soaked bundle in the book. The ferryman's neighbor had liked the look of the round dark stones, crossed on them at noon, and the stones rolled and the water took what he carried. Ilse asked if the picture should change. Maren asked what hour he had chosen and whether he had felt for the pale stones. Noon, and no. "The paint is not at fault," she said, and changed nothing.

But the sixth spring brought a shepherd from the high valley who had done everything gently and in order, had read the flat dawn water and the yellow sky as the same water, and been pushed against the rope until his cousin hauled him out. His eyes went bad at dusk, Ilse said; he had refused the dark stones, and still the hour had been wrong. Maren took up the brush that night, made the dawn cold and blue and unmistakable, made the day-after sky sicker and yellower, and sent the picture down the roads again.

After that she sent it every winter. In time the book held six accounts from six villages that had no road between them and no trade. Each of the six, without the others and without Maren, had found the same five things in the picture and done them in the same order. Where the six agreed, Maren would not change a stroke again, and called those the strokes that carried. Where two villages differed, she painted the part clearer or let it go.

In her last spring the ferryman came up the high road at first light and crossed dry on the pale stones, and stood at her door with his rolled copy in his hands. He asked her to tell him what the picture meant — the true meaning, from the maker's mouth. Maren brought out the book and turned to the page with the six entries, and above them one entry in her own hand, dated before any of them, which said only: the water at dawn. She asked him what the picture had said to him. He told her. She wrote it under the six, closed the book, and said, "Now there are seven." She had never once managed to read past the dawn water herself.
