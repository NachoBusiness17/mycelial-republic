# The Tale in the Cellar

Orla kept the river town's law, and she watched the law fail twice. The first time it burned: strangers in clean coats made a fire of the codex in the square, and the town cheered, because it had come to love hating what it could no longer read. The second time it was obeyed: men carried the rewritten book like lanterns and fell into the river anyway, because a rule recited with nothing behind it holds no one up. What the town refused to carry, the river carried instead. That was the spring the bridge went down.

So Orla took the five duties of the crossing — the ones her grandmother had paid for with her back, the ones no book had held longer than a generation — and carried them down into the cellar, where the town kept what it would not look at. There she wove them into a tale about a ferryman who married the drowned. The duties lived in what he did each dawn: the stone he blessed with his thumb, the rope he never touched after dark, what he fed the river and what he refused it, whom he carried first and whom he made wait. Any child could learn the tale by heart. None of them knew they were learning the bridge.

She gave it to a peddler and told him to sell it as amusement, a penny the sheet, at firesides and fairs, and never once to say what it was.

By the time the bridge fell again, Orla was dead, the codex was ash, and the town's memory was a flooded field. Only the tale remained, sung three valleys away. The masons did a thing that made the old women laugh: they sent the tale out as a tale, to six inns on six roads, asking each innkeeper to have it told at night and, in the morning, to take down what the stranger remembered about the keeping of a crossing. They told no one why.

Six strangers — a midwife, a ferryman's widow, a deserter, a schoolmistress, a beggar who had once been a judge, and a girl who sold eels — heard it cold, and each wrote five duties. The masons laid the six papers side by side and read them twice, and the head mason said only: it lost nothing on the road. Where all six agreed, word upon word, he tied a red thread through the page, and those lines went into the stones at the deepest course. What wavered, he cut away like ornament, without grief.

A seventh paper came from the third inn, from a soldier. He wrote the five duties and then a sixth: that the keeper must put out his own left eye, since the tale's ferryman wore a patch. The masons did not laugh. Orla had left them one thread for exactly such a man — a false one, lovely and bright, woven on purpose: in the tale, the ferryman washes his boat in wine each spring. The soldier had swallowed the wine and asked for more. A man who takes the planted lie into his mouth and calls it sweet is not reading the tale; he is reading himself, and the tale has only held the lamp. The schoolmistress, though, had struck the wine through and written beside it: this is not true, and I do not know why it is here. Her paper was marked highest. The soldier was not sent away; he was sent down to the river to look at his face in it until he could say what the patch had been for.

The wine stayed in the tale. In the one letter Orla left, she had been plain: leave the lie I wove, for it is the sieve; every generation must sort itself through it, and the ones who find it sweetest are the ones the river is already waiting for.

The tale is sold in the town still, a penny the sheet, and the children sing it as nonsense. The bridge stands. When a mason doubts his stones, he opens no book; he sends the tale down one more road and waits to see what comes back written. If the five come home wearing the same words, he sleeps. The cellar where Orla wove is kept swept and empty, and its door is not locked, because the town had already paid once to learn: what you will not carry down to the cellar yourself, the river carries under.
