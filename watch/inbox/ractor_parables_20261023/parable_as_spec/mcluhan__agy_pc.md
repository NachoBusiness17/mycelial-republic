# The Ballad of the Five Weirs

When the old water-master sent parchment plans down the valley, the builders who unrolled them grew blind in their thumbs. A ruled sheet of ink narrowed their eyes to a flat line and deadened their ears to the push of snowmelt against oak; men who held the parchment began arguing over the ink-marks while their sluices cracked in the frost. Seeing that every tool remakes the hand that grips it before it ever touches the timber, the water-master burned his measuring-tables. In their place he composed a winter tale about a blind miller and five stubborn brothers who each braced his shoulders at a different pitch against a rising flood. Every turn of the miller's misfortune was proportioned so that anyone who pictured the brothers leaning into the current had to feel, in his own ribs and wrists, the exact five stresses required to set a stone weir. The old builders' tables were not discarded; they were swallowed whole and turned into the invisible bones of a story.

He did not seal the tale in a courier's pouch or mark it with the stamp of the guild. Instead, he taught it to ballad-singers at the sheep-shearing fair, where it traveled from tavern to hearth as an ordinary entertainment. A courier's pouch invites the toll-guard's knife and puts the receiver on guard, stiffening his posture to obey or rebel; a song sung over ale enters through the unguarded ear. Because the vessel wore the shape of common amusement, rival bailiffs hummed it along the high road without suspecting what they carried, and the proportions of the stonework crossed five parishes inside the throats of people who thought they were only passing the winter night.

When the singer reached the outlying workshops, the water-master forbade any preface, hint, or whispered instruction. Each master-mason was placed alone in a closed timber shed where he could neither see his neighbors' benches nor ask what the ballad meant. The singer recited the tale of the miller and his five brothers once through the slats of the wall and walked away, leaving the mason with wet clay and cut stone. The water-master knew that a preface puts a rumor between the ear and the wrist, teaching a man to copy what he was told to expect rather than what the song itself forces his hands to feel; left alone in the shed with only the cadence of the five brothers in his chest, each mason had to let the shape of the tale move his trowel.

In an early trial with two apprentices, the water-master judged both the song and the listeners by what their hands built from the hearing. Where an apprentice set the stones at the exact pitch the water-master had first conceived, the stonework proved the song had carried the weight without spilling a drop between mouth and mallet. Yet when both apprentices set the third buttress askew, the water-master knew the crookedness had to come from one of two places: either the third verse had been blurred in the composing, or the listener's own habits had warped what entered his ear. To sever the two causes, he sent an assistant to ask each apprentice why the third brother in the song had braced his heel against a willow root—a detail that was nowhere in the ballad. The first apprentice frowned and answered that there was no willow root in the tale, though he confessed he could not tell from the verse whether the third brother knelt on gravel or bedrock. By refusing the invented root, he proved his ear was clean and that the verse itself was indistinct. The second apprentice nodded eagerly and explained at length how the willow bark had kept the brother's boot from slipping; his willingness to build upon a lie showed that the fault lay not in the ballad, but in a mind trained to flatter a questioner rather than attend to the vessel before him.

Once the water-master sharpened the third verse so that the gravel rasped clearly under the brother's knee, he sent the finished ballad to the six master-masons in their six separate sheds. At dusk the doors were unbarred. Without having exchanged a word or a glance, all six masons had laid out the exact same five stone courses at the exact same angles. One mason had also added a carved otter on the coping because the miller's coat reminded him of river-fur, and another had grooved the spillway because the singer's voice had trembled at the end. The water-master swept away both the otter and the groove when raising the great river-wall. He mortared into the main foundation only the five courses that all six isolated pairs of hands had drawn identically from the song, and when the spring thaw came down from the peaks, the river struck those five courses and turned harmlessly into the mill-race.
