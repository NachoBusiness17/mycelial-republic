# The Copper Picture

When the guild of well-diggers gave up writing, the master Roza locked the old book of law in a chest and hung the key on the same cord as her knives. The book had carried the whole craft in sentences; she meant to carry it in something that could hang on a wall. She cut a fair. Puppets in the foreground, a pie-seller, children chasing a goose; and behind them, small as accidents, men at work on wells — one sunk in the low ground and drowning under morning rain; one dug wide-mouthed and already caving at the lips; one lined with stone that stopped exactly at the water, standing a hundred years; and in a far corner, at the bend where the river turns, a well brimming sweet and clear while the flood ran brown against its stone. Every line of the law went into the fair as something happening, and no line went in as a line. The chest was shut, and the craft existed nowhere as words.

The guild had the plank pressed by the thousand and gave the sheets to the fair-criers to sell at a copper apiece, and the criers were told to say nothing about wells — nothing at all, only pretty, only cheap. The sheets went up on kitchen walls over the hearths of three provinces. Nobody was taught. The kitchens did the teaching, at supper, year after year, to whoever looked up from the pot.

Over the pass, in valleys where the guild's name meant nothing, diggers hung the sheet because it was pretty and cheap. In spring they dug. Their spades refused the low ground; their wells came out narrow at the mouth; they carried stone down to the water and set none below it; and they left the river's knee alone, having seen what ran against the lip there. If a stranger had asked them why, they would only have said that their hands had always wanted to dig this way.

When word came back over the pass that these wells stood sweet, the guild did not praise the diggers. They praised the plank. A well standing true in a valley that had never heard of them was the only proof they trusted that the picture had lost nothing of the book: the craft had crossed the mountains and come home whole, wearing no words at all.

One valley sent worse word — a well at the river's knee, flooded foul within the month. The guild did not ask what the digger had done wrong; they asked which part of the fair he had lived in. He had lived in the far corner, where the flood runs brown against the lip of a well that brims sweet: the scene Roza had cut on purpose, a promise planted against the grain of the land. He had looked at the brimming and not at the flood. The fault was in his wanting, and the corner stayed as it was.

Then another valley failed, and this time the guild looked at the plank and not at the man. A well there had been lined with stone clear to the floor, and no water would come. The scene of the lining had been cut crowded: an eye could take the stone as going all the way down, or as stopping at the water, and both takings lived in it. Two valleys, two wells, one plank speaking twice. That winter Roza re-cut the lining until it could say one thing only, and the guild called the dry well a fair price for finding where the plank had murmured.

Each summer the guild laid every valley's word side by side. The scenes that all valleys, stranger to stranger, had dug the same way — the stone stopping at the water, the narrow mouth, the low ground left undug, the knee left alone — these they called the spine of the plank, and their own law was that a spine is never re-cut, not for any wish, because what all strangers take alike can carry the weight of the whole craft. The crowded corners that valleys took variously were re-carved, winter after winter, until one taking remained.

Roza grew old, and a young digger asked her once why she had never taught a soul. The book, she said, taught whoever opened it, one careful man in a lifetime; a picture over a hearth teaches a houseful every day without their noticing, and what enters a man unannounced enters whole. When the guild's men finally crossed the pass to see the wells, the valley diggers met them as strangers, drew them sweet water to drink, and offered, for a fair copper, a pretty picture of a country fair.
