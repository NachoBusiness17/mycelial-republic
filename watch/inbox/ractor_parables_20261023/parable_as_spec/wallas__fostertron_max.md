# The Tale That Carried the Valley

Sela kept the town's parchments in a chest under her step, and she had read them so often she no longer needed light. Five customs, no more: the well is never locked and the rope is left wet for whoever comes after; no graft is cut from a neighbor's tree unless the owner stands beneath it; a fence found broken is mended by whoever finds it; the scale-stone at the fair sits on the buyer's side of the board; at first snow, debts under the price of a lamb are forgiven. She had watched paper die all her life — a spark, a spiteful man, a wet March — and she had watched one thing refuse to die: the stories people beg to hear twice. Soldiers burn archives; at night they beg for tales. So one autumn she took the five customs off the parchment and hid them inside a story about three brothers, a dry summer, and a well, and she told it to children as the fire was banked, as though it were only a treat.

The tale walked out of the valley on its own feet. A tinker carried it over the ford so he could be first to tell it there; a fiddler learned it for slow evenings; grandmothers kept it to buy the patience of children. No one ordered it carried, and no one searched it, because nobody searches a story for a law. It went east with the wool and south with the pilgrims, changing coats and geese as it went, and the town it described burned in a dry August, spite and a spark, the way she had feared. The chest under the step held ash.

A lifetime later, settlers came back to the valley. They found water, grass, and no law, nothing written and nothing remembered, except that everyone on the roads seemed to know a children's tale about this valley, and no two told it quite alike. Their headwoman, Ista, did not ask the roads for laws. She asked for hearers. She sent for six people who knew the tale and had never seen the valley — a ferryman, a midwife, a tinker, a shepherd, a schoolmistress, a wheelwright — and heard each of them alone, on six separate mornings, asking only: what does the tale say the town holds?

All six, who had never met, gave her the same five customs in nearly the same words: the wet rope, the standing owner, the mended fence, the buyer's stone, the snow-forgiven debts.

The tale carried one more line: that the oldest house drinks first at the spring. That line was Sela's own, stitched in late, and she had never told anyone why. The ferryman called it a liar's stitch. The schoolmistress said a tale that waters strangers does not crown the oldest roof. The tinker simply left it out, the way one leaves a rotten rung out of a ladder.

Then a man with wagons came up the road. He had wanted the valley before he ever heard the tale, and the tale handed him words for the wanting: the oldest house — his, he said, being oldest — drinks first, grazes the high meadow, speaks for the ford. Ista noticed that when he recited the tale he kept the crooked line and lost the wet rope, and she did not argue the text with him. She had watched what he wanted first, and the want had chosen his lines for him. She set him at the far end of the common, and the town went on with its water.

The tinker's fourth custom wobbled: he set the scale-stone at the seller's elbow, and no other hearer had. But his ear had already proven itself on the crooked line, so Ista judged that the tale had worn thin on his road, not that his ear was crooked, and she sent the mended tale back along the salt route with his blessing.

She took the words all six had spoken alike and had them cut into the lintel over the new well-house. The rest of the tale — the youngest brother's coat, the number of geese — she left to the evenings, where it went on changing and was none the worse. What every road had kept the same, she allowed no one to touch.

The town stood again on five customs and one story, and neither had ever been commanded anywhere. The parchments had asked to be obeyed and died asking; the tale had asked only to be wanted, and strangers carried it over the mountains without once thinking of law. Ista wondered sometimes whether Sela had trusted the tale or the appetite of strangers. By then the well was sweet, the rope was wet, and the geese, by common consent, had become seven.
