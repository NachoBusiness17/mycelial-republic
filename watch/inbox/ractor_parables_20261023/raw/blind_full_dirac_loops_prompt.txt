# The Rounds of the Fen

Ines was given her reed at the toll-house door on the morning she joined the walkers, beneath the charter cut into the lintel: ONE TOWN, EVERY PLANK TRUE. The reed was straight as a heron's leg. The tallyman scratched its shadow on the doorpost before she left, so its lean could be read against the scratch when she came back. That was the whole of her teaching: the reed, the scratch, and the rounds.

The fen held thirty-three cairns. Twenty-one planks crossed the water between them, and the planks did not make one town but twenty — twenty islets, thirteen of them single cairns standing in the reeds that no plank had ever touched. A patient eye could trace eight rounds among the planks: eight ways to leave a cairn and come back to it without retracing a step. Everything the fen asked of anybody was worked in this shape. When a fisherman brought a trouble to the toll-house, he brought it as a snarl of cord, and no one pulled ends. They looked for where the cord crossed itself, and a walker walked that crossing, reed on her palms, out over the planks and back.

Then the reading. If the reed came back with its shadow still lying along the scratch, the tallyman shut his mouth and wrote nothing; that round was already the town's, worn in like a doorstep. If the reed had turned — a finger's width, a hand's width — the turning was the catch of the day. He copied the lean into the tallies, and the lean pointed, as everyone knew, at a crossing the town had never laid.

Ines walked six rounds that first autumn. Six reeds came back turned. Not one came back true. They hung on the toll-house wall in a row, and men came to look at them the way they looked at weather.

The horn governed everything. It hung on its own stand inside the door, older than the planks, and no walker left or returned in silence — one note going out, one coming in, the second flatter than the first by exactly the lean of the reed. The tallyman kept the horn in the tallies too, entered among the cairns as if it were a stone, and he kept the tallies themselves in the tallies, page by page, each page numbered and that number written down. Ines noticed that the pages about the horn, and the pages about the pages, now ran longer than the pages about the planks. She said so once. The tallyman said the book was a cairn like any other, and kept writing.

When a reed came back turned, no second walker was sent out to walk the same round and see whether the lean repeated. And no mason ever opened the tallies. The horn called the count at dusk; the town came down to the water to hear the number and went home. In living memory the note had summoned counters, and counters only. The six missing planks stayed missing.

The walkers themselves were the guild's true work, though no one said so. A man who carried a reed for a season could not afterward cross a room without pacing its edges. They read a table, a net, an argument, a marriage; anywhere the cord crossed itself, they itched to walk it. The old ones sat by the fire with their fingers moving on their knees, tracing rounds in the air of a fen they no longer crossed.

The first walker was the grandmother of all of it, and the fire had taken even her name. In the fever year the toll-house burned to the waterline and every paper went to ash — the maps, the tallies, the charter itself, copied four times and burned four times. But her rounds did not burn. The walkers had chanted them at the fires each night, turn by turn, plank by plank, and the chant walked out of the flames in forty mouths. What the town held on paper it lost; what it held in one another it kept. The first walker's name survived only inside the chant, at the turning of the third round, where the voices still carried it.

On the last night of autumn Ines did not strike the horn. She stood at the fire and chanted the six rounds that had turned her reed — cairn to cairn, the missing crossings spoken flat where the planks should have been — the way the first walker's rounds were kept. The chant moved among the walkers like water finding level. By first light, bundles of cut reed stood at the first missing crossing, and by noon there were men at the second, laying plank, and nobody had called them. The horn stayed on its hook. In the toll-house the tallyman opened the tallies to a fresh page, wrote the date, put the pen down, and went out to the water.

# The Six Missing Spans

I reached the fen town in a spring of rains. My trade is quiet work: I sit with people and listen to what their hands do twice, so I stayed.

Petitioners came to the toll-house each morning carrying snarls of cord, some small as a fist, some heavy as a sleeping cat, and not one could be pulled straight. The elders took each snarl, felt it all over, and never tugged an end. That was the first thing I learned without being told. A trouble is a ball, their hands said; you enter it, you do not unwind it. Follow where it leads, and if the following returns you to the place you entered, the ball has made a round, and a round can be read.

The town kept its knowing out in the water. Each settled thing — this ford drowns oxen in March, that roof takes the south wind — stood as a stone, and where one knowing leaned on another, a plank joined the two. Walkers crossed the planks with straight wands on their shoulders. To walk a round was to leave a stone and arrive, plank after plank, at the stone you left; at arrival the wand was read, how it lay at departure against how it lay at return, and the wand's word was the round's worth, notched and said aloud.

A whole round, so walked, was finished. The eldest walker answered it — held — cut the word into the first stone, and no one walked it again except to show a child what finished means. Whole rounds rest.

What did not rest were the six. Six rounds wanted their last span: the planks ran, the stones stood ready, and between one stone and its return the water lay open. A walker came to that shore, stood, and went home with the wand unread. The custom was older than the planks: when a round wants its span, the bell calls all hands, and the span is laid by everyone together, never by one pair. The bell had not been rung in years. Instead, every spring, the walkers walked the six broken rounds, stood at the same six shores, said the same half of a word — the syllable cut off at the same shore, walker after walker, year after year — and went home. I wrote the syllable down. My trade is with such syllables.

The counter kept the tally in a stilt-house at the fen's rim. Her book said thirty-three stones, twenty-one planks, and twenty of the stones standing alone in the water, joined to nothing — twenty small islands of knowing that touched none of the rest. I rowed to one and read a whole certainty, perfectly said, useless for standing alone. Her book said more: eight rounds in the fen that no joining of shorter walks could counterfeit, six wanting their span, none whole. Nothing in the town had yet come all the way home.

I asked who walks behind the first walker to read the wand a second time. She looked at me as though I had asked who sings behind the singer; no second feet had ever checked the tally. I asked who had ever come from outside to be answered by a round — the ford-keeper guesses his floods by his knees, and nobody has brought him a word. The book answers no one, she agreed, and I let it lie.

Her house stood on the last stone, and the last stone said only: here stands the counting house. When she counts the stones she counts her own floor. She built the book from a song her grandmother sang — the one thing of the older house that crossed the burning year, when the town's papers were raked into the fen and lit, everything written lost, everything sung kept.

I did not scold; I never do. I asked my one question, the one I ask every town: what keeps returning, and what does it ask for? She was quiet a long while. Then she took the bell rope, and I took the other end, and we rang it — in that town two honest hands are all hands — and the two of us, the smallest crowd the custom allows, laid one green plank across the nearest missing span.

The eldest walker went out at dawn, walked the round whole, and the wand came home as it left. Held, she said, and cut it, and the feet that had returned to that shore every spring simply went there no more; they went to the next water. By nightfall the grandmothers sang the new line, by morning the children sang it wrong and were corrected, and the correction crossed the water the way everything kept in that town crosses it — by being said again.

# The String [-]

Every trouble in the coast town was carried to the string hall before anyone dared judge it. The keeper tied what he heard: a peg driven for each thing named, a string stretched for every "is tied to," until the grievance stood on the long board as a small tangle a person could walk through with her fingers. The fishers' quarrel over the wrecked weir became nine pegs and eleven strings. The widow's boundary became four pegs and three. Nothing was settled until it had been tied.

Anke was the walker. She began at one peg and followed strings only, never stepping off them, until the path either died under her or delivered her back at the peg she had left. Only arrival paid. And not every arrival: a ring that could be assembled out of rings already walked earned nothing, however long the night; a ring stood for a stone only when it stood on its own strings. Then she held out her hand, and the hall gave her a stone, and what the walking returned was cut into it.

The shape of the return was fixed by custom older than the boards. A ring with every string in place, beginning meeting end, paid a smooth stone, and smooth meant already held: the thing enclosed owed nothing and taught nothing, the town owned it the way it owns a wall. A ring that fell one string short of closing paid a notched stone, and the notch sat exactly where the missing string belonged. The apprentices lived on notches. A gap with a notch pointing at it was the only gap they closed, because a gap no one can point to is not a gap at all, only weather.

At the equinox the keeper counted the hall, because the hall had always been counted. That year the board carried thirty-three pegs and twenty-one strings. It fell into twenty islands, with no string crossing between them. Eight rings stood on their own strings — eight, no more, no matter how the night was used. Six of the eight were broken, each one notched. None was whole. No one living had seen a smooth stone.

The newest strings were the hall's own. Three winters back, the keeper had driven a peg for the count of pegs and a peg for the count of strings, and strung them to the board and back again. Since then, Anke passed among records of her own walking as she walked; her rounds were pegged while she made them, and the equinox count counted the counting too. She asked him once who had wanted this. No one had wanted it. It had been there to be done, and what is there to be done in a hall of string gets done. She asked what the count of the count would serve. He showed her the shelf.

The shelf ran the north wall, and on it lay every stone the hall had ever issued, in dated rows, back past the memory of anyone. No one came to the shelf. The fishers had long since mended the weir themselves, with green wood, and it held; the widow had hired her own surveyor. The stones went to the shelf because stones went to the shelf. And no one had ever walked behind Anke to see whether her stones said true — the count was trusted because it was a count, and the method kept its own books about its own books.

The first hall had burned in the clearing year. The provost ordered every paper of the method into the fire, and the paper obeyed. But the old mother had been a girl in that hall, and each winter she recited the whole walking-song to the apprentices, word for word, and the second hall was raised on her mouth alone. What the fire cleared, the recitation carried.

One evening, with the equinox stones in her apron, Anke asked the keeper — not unkindly, which was worse — what all of it made inevitable, and who had chosen that. He had rows; he had no answer. So she did one small thing and one small refusal. She carried the six notched stones down the hill to the harbor and the boundary, and let the stringers work where a torn ring hurt somebody. And she declined to shelve the stones of the count-of-the-count; when they were cut, she left them on the board, where anyone could watch them going nowhere. The keeper kept counting. She did not fight him.

What she did not doubt was the walk. A ring walked honestly returns what it returns, and a notch is a true report of an absence. In the spring after her descent to the harbor, one strung gap closed the widow's ring, and the hall cut the first smooth stone in living memory. Anke carried it past the shelf, past the hall, to the widow's door — already held, owed nothing — and the widow set it in her garden wall. The old mother died that spring with the apprentices reciting the song back to her, word for word, and the hall burned nothing.

# The Cellar of Circles

Every trouble that came to the house was tied once, knotted, and hung from its own peg in the cellar beams, and when one trouble was found to lean on another, the keeper stretched a cord between their pegs. Over the years the beams gathered thirty-three pegs and twenty-one cords, and the web hung mostly apart from itself: single pegs, joined pairs, twenty clusters that never touched, and only in a few dark places, where the shape of the house's griefs curved back toward their beginnings, did cord cross cord.

The grandmother had taught that a hanging trouble is not understood by looking. On her evenings the apprentice was sent down with the lamp and the rule of the hand: lay fingers on a cord and go where it goes, peg to peg, until the run either breaks or returns. When a run did return — when the apprentice came back to the very peg she had left — she did not report the cords. She reported the turning: which way her body faced now, against which way she had set out. That turning was the only thing the walk made, and it was carried up the stairs like water, carefully, and spilled nowhere.

On the far beam hung the iron rings from the grandmother's grandmother, cast whole in the forge. Walking an iron ring always brought a walker back facing exactly as she had set out, empty-handed, and the house said of those rings that they were owned. No one was sent to walk them anymore; there was nothing in them to bring up the stairs.

The web's other circles were not whole. A run of cord would go so far, and the shape of it would plainly want to curve home, and then the cord would end at a peg with nothing continuing, a missing span of dark air between one hook and the hook that would complete the return. Over each such gap the household had nailed a board, and the boards were kept clean. Visitors were brought down and shown the gaps as though they were windows, and in recent years the household had begun to call them the house's openness, and visitors went away envying it. The grandmother had said the gaps were the house being asked to lay the next length. No length had ever been laid.

One winter the apprentice counted the cellar honestly, following every crossing with her lamp. The tangle held eight circles — she traced the possibilities with her fingers until no ninth could be found — while the keeper's book of counts named six, every one of them broken, and not one whole ring anywhere in the house. She said eight in the kitchen. The keeper corrected her to six, gently, as one corrects a child, and no one walked behind her to see; the six were received the way weather is received.

In the corner of the cellar stood the finding hoop the keeper had built in a hungry year, a ring of wood with cords drawn across it, made because the work needed a thing that could show where circles hid. A wet evening, the apprentice traced every cord on the hoop, and every run of it led around the hoop and back into the hoop, so that what it found, always, was itself. It had never once been matched against a walked circle in the beams. She said so at table: it finds itself, and we have been calling that finding. The keeper's face did something small and wrong, and the apprentice was not sent down for a week, and the hoop was oiled.

Every turning she had ever carried up had been said aloud and dropped into the clay bowl by the door, as the custom required. The bowl had never been emptied. No cook had ever taken a word from it; nothing baked in the house had ever been salted with one. Beside the bowl, against the wall, stood a small chair that the grandmother said was meant for whoever would taste the turnings and name the spoiled ones. The chair had never been sat in. It wore a cloth over it, like furniture in a dead room.

When the apprentice asked how the walking had come through the great clearing — the year the shelves were carried out and every written pattern burned — the grandmother laughed in a way that was mostly not laughing. Paper burns, she said. The walking had lived that year in the mouths of the kneading women, hummed over the dough at dawn, and it came back up out of the ashes a little bent and still true. What the house had stored, it lost; what it had recited, it kept.

In the spring the apprentice went down alone, walked one broken circle as far as the last peg, and stood before the board nailed over the missing span. She took the board down and set it against the wall, and measured the dark air with a cord of her own, and cut the length, and left it lying on the floor beneath the gap — laid nothing, fixed nothing. Then she went up and took the cloth off the small chair and set the bowl in front of it. In the morning the household found the open gap, the cut cord, the bare chair, and did not know what any of it was asking, and the keeper came down last, and counted, and stopped at seven, and did not say the eighth.

# The Rounds of the Salt Fen

Matters came to the salt fen the way weather came, and the Order of the Staff received each one as it arrived: a tangle of cord, wet and pulled tight by whoever had carried it. The doctrine of the house was plain and was taught to every apprentice on the first morning. A tangle is only thread, and thread can be followed. What must be found in it are the rounds — paths that leave a stone, cross by the bridges, and come back to the stone they left, never putting a foot on the same plank twice. And a round counts for the house only if it cannot be walked by joining shorter rounds end to end, because a round made of other rounds has already been paid for.

When a walker came back to her stone she planted her staff in the mark she had cut before leaving, and the staff leaned — a finger's width east, or west — and the lean was written in the rolls. That was what a round gave. Not the far side of the fen, which anyone could see for nothing, but the tilt in the returning step. Two walkers sent around the same ground came back leaning the same way, and that agreement was how the house knew the ground had spoken and not the walker.

Some rounds lay whole. Where the crossing was old — where the mortar had forgotten being laid — the staff came back upright, and the walker had learned nothing the house did not already hold; those rounds were left to the weather and marked settled. But when a walker came to black water where the far span should have been, she did not guess a crossing and she did not swim. She marked the gap on her slate and turned back, and the apprentices were woken before light and sent out with planks, because a broken round was the fen's way of asking to be joined.

That autumn the Tallyman called the numbers as his line had always called them: thirty-three stones standing above the water, twenty-one bridges, and the stones gathered into twenty companies, most of them a single stone alone, crossed by nothing at all. Out of the stones, the bridges, and the companies his figures made eight rounds, no more, no fewer. The walkers went out to walk what the figures promised. Six came back with slates marked at missing spans. Not one staff came back upright. The last two rounds no one could find, and the Tallyman did not amend his figures, because figures, he said, were what the house kept instead of luck.

Among the eight, one round was the founder's own insistence: the walk that measured walking. It began at the rolls, went out by the rule of the staff, and returned to the rolls, so that the house's way of examining was entered among the things to be examined. The walkers walked it like the others and found it broken like the others, though its missing spans were strange ones. One gap stood where a second order of walkers should have been, re-walking a round to dispute a lean; no such order had ever come. The other gap stood where a use should have waited; the returns were bound each year and set in the drawer under the rolls, and no dike-master, no farmer, no court ever sent for the volumes or moved a ditch by a hair on their account. The walkers saw both gaps plainly, named them on the slate, and the slate went into the drawer with the rest.

The founder's own survey had burned in the year the records room caught from a lamp. What survived was not on any shelf. At dusk the oldest walkers stood on the bridges and said the survey aloud, and the apprentices repeated it until the words stood in them like stones in water. The rolls admitted only written leans, signed by sworn staffs; so the one survey that had outlived the fire was, by the house's own rule, the one that could not be entered.

A reed-cutter crossed the fen every day of her life and knew every eaten span by the ache in her calves. She told the walkers where the water had taken the crossings, and they thanked her, and nothing she said was written, because she carried no staff. Still the apprentices learned the founder's words out on the bridges, saying them over in the dark. In the drawer the slates lay squared: six gaps named, eight rounds promised, no staff upright. The house kept its count of the fen, and the fen kept everything else.

# The Copper Rings

When a matter came to the guild, the porter tied a tangle in the great cord that hung the length of the hall, and the matter was not finished until the tangle was cut out and swept. That was the whole of the law, and nobody remembered who had tied the first.

The guild had burned once. The fire took the shelves, the deeds, and the street plans, and what the fire missed, the magistrates' wagons carried away to be pulped. One grandmother stayed through both, and afterward she could walk the town blindfolded. She recited the streets each morning to the apprentices — here was the fountain, here is the fountain — and her voice was the only record that had outlived its paper.

The guild hired Marthe to draw the town anew. She counted thirty-three wells and twenty-one streets still running between them, and the streets were so few that the wells fell into twenty neighborhoods, most of them a lone well with no street at all. She engraved the wells as dots and the streets as lines on a copper plate, and when she counted the ways a walker might leave a well and come back to it without ever turning around, the count came to eight.

The guild kept a rite older than any plate. To judge a circle you walked it, a pole on your shoulder, the pole's shadow held on one fixed side of you, no turning at the crossings. When you came back to your starting stone you looked at the pole: what the shadow had gained, the circle had paid you. If the shadow lay exactly where it began, the circle was old ground — the town had long since absorbed it, and it would teach nothing further. And if there was no walking it at all, if the street broke and the far side could be reached only by stepping off the plate, then the break itself was the matter, and the matter was for the guild to close with brick.

The rite stood on the copper too. Marthe had drawn her own table among the wells — dot thirty-three — and one of the eight circles ran through the map-room, around the table on which the plate lay. Whoever judged that circle would carry the paper while walking around the paper.

No one had walked any of them. The circles had been counted, engraved, and gilded at the rim; the apprentices recited them at supper; and the book of readings lay unopened, because no mason, no magistrate, no merchant had ever asked what a walked circle pays.

The town admired the copper. Pilgrims came up from the coast to see it, eight perfect rings on a green plate, and they stepped over the bricked alley and around the broken bridge to reach it, and the wells they drank at on the way were dry.

Marthe took the pole and went out at first light. By dusk she had this: six of the eight circles were broken — a bridge out, an alley bricked shut, a street that stopped dead at the cistern and resumed beyond it. Of the rest, one ran only around the map-room table, and the last passed through a courtyard locked since the fire. Not one circle was whole in the town; not one shadow had been turned; the plate paid nothing it had not drawn for itself.

She laid the pole on the magistrates' table. They wanted the copper hung higher, lit by two more lamps; the pilgrims were expected at harvest. Marthe asked for bricklayers. A drawn circle, she said, is either a promise of a street or a lie about one; the town held eight of them outstanding — six payable in brick, two payable in scraping.

The guild argued through the autumn. The grandmother died in the first frost, reciting to the end, and the apprentices finally wrote her streets down, badly, from memory of her memory — a copy of a copy, though it still named wet stones the copper had made dry.

In the spring the apprentices went out with poles and stakes. Where a circle broke they planted a stake with a red rag, so the gap could be seen from a distance instead of admired up close. Where a circle ran only around the map-room, Marthe scraped its dot from the plate and moved the table to the margin, noted like any other object that happens to sit in a room. A circle would be walked, or it would not be drawn. The cord in the hall took new tangles daily; the cut ends were swept weekly; and what the sweep missed, the apprentices could still recite.

# The Rounds of the Standings

The town stood on thirty-three standings in shallow salt water, plank bridges strung between some of them and none between most. Troubles came to it the way weather came, and the grandmothers at the kilns taught every child the same first lesson: a trouble is a snarl, and nobody in the history of the town was ever paid for pulling a snarl apart. The work was to find what the snarl hid — returns, ways out that come home again by water the walker never crossed on the way out — and to walk them.

Alba learned early that a return gives its truth only to the whole foot. Leave Mosspoint by the north stringer, cross the two standings beyond, come back to Mosspoint by the south stringer, and only the arrival tells you what the round carries: the give of the third plank, the depth at the middle crossing, things no one could know by standing still on Mosspoint or by any single stretch alone. Half a return teaches nothing. The truth lives in the coming-back.

Her teacher had held the lesson in two hands. This hand, the resting one: a return already whole hands back only what the town has kept for generations, and nobody owes it a walk. This hand, pointed at black water: a return broken — two standings whose home-stretch was never built — is a promise the town made to itself, and to that missing stretch the barge must go with planks, because until the return can be walked its truth stays drowned. The teacher said the town had twice gone under for forgetting which hand was which.

On the twenty-seventh morning of the harvest month the tally-keeper chalked the boathouse door. Thirty-three standings fit to bear weight. Twenty-one bridges sound. Twenty neighborhoods, standings that touched none but their own. And by the old arithmetic — bridges taken, standings given back, neighborhoods put in — eight returns, counting only such returns as could not be pieced together out of the parts of other returns. Eight, said the chalk, in a steady hand.

The walkers went out at low tide and came back at dusk with salt to the knee. Every one of the six returns they had walked was broken somewhere — a missing stretch, open water where a coming-home should run — and not one round in all the Standings was whole. Of the chalk's eight, two no foot ever found to walk.

Pello the crier stood on the fish-steps and called the count instead of the walking. Eight, he cried, a town of eight, cross freely, the crossings keep themselves. The crowd went easy in its shoulders, and the menders' barge stayed tied to its post, and Pello's own ferry took fares at both landings. Alba asked, as she always asked, who was lighter when this was believed, and watched the answer cross his palm in copper. At supper the town said eight, because eight could be said between spoonfuls; the six, each with its black stretch of water, needed the whole evening, and supper is short.

One work the town had never let drown: the crossing-craft itself. The bridge-wright kept her drawings on a standing of their own, and the bridges there ran strangest of all, for the plan of the rounds was strung into a round of its own — out by the second plan, home by the first, arriving where you began, at the making of the plan. Every other craft had gone into the water; the one that circled back on itself was the one still above it.

The menders, dripping on the boathouse steps, gave their verdict on the six: two lacks, and no third. No office existed for proving a finished stretch — no walker had ever been sent back across a built return to learn whether it still held. And no buyer had ever come from any far shore asking for what a walked return hands back; the truths came home and sat unsold along the wall.

All of it the grandmothers had carried before any chalk. The first craft had gone down when the old town drowned, every line of it, wax and board and bone, and it lived anyway, because at the kilns the old women had told it whole to children who told it again; the telling was the one record the water could not reach. Alba had all of it by mouth before she ever set a plank.

In the morning the barge went out after all, loaded at her asking, and made for the nearest missing stretch, where the water lay open in the shape of a return. The grandmothers watched it go, called the children close, and told them, again, the whole of it by heart.

# 灯笼与六环

在小镇的边缘，有一个侧放着的泥罐，里面住着一个名叫 Ilma 的女人，她拥有一只碗、一件外套，还有一盏她经常在正午时分点燃并提在手里的灯笼。孩子们问她，在光天化日之下，她期望找到什么。“一个完整的圆环（ring），”她说，然后继续走自己的路。

这个小镇最近为圆环而疯狂，原因如下。问题总是像缠绕的绳索一样来到井边，而关于绳结的古老法则，仍然由一位老祖母在大多数早晨向前来打水的人吟诵。法则说：顺着绳结找到它的两端。如果它能聚拢成一个闭合的圆环，那么这个东西就属于你了：一个闭合的圆环，无论是今年、明年，对陌生人还是对小孩，对每一个走完它的人来说，答案都是一样的。如果它无法闭合，不要为缺失的那段而哭泣；缺失的那段是一个差事（errand）。铺好那段路，圆环就会开始产生回报。

祖母的口述是这条法则得以幸存的唯一地方。记录厅在大扫除的那一年被烧毁了，这种古老行走方法的每一份书面副本都化为了灰烬。大火无法触及的，正是那位老妇人不愿放下的东西。

年轻人希望在不失去祖母耐心的情况下尊重这条法则，于是他们中的一位轮匠（wheelwright）制作了一个可以计数的轮子。沿着路推它，它会发出咔哒声，这些声音表明了这条路是什么。然后，他们勘察了小镇西边的沼泽地，小镇把它的认知像沼泽保存石头一样保存在那里：33块石头立在黑色的水面上，这里一块那里一块地由21块木板连接起来。木板稀少且分散，以至于这些石头形成了20个独立的立石群（stands），大多数都孤零零地矗立着，不与任何东西接触。在那种孤独中，理论上可以构建出八个圆环。轮匠在一块木板上画了六个，每一个都是同样的故事：一条路从一块石头出发，跨过一块又一块木板延伸到水面上，却在离家仅差一段的地方停了下来。六个期望（promises）。然而，在沼泽地的任何地方，都没有一个圆环闭合过，因此，小镇对沼泽地的了解，还没有一件是那种能给出两次相同答案的东西。

在勘察图中，在这三十三块石头中，立着一块不是石头的石头：那是放置计数轮本身的架子，上面钉着一块木板，除了绕回到轮子下面之外，通向任何地方。

这个轮子从未被检验过。东边的路上有一段路程，每个孩子都通过步数和栅栏熟记于心，但没有人把轮子推到那上面去，听听它是否能准确地咔哒出那段距离。给轮子做一个第二层架子（放荣誉）要比检验它简单得多。每天晚上，它的计数都被写出来，钉在广场的柱子上，而柱子立在那里的效果却最没人阅读。没有船因为它们而划动，没有田垄因为它们而耕种，也没有因为那些计数而烤出面包。镇上的狗，作为诚实的经济学家，在新的计数被公布时，甚至头都不抬一下。

Ilma 走了那六条画出的路中最长的一条，直到它能到达的最远端。她带着一把芦苇回到了自己的家门口，脸转向了她从未面对过的一片天空，她说：这就是一个完整的圆环能带来的。它给予了你能握在手中的东西，并改变了你。它所缺少的那一段，正是工作的入口，而入口正是用来进食（喂养）的。

黄昏时分，她发现轮匠正在灯光下擦拭轮子，邻居们则站成一圈围观赞赏它。她把她燃烧的灯笼举到了灯光下。邻居们大笑起来，笑声为她腾出了空间。

“你是在要求这个镇子的人们站成一个圈来赞赏，”她说。“你今天就可以停止这种请求。把你的轮子推到那段已知的路程上，让我们知道它是否在说谎（误差）。在你那六条路中的任何一条上，铺好那缺失的一段，并沿着这个圆环走到你自己家门口的另一边（即从另一侧接近家门口）。然后，把它产生的东西摆在公共桌子上，让进食本身成为掌声。一个圆环不需要追捧者（admirers）。它需要的是走完它的人（walkers）。”

邻居们纷纷散去吃晚饭。轮匠看着他的木板，上面六条路都在离家一段的地方停了下来，而她并没有为此安慰他。“你是故意让它们保持未完成状态的，”她说。“一个期望（promise）不会像一个已完成的事物那样让你失望。这就是白昼（daylight）存在的意义。”

她回到了她的泥罐中。她只在太阳完全落山后才吹灭灯笼，因为它就像所有真诚（honest）的东西一样，只在白天工作。

# The Eight Rounds

The town stood on thirty-three mounds scattered through a fog lagoon, joined here and there by twenty-one bridges, so that the whole place made twenty neighborhoods that never touched, and no one living had seen it all at once. The fog was old there. When strangers asked when it would lift, the townsfolk said fog was fog, the way water is wet, and went on with their oars.

Every trouble in that country went up to the counting-house on the ninth mound. Whatever a household brought — a boundary quarrel, a drowned ox, a disputed bride-price — the warden on duty tied it into the office cord, one twist apiece, and the cord hung by the door and grew heavier every year. Beside the threshold lay a flat stone with a fan of grooves cut by the founders, and a child who asked about it was told only not to trip.

The town's other habit was the rounds. A round left the counting-house door, crossed bridges, and came home to the same door without crossing any bridge twice. The walker carried the ash pole with a red eye painted on one face, and set out with the eye toward the door; at homecoming the eye had wandered — toward the kiln, the heron rock, the open water — and wherever it had wandered was written in the office book as that walk's answer. A stroll that returned by its own outgoing path was not a round, and nothing was written. A round whose eye came to rest along one of the stone's grooves was called settled, and settled rounds were not walked again.

The ferrymen sang that there were eight rounds and no more: whatever walk a clever child invented came apart, watched long enough, into those eight or several of them strung end to end, and no ninth had ever been found. They had sung the rounds since before any paper. In the burning year every chart in town had gone to the kilns by order from over the water, and the chart now hanging in the office had been drawn afterward by Petra's grandmother, from the singing.

That season two wardens kept the office and shared one skiff: Jonas, the elder, who was never long in any room, and Petra, the younger, who could not pass a drawing without a pencil.

On the seventh mound stood the dead light-tower, the only thing that rose above the fog. One still morning Jonas climbed it and saw whole what no one below had seen: the mounds in their scatterings, the threads of the bridges, and — wherever the chart promised a span — the black breaches where no plank had ever been laid, the water running straight through the ink. He came down dazzled, and for a day could not tell rope from rail, and learned his own home bridges again with his feet.

Petra walked the rounds against the chart. Six of the eight were broken, each somewhere missing a span, and at every breach she had to turn about, and a pole that is turned about keeps its eye where it started, so she wrote nothing. The custom was plain: a warden chalked the breach, and apprentices owed planks before the next fog; none had come in three seasons, and the chalk had gone grey. The last two rounds were whole, but no one had walked them since the burning. And no round in the book had ever been written twice, no walker having followed another to see whether the eye wandered the same way twice; the answers piled page over page, no barge ever called to carry them off, and the fisherfolk steered by the heron rock and the smell of the kiln.

At fog's end the reeve crossed the longest bridge to shut the book. Zero settled, he said; a losing count; the office could stop pretending. Jonas asked him to climb the tower first, and Petra climbed behind, and the three of them stood in the clear light above the fog and marked, together, where the ink promised what the water refused.

Then Petra did not argue the count; she drew. She re-inked the six breaches as spans awaiting hands, a date chalked beside each. She set two fresh eye-marks on the two whole rounds, one walker to go first and a second to follow. She drew the office threshold honestly as a crossing, the counting-house being itself one of the thirty-three, its doings part of the country it counted. And into the cord she tied one thin twist for the counting of the counting-house. The reeve watched her pencil the whole way down and said, at the door, that the book could stay open another year.

By first frost planks were going down across the widest breach, and the book held one round written twice, the two answers sitting a hand apart on the page. The fog stayed. The planks went down anyway.

# The Tokens No One Wants

In the fen country, every hard question is a tangle of ways, and the custom is older than the moot hall: no tangle is answered by looking at it. You take boots and a staff and you walk. Some of the ways curl back on themselves, so that a walker who steps off a boundary stone at first light comes to that same stone by dusk with the dust of the whole round on her boots. The round pays as it is walked. At the stone she presses a clay token, and the face that rises is the day's turning — how far the road swung her before it brought her home.

Where a round is whole — every plank sound, every bridge in — the face never differs. Generation after generation mints the same one, and a single token of each such round hangs nailed above the moot-hall door, put there before any living walker was born. Walking a whole round is pleasant and teaches nothing. But where a plank has rotted or the water has taken a bridge, the walker comes home empty-handed and wet to the knee, and the custom is plain about that too: the missing stretch is the errand. Masons are fed where the way is absent. The fen does not pity a wet walker; it hands him timber.

The roadwright was a dry man named Ove, who counted before he walked. One season he set it all down: thirty-three boundary stones, twenty-one stretches of sound way, and the ways hanging in twenty clusters that never touch — half the stones stand alone in the sedge, a meeting of ways with nothing meeting. From the arithmetic alone he could say the fen held eight rounds. He sent walkers out. Six of the eight could be attempted at all, and of the six, not one was whole. The lintel of the moot hall got no new nail.

Ove's counting-house stood where the ways curl. Whichever gallery a man took through his premises, he came out by the door he went in, and the eighth round ran through Ove's own gate. Below his garden the ford was out, crossed on a single private plank laid for one man's legs. No mason had ever been fed there. The trade of rounds sat on a round, and kept its own break the way a man keeps a limp he has stopped mentioning.

What each party wanted was no secret. The masons wanted named breaks, because a named break was wages. The walkers wanted the closing of what they had begun; a broken round itched in them like a song stopped a bar short, and they would walk for the itch alone, paid or not — and Ove's trade rode that itch, naming six breaks and keeping the walkers moving without ever buying anything from them. Ove wanted his eight believed. The town wanted herring, salt, and a dry road to market, and no one standing in that road had once asked the price of a token. The shelf of them grew, mud-colored, each with its turning, taken on faith — for there was no assayer in the fen either; nobody had ever walked a round twice to see whether the face held.

In the grandmother Greet's time the water rose and took every paper in the fen — the maps, the tallies, the first walker's own name. What survived survived at the wool-carding, where the old women recited the stretches until the words were smooth as pebbles. What was said aloud could not drown. The first walker herself is only a voice at the wool now, and the fen trusts her anyway; Ove's count agrees with her recitation plank for plank.

One evening at the wool a child asked what the tokens were for, and none of the grandmothers answered, and the silence was the kind that settles over things nobody has ever wanted. Ove heard it and did not answer either. But on the next market day he set the six break-names in a row on the sill where the herring-buyers pass, and stood a little apart to see whether any hand would reach. The buyers bought herring. The walkers walked. And below Ove's own garden the ford lay broken still — the one missing stretch in all the fen that had never once been said aloud at the wool.

# The Round Walks

Salt Harrow stood in its shallows: thirty-three stones, twenty-one planks of causeway, no plank longer than a shout. The stones fell into twenty companies. Within a company you could pass from stone to stone with dry boots; between companies there was only water, and the ferrymen had their living.

There were eight ways to leave a stone, keep to the planks, and come home without retracing a step. The woman who laid the first planks had left one teaching about those eight rounds — or the ferrymen said she had. Her book had gone to the pulper in the year the town weighed its papers and sold them, so what remained was the ferry-stove telling, and the kitchen telling, which differed, and the telling given to children, which was plainer than either. But all the versions carried the same center: a full circle pays a fare. A walker who completes a round arrives home speaking a word she never carried out. It is not written on the planks and not dropped by gulls. The walking mints it.

Oren kept the tally. When a fare matched a word already cut into the home stone, he called the round settled — the circle had brought back only what the town already held. When a fare named a thing with no place to stand — a crossing never laid, a stone never set — he called the round owing. An owing round was not the walker's fault and not the walker's work. What the fare pointed at belonged to the town.

At midwinter he read the count aloud on the ferry steps: thirty-three stones, twenty-one planks, twenty companies, eight rounds; six of the rounds owing, and not one settled. Then he walked back in, because the thirty-third stone was his own doorsill. The plan chalked on his wall put the tally room at the middle of the water, and the room the plan hung in was one of the things the plan counted.

No round had ever been walked twice. The first fare went into the book, and the book moved on; whether a second pair of feet would be paid the same word, no page could say, for no one had gone back to see. And once written, the fares went up to the loft over the ferry shed and stayed there. No farmer asked after them, no ferryman, no schoolmaster. The loft was opened after rains, to see if the roof held, and for no other errand, and it always held.

In spring a seed-trader came through, waiting on the ice to clear. She walked the eastern round with Oren one grey morning, stone to plank to stone, and at the last plank the fare arrived, and the word was elm-crossing. Nothing on the water answered to it. No plank was elm; no gap in the causeways wanted one, so far as anyone had measured; the word stood on nothing and pointed anyway.

At the home stone the word was heard three times. Oren wrote it down and said it was a fare misdelivered — the plank-layer had meant some other word, and the circle had carried it badly. Bree, who rowed mail between the companies, heard a commission: a crossing that wanted laying. She asked Oren which two stones it would join, and took out her pencil, and Oren had no answer ready. The trader said the circle paid nothing at all. The word was the walker's own breath, she said, gone out of her at the first step and come home wearing a coat; the town had laid its planks to hear itself, and named the echo a fare.

Oren wrote all three hearings beneath the word, because he wrote everything, and carried the book up to the loft, and the loft kept it as it kept the rest.

The trader left on the first ferry after the ice broke. She had walked one round and heard one word heard three ways, and she carried the story inland, where it was heard a fourth.

That night at the stove the ferrymen told the plank-layer again, each telling a little different from the last, and every telling found hearers, and above the shed the rain came down on the loft roof, and the fares lay dry beneath it.

# The String Room

Odile came up the river to the dyers' hall with clean hands and a small slate. She had audited for thirty years and took the work the way other people took cures: alone, early, on an empty stomach. The string room sat at the back, behind the vats, and down its center ran a long board of black oak on which the hall kept its knots.

Every petitioner who sat one day with the hall about a question tied one knot to the board — one knot for one sitting, no more — and when the question was answered, or the petitioner gave up and went home, the knot stayed. A man who had come three times about his brother's will had left three knots in a row, a small account of his patience. Nothing on the board was ever untied. Where two knots asked after the same matter, the warden of strings ran a thread of dyed wool between them.

When threads led out of a knot and, followed hand over hand, came back to it, the route was called a circuit, and the hall walked it formally, once. The walker carried the hazel staff with the white mark at its head and turned it a quarter-turn in her palms at every knot she passed, as the rule-post required. Back at her start, she read where the mark pointed — the door, the ceiling, her own shoulder — and that direction was the circuit's answer, cut into the tablet by the door. "A circuit that walks whole is a settled thing," the warden said, tapping the stone. "Its reading is here already, and always was. It is the broken ones that are owed." He unrolled the walkers' map, where six circuits were drawn in ink, each with a red bead at an empty span, where the route wanted a thread that had never been strung. Those spans were the hall's debt: wool spun, span strung, walk finished, so the circuit could be asked what it carried.

Odile took the numbers herself. Thirty-three knots. Twenty-one threads. The threads had not made the board one country but twenty small thickets, knots strung to near neighbors with provinces of bare oak between. She used the reckoning her mother had taught her at the loom: take the threads, take away the knots, give back the thickets; the remainder is the number of circuits hiding in the frame. Twenty-one, less thirty-three, plus twenty. She turned the slate so the warden could see the eight. His map named six. Two circuits somewhere on that board had never been drawn by anyone; the warden could not point to them and did not argue, because the arithmetic was the arithmetic. And of the six the map did name, every one carried its red bead; not one circuit on the whole board walked unbroken.

One knot near the rule-post was the warden's own, tied the day he took his office, and its question was the board itself: whether the keeping was well done. Its circuit ran from that knot to the rule-post and back and through no other question. In the tablet's margin, beside that circuit, a later hand had cut: the mending of this one is known to me, and the showing of it would not fit this edge. "Then the tablet says only that it is claimed," Odile said. "Not that it is done." The warden dusted the edge with his sleeve and said nothing.

She asked to see the second walks, the re-walkings by which the first readings had been checked. There were none. In the hall's whole history no circuit had been walked twice; no one had ever followed behind a walker to see whether the staff turned truly. She asked who came for the answers — which merchants, which petitioners had ever sent for a reading. No one ever had. The readings lay cut in the stone, were dusted on feast days, and that was everything that had ever been done with them.

At supper the walkers recited. There had been an older hall before the fire, where the tannery stood, and an older board, and all of it had burned — knots, threads, the tablet of that day. But the walkers of that time had made a habit of saying one circuit aloud each evening, knot by knot, turn by turn, and what is held in mouths does not burn. They said it still, every evening over the stew, though not one thread of it existed anywhere but in the saying. Odile listened to the whole of it, twice.

Before she left she took the staff and walked the nearest of the six until her hands found the red bead and the empty air beyond it. She stood at the break, the staff at its quarter-turn, and the warden studied his boots. "When I come again," she said, "have one of the six walked whole, and let your apprentice do the same with another. We will lay the two readings side by side with no names on either, and let strangers say which circuit gave which. Bring me the staff, not the story." Then, because she had sat one day in the hall about one matter, she tied a single knot of her own and strung it to the warden's knot, her question and his being neighbors now, and went down the river with her slate.
