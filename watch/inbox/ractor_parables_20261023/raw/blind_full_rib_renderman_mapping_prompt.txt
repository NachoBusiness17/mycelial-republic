# The Fold at the Edge of the Sand

The trade house stood where the paved road gave out and the sand began. Its wealth had always been weight: bolts and ingots loaded onto animals and dragged over the pass. Every autumn the coastal houses held the great run to see whose load crossed first. The house entered every year, lost animals every year, buried a driver some years, and each spring the master paid the entry again, because the run was how trade had always been done and no one in the yard had thought to ask the sand about it.

Then a grandfather of the house, Ahn, walked out — not with a caravan but on foot, with water and nothing to sell. He was gone a season. He came back with his eyes ruined, having looked too long at the brightness of the far side; for weeks he could not tell a coat from its own shadow. He brought no goods, only one sentence: the desert taxes weight, and nothing else.

His sister Bree, who had never crossed, did not breed a stronger animal. She stopped paying the entry, and the run went off without the house, and nobody died of it. In place of freight she sent folds. A pattern for a coat weighs less than the coat's button; a card of marks for a whole carpet crosses the pass in a child's sleeve. The heavy looms never crossed at all. They stayed in the river towns on the cheap side, where thread and hands cost little, and wove from the cards there; the finished things were sold on that side and never saw the sand. What crossed was the description, small enough to be forgotten while it crossed.

After that, the house wrote everything the way it wrote to the weavers. An order went out as a terse card of lines and measures, readable in wind, and was fattened into cloth only where fattening was cheap. Over the counting-room door a law was cut into the lintel — carry the fold, not the cloth — and beside the words crawled a line of marks and letters that no one living could read, copied out faithfully by the carpenter because the old act had been signed that way and he did not feel entitled to shorten it.

Three portraits hang above the counting-room fire; the clerk who dusts them says every card in the house is one of those three in different ink. The first is a painter of the coast, who showed that even a picture can travel as a card of marks and be baked into color by whoever holds the card at the far end. The second is a surveyor who taught that a hill need not be walked to be named: give any standing place its distance to the hill's edge, and the hill is fully said. The third is the smallest and oldest, a steward whose houses settled themselves straight after any shaking, so long as each crooked door was allowed to trouble only its neighbors.

The riders who carry cards out and bring word back keep a custom visitors find austere: no report longer than six lines. The first line is a number; the second, what the rider claims; the third names anyone who may carry the claim off and call it his own; the fourth names the test that would kill the claim if it deserves dying; two lines more hold where the thing was seen and what it stands on. What unsettles visitors is not the custom but the page it lands on: the house has ruled its own memory in the same six lines for longer than the riders have been riding, and a slip straight off the sand drops onto the page like a coin minted for that pocket.

In the stable yard stands proof that a true thing may exist without being in force: a loft of small boxes, every lid oiled, each cut to take one bird and one word, carpentered with more care than the stalls. A bell is hung beside it for the loft's sake, and the bell's cord is still coiled on its nail. The steward passes it twice a day and has never apologized for it.

In the cold room stands a cabinet the riders may not open. Its drawers hold no cloth and no iron, only directions to forges up the far mountain: how to find each forge, how to summon its work down, how to bend a sample of it before trusting a beam of it. The forges themselves never come down. A journeyman asked once when the iron would arrive, and was told that iron arrives when there is a bridge to build and not an hour sooner. On the corner of every drawer lies a white pebble, which in that house means one voice has vouched and the house has not yet sworn.

The great run still runs every autumn on the coast road. The house pays no entry, hauls nothing, buries no one, and its cloth is worn on the far side of the sand by people who do not know it exists. In the counting room two chairs face each other across one lamp: the walker's, narrowed toward the door, and the rewriter's, turned toward the cold cabinet. Nobody who works there sits in only one of them for long.

# The Iron That Never Crossed

The house on the ridge lived by describing things it did not own. In a long room under a northern light, scribes wrote from morning to bell: short running strings of marks, each string naming a thing not by its picture but by its distances — from every point in the field, how far to the nearest surface of the thing named — and the distances turned and counted the other way once a point had crossed inside the skin. A wheel entered the house as a column of numbers, and from the column alone the wheel could be rebuilt downstream by someone who never saw it.

No one proposed a new alphabet twice. The one who ended such proposals was the keeper of pedigrees, an old officer in a pale wax mask; no one living had seen the face behind it. He argued nothing. He recited three bequests: the two brothers of the old playhouses, dead before the founders, who had made machines read whole scenes off a running string of marks; the lone carver, lately buried, who shaped bowls, arches and faces by distance alone and never once by outline; and, oldest of all, the road-surveyor who proved that walking always to the nearest unvisited town crosses a country for the least leather. Then he said: the string of distances is the custom of this house; each of those three paid for it; bring me a fourth who has paid, and we will talk. No one ever brought him a fourth.

Once a year, at the packing of the messengers, he said the other thing. The high pass took its toll by the ounce; every strip that crossed it rode a mule and was weighed like silver. So the strings went up thinned to wire, a day of road on a thumb of paper, nothing between the marks. And in the valley warehouses, where paper cost less than bread and the shelves stood empty as though famine had forgotten them, the same strings were written out wide, mark by mark across broad pages, at leisure. Small at the frontier, wide in the valley, he said, and turned back to his shelf for another year.

What the house learned, it learned in rows of six lines. In the back room the memory books stood open on iron stands: first the number, growing by one; second the finding, one sentence; third, what another house might take and use without asking leave; fourth, the test that, met, made the second line a lie; fifth and sixth, the hand and the day. A child could file a row. A stranger could read one and know at a glance how to rob it and how to ruin it.

On the table beside the stands lay a box of slips, cut to the same six lines, corners rounded by the binder's thumb, the finest object in the room. The plan was known to every soul: when the scouts came in from the roads, they would trade findings on these, number to breaking, so that a thing learned on the road would arrive already shaped like memory and slide into the books without translation. The scouts, when they came in, shouted across the courtyard, drank, argued, slept, and rode out again. The box stayed full. Visitors were shown it often, and admired it, and it gleamed.

The deepest shelf held the catalog of the far forges. No forge stood on the ridge and no iron was stored anywhere in the house. Each entry carried exactly three things: where the forge stood, past the pass, by road and landmark; the words of summons that brought its fire across the distance; and the small test by which the arriving fire could be told from any other. Across the first page, where a preface would go, the house had written in fair hand: the metal stays where it lies; nothing heavy crosses the pass until a job calls for it, and then only what the job eats. In the margin, smaller, in the head clerk's hand: proposed; the seal has not been set.

A job came up from the town: a bridge to be drawn before the spring floods. A scribe opened the catalog, found the entry, spoke the summons; fire arrived; the strings of the bridge ran off warm; the town paid, and crossed. No one in the house ever saw so much as a horseshoe of that iron, and no one needed to.

Marta, the youngest of the scribes, took her walking holiday east. Where entry forty-one put the forge she found grass, low stones, the rusted shoes of a wall, a ruin her grandfather could not have seen burning. The shepherd on the hill said no fire had stood in that field since his father's father. She walked home slowly and told the room. The room thanked her. That same evening the sister of the bridge commission arrived; the scribe opened forty-one, spoke the summons, and the fire came, and the work was done, and done well, and the long room smelled of hot iron. No one had ever been east but her.

She filed her row in the memory book with the rest: the number; the finding, one sentence; the taking — any reader may reuse the road east; the breaking — walk it and stand in the field; her hand; the day. It was the truest row in the book that year. It was consulted twice, both times by her. In spring the flood commissions came in again, and she stood in the doorway of the long room and watched forty-one being opened, and did not stop it. Afterwards the box of slips was dusted, and shone.

# The Lantern Works

Odile came to the Lantern Works at the beginning of winter, looking for work she could not yet name. The works made everything — lamps, figures, whole rooms of glass — yet no shelf in the place held a sample. What the works kept instead were tellings.

In the first hall sat the caster, an old man gone blind long before Odile was born. He could not read a drawing, and so the house had learned, generations back, to speak its wants to him line by line: first the light and where it stood, then each vessel and what it was made of, then the surfaces, near to far, every line naming only what should appear and where it should stand. He never asked to touch a finished thing. What he wanted was to be told, exactly, and the whole house had been shaped by that wanting; every order written in the works was a scene spoken aloud for a maker, because the first maker among them had wanted nothing else for himself.

The second hall belonged to the measurer. She never walked an edge to learn a shape. She stood at a point and called out its distance from every neighboring surface — this near the floor, that far from the wall — and the shape arrived out of the distances without anyone tracing it. Odile asked her why she worked this way. "An inside I have not measured," the measurer said, "is an inside that can surprise me," and she went back to her calling.

Above the counting-room door hung four frames, though visitors were told to speak of three lines of descent. The widest frame held two men painted shoulder to shoulder, and under it the year 1988 — the brothers of the first grammar, who had shown that a whole evening's stage could be written down, line upon line, and handed to a maker who would build exactly that and nothing else. The next frame held a woman with a rule, and the year 2013 — the measurer's grandmother, who had reduced every shape in the world to a distance. The last frame held a man with a knotted cord, and the year 1974 — the patient one, who had proved that a ring of careless shepherds, each minding only his two neighbors, still ends by walking one circle. Nailed beneath the frames was the ruling of the house, sealed with a ring of seventeen marks: little on the far roads, much by the cheap fire. The caravans that went out from the works carried packs Odile could lift with one hand; the hearths at home, where wood was cheap, spread as wide as the hall.

Each morning the young riders went out to find what the works needed, and each evening they wrote their returns on slips of exactly six lines, never more: a number first; then what the rider said was true; then what he had lifted from another rider's slip to build it; then the one thing that would prove him wrong; then where the thing lay; then what it would cost the house to use. The slips went into the same drawers as the works' own memory, ruled with the same six lines, so that a finding and a remembering lay down together and nobody had to learn two ways of reading.

On the counting-room table stood a carved hive with paper wings, perfect in every joint. It was the plan for the riders to move as one flock, each reading the others' slips before the ink had dried. Visitors admired it; the riders raised their cups to it; in three winters it had not once flown for the works. It was true the way a rehearsal is true.

The last room held the green book. Its keeper was a small woman who wanted, as far as Odile could tell, shelves that weighed nothing. Her book held every heavy beast of the works: where each ox grazed in the far valley, the words that would bring it to the fence, and the small chore by which it could be tried before it was hired. The book held no ox. The oxen stayed in the valley, grazing at their own pace, until a plough stood in a furrow that truly needed them; then the calling-words were read aloud and the pull arrived at the fence already moving. The ploughmen wanted the pull without the pasture; the keeper wanted the pasture without the weight; and between those two wants the green book governed more of the works than the mistress did, without lifting anything at all.

On her last evening Odile stood by the wide hearth and counted what she had never been taught: the caster wanted to be told; the measurer wanted no surprises; the riders wanted to be first and to be believed; the keeper wanted light shelves; the mistress wanted the works to matter. No one had written these wants down, and every rule in the house had grown up around them, the way a path grows around a wish to get somewhere. Nothing in the Lantern Works was heavy until somebody wanted something through it — and whoever wrote the words that answered the wanting held more of the house than whoever owned it.

# The Jar at the Gate of the Picture-Writers

In the city of the picture-writers, nobody carried pictures. A fresco cannot cross a mountain, but a page of commands can, and so the house drew its walls, its bowls, its faces as lines of instruction: press these six points and smooth; whatever stands within three fingers of this bone, draw toward the skin. Every page had to be written in the descending lines of the three dead teachers whose small ink portraits hung beside the door — the first, who found that a few handles could command any curve; the second, who swore a shape was known only when you could say, from any point in the room, how far you stood from its skin; the third, who taught the shortest walk between any two chambers of any house. A page written outside their lines was not read at the door.

Above the entrance someone long ago had cut the house's one law, with the year and a mark of eight strange signs beside it: small through the pass, wide in the yard. Bundles left the house no thicker than a thumb. The toll-keeper at the bridge charged by the fistful and stared at nothing while he counted, and the tight rolls crossed paying little. Inside the walls, board and paper cost almost nothing, and there, and only there, the rolls were opened and copied out as long as anyone pleased.

The house sent its young ones out with lanterns, lit at noon. Strangers laughed at that, and the travelers let them. Each one sent home what she found on slips of six lines and never seven: the number; the claim; what in it could be borrowed from another house; the one test that would break the claim if the claim was false; where the thing was seen; who would vouch for it. Six lines, the same six the house used in its own book of memory, so that one eye could read a slip and a page of the book without translating between them.

The slips were dropped into a walnut box by the door. The box sat on a stool. No cord ran from the box to the book, and no runner held that errand. Yet at supper the masters spoke of the travelers' findings as things the house already remembered, and the fires were warm, and everyone nodded.

At the gate lived an old woman who had refused every room the house offered and kept to a clay jar, which was large enough, she said, for everything she owned. She carried her lamp lit in full daylight. The house called her the dog, and she answered to it. Most days at noon she set the lamp on the walnut box and asked the young keeper — the one whose errand this account is — "The house says it remembers. Show me one slip that has reached the book." He could not. "Then it remembers nothing tonight," she said, "and supper is a tale."

Inside, on the long table, lay the catalog of the far chests. The great dead masters had left iron chests in warehouses across the water, and for each chest the catalog said exactly where it lay, what a courier must do to have it set up on a bench, and how to try it — strike one note, one joint, one seam — before trusting the iron. No chest moved for the catalog's sake. The iron stayed on the far shore until a true job, a wall promised, a door hung, called for it across the water; until then only the page traveled. The catalog itself was written in chalk. It had not been sworn, and its own margin said so: a maybe, still.

One evening she spoke to the keeper plainly, the way bread is warm. "You want the council to bless the chalk. You want the strangers to stop laughing at the noon lamp. You want the toll-keeper to bow. Notice that you are asking, and asking, and asking. You could stop today. Tonight, before you sleep, carry one slip from that box to the book in your own hand — the cord you keep describing will be strung someday by someone, but a hand is a cord tonight. And when a true wall is promised, fetch one chest across the water for it and strike its one note in the open street. The house does not need to be believed, boy. It needs to be seen doing."

He carried the slip that night, one page of six lines, and set it where the book could find it. She held the lamp while he did, which was unnecessary, and that was the point: what is done shows in any light; what is only said needs the dark. The chalk stayed chalk. The box stayed on its stool, honest about being a stool, until the day someone should nail it to the wall; the jar stayed at the gate; and the dog answered to her name.

# The Freight That Stayed Home

Véra came to the harbor house of the picture-writers to learn who had taught them to speak. She found a building with no books of prose at all. Every order the house sent abroad was drawn: a facade, a tide-table, a procession of torches, all pressed into a picture no wider than a thumb and folded until the courier felt nothing in his coat. Only in the inland towns, where paper cost little and copyists idled at every well, was the picture opened and redrawn at its full size. The masters kept a saying about it: what is heavy must not travel; let the small thing cross the world and grow where growing costs nothing. Véra wrote the saying down, because sayings are where houses hide their laws.

In the long room hung three portraits. Two showed men of the same generation who had, a lifetime ago, taught the guild to describe an entire scene — light, surfaces, weather, faces — as marks that a lantern-room downstream could carry out without asking questions. The third showed a woman with brass compasses, younger in the craft's memory, who had proved that a shape could be known entirely by its edge: measure the distance from any point to the nearest boundary and you possessed the whole figure, its inside and its outside, without ever drawing either. Below her, painted smallest and oldest of all, hung a man who had never drawn a picture in his life; he had spent his years finding the shortest road between any two towns, and the guild counted him an ancestor because every instruction they sent was, in the end, a road chosen. The porter dusted all three each morning and could not say which one the house owed the most.

In the exchange hall, traders were permitted to report only in strings of six. A number first; then the thing asserted; then what it would cost a rival to take it; then the test that would break it; then two further knots whose uses Véra did not learn that day. The strings were shaped exactly like the entries in the house's book of memory — same knots, same order — so that a finding could pass from a stranger's mouth into the book without changing its shape. The custom was carved above the dais in fresh letters. When Véra ran her thumb along the carving, the wood beneath the letters was pale and unworn. No string had yet changed hands under it. The rule hung the way an unused door hangs: fully fitted, opening on nothing.

The warden showed her the catalogue of distant teachers. One page to each: where the teacher kept house, by what road to summon them, and by what trial to test them once they stood in the hall — for a teacher who arrived was always examined before being believed. But each was summoned light. No chests, no instruments, none of the heavy freight in which a teacher's actual skill was stored; that remained in the teacher's own country until some definite labor asked for it by name. The warden turned to the page where this was written and pressed it flat with two fingers. "You should know that this page is still a petition," he said. "Some have signed it. No one has yet obeyed it. It waits to become law." He had told her without being asked, and he watched her face while he told it — the examiner examining himself being examined.

Late in the day she saw a woman turned away from the dais. The woman had stood in line with her string ready and, at the front, had said only that she did not yet know whether her finding held. The steward was not cruel. He explained that the six knots had a place for what is asserted and a place for how to break it, and no place at all for not-knowing; a string that would not assert could not be entered, and what cannot be entered cannot be traded or kept. The woman folded her cord and left. Véra asked what became of such people, and the steward said, kindly enough, that they came back once they had decided. It was said without malice, which is how a house says the thing it cannot hear.

Walking out, Véra passed the courier setting off with the evening post, his coat light as ever. In the breast pocket he carried the newest picture of all: the hall, the dais, the fresh-carved custom, the catalogue, drawn and pressed small enough to cross the world, six empty knots waiting inside it, each the size of a grain of salt, for someone far away to open where growing is cheap.

# The Knots That Rode the Pass

I spent my first life making crowds want what others were selling, and I have learned to read a house the way I once read a market: by asking who gains when its habits are believed. The painting house at the narrow pass rewarded that question better than most.

The house served patrons it would never meet, in cities beyond the switchbacks. Finished paintings rode badly — mules starved on the grades, varnish split in the cold — so the mistress taught her painters to tie cords. A knot doubled to the left stood for fog. A knot bitten flat stood for brass. A run of small knots, packed close as grain, was an entire hillside of wheat. What would have filled a wagon rode in a pocket.

Three portraits hung above the mixing benches: the twins of '88, who first sold whole scenes as strings of marks to the film cities and grew rich at it; the wanderer of '13, who taught that any point in a world needs to know a single number — how far it stands from the nearest surface — and that a world can be walked on that alone; and the surveyor of '74, who showed that a path broken in the middle mends itself if each stone asks only its neighbors. When apprentices asked why the portraits hung there, the mistress said only: nothing we tie was invented here.

Over the door, in tar, she kept one sentence: tight at the pass, loose in the valley. Above the tree line, where every ounce was paid for in mule sweat, work traveled as cord. Down in the valley, where paper grew cheap as reeds, the same cords were unwound and read aloud into long pages that said everything twice and apologized for none of it. One scene, two bodies. The road's price chose the body.

The runners who went out to prove cords against real mountains brought back cards, and every card carried six lines — never a fifth, never a seventh. The first gave the path's number. The second said what was found. The third said what another house could lawfully borrow. The fourth named the stone that would break the claim, and the runner himself had to carry that stone home. The fifth named the trail; the sixth, the day the stone was turned. The cards were cut to the same shape as the house's own memory books, so a finding slid from a runner's fist onto the long shelves without being copied twice. I noticed how convenient that was, and whose convenience it served.

For all that, the card road went nowhere. The pigeonholes were carved and set into the wall, forty sample cards stood in them true as teeth, and the tube that would carry cards between houses lay in crates in the yard. The mistress would not have it strung. "Not until the shape survives a winter," she said. So the runners' findings existed, whole and honest, and traveled exactly as far as the room.

In a locked case lay the pattern-book of the far masters. One page each: where the master keeps his rooms, how to call him, and how to test — before any coin moved — that the answering voice was his and not a servant's imitation. Nowhere did the book offer to ship a master's chests. A master's learning sat in a thousand drawers of practiced strokes, and the drawers stayed in the far city until a named job, with a patron's money behind it, genuinely wanted them. An apprentice once asked to fetch a chest over "in case." The mistress refused. "You do not haul a man's life across the pass on the strength of maybe." The book's cover bore a sprig of green wax: offered, examined, not yet sworn.

And the mistress — I watched her the way I once watched clients. She gained when the valley believed the cord-form was finished; no patron then asked for the long pages except where paper was cheap, and the dear roads stayed hers alone. The runners gained when the house believed six lines could hold a mountain; their cards were read, and they were fed. What the accepted card cropped was what acceptance always crops: every finding that needed a seventh line to be true. I searched the shelves for those. They were not there — not hidden, simply never written. The house believed it owned a full map of the pass, and the map it owned was precisely the map that fit the card. I built a career on that arithmetic before I learned to run it backward.

# The Tight Rows

Ilse came to the workshop in the dry autumn to learn the writing of orders. The house stood at the top of the valley, and from its windows she could see the far pastures where the planters worked, small as nails against the brown hills. Everything the house commanded, it commanded in writing; no planter was ever brought to the house to be shown anything.

Her first lesson was the rows. An order was not a letter. It was a ladder of small marks, row under row, and every mark placed a thing by one fact only: how far it stood from the nearest border of the ground. A tree was not "the beautiful tree by the well"; it was a mark, and a number, and the number was the distance from the closest edge. Old Mother Aldis, who kept the origins, had nailed the house's hand to this writing long ago, with a ruling pressed by a stamp cut with seventeen teeth. She showed Ilse the corridor of portraits: the first grandmother, who had proved a picture could be grown out of words alone, without anyone standing before the field; the second, who had measured her whole country by distance from the nearest fence and found that distance was the only fact that never argued; the third, who had watched scattered flocks all night and shown that a disturbed flock, left alone, walks back to its old order with no shepherd directing it. The portraits were dusted weekly. The rows were dusted never, because they were used too often to hold dust.

In her second week Ilse copied two orders for the same garden. One went to the high pasture, where the mule track was bad and every pound of paper rode on a mule's back; its rows were pressed so tight the whole garden sat on a thumbnail, and the planter up there read them the way others read weather. The other stayed in the valley, where paper was cheap as straw; it sprawled across three sheets, every mark spelled out for apprentices. When Ilse asked which was correct, the clerk shrugged: tight where carriage is dear, loose where paper is cheap — the mule decided, and nobody had ever overruled the mule. She noticed, later, that the tight rows were coming down into the valley now, that valley letters were beginning to look like pasture letters, that even the baker's boy compressed his notes. Nobody had ordered this. It arrived with the practice itself.

The memory of the house lived in books of six columns. Each row carried its number, its claim, what it took from older rows, the question that would undo it if anyone dared ask it well — and two more slots too plain to need describing. Beside each row stood a small mark: seen, quarreled, or not yet. Ilse learned that a row without its undoing question was refused at the door, however fine its claim.

In the message room hung a stencil that a young clerk named Tomas had cut one evening the winter before. It held the same six slots as the memory books, exactly, so that a finding could pass from a messenger's hand into the book without being rewritten by anyone. It was well cut. It hung on its nail. The messengers still arrived talking, scattering their news in their own voices, and the clerks still listened and wrote in their own hands, and the stencil governed nothing yet. Tomas said it would, in time. The drawer did not disagree.

The last room held the gray catalog. In it stood every far master the house might ever call: where each one lived, the road by which to summon them, and the small trial each would have to pass before being trusted with the house's ground. No master lived in the valley. Their hands, their heavy chests of tools, stayed in their far villages until a job truly called for them — and the house had learned to want a thing done before it paid for the hauling of a chest. The catalog was bound in gray, not black. Gray meant asked-for: an elder had proposed it, and the house had not yet sworn it. Ilse asked when it would be sworn, and was told that gray books wait for their work to prove their color.

At the end of autumn she wrote her first order, for a hedge at the far fence, a mark and a number, tight enough for a mule. She watched the planters place it without a word coming back up the track, and she understood that the writing had already decided what the planters could be asked for and what they never would be. She put her thumb over the order, and it disappeared whole.

# The Scroll No One Read Aloud

Tilde came to the house of the picture-makers in the month the pass closes. The house stood on the near side of the mountains and served the far valleys, and it kept one rule older than any of its members: nothing heavy crosses the high ground. When a valley wanted a picture of a harvest or a saint, the house did not haul the finished thing over the snow. It wrote the picture down small — a folded page of terse orders, light here, hill where the line says hill — and sent the page; the picture was built on the far side, where room and patience were cheap. The packs that crossed were the size of a hand. What they became, in the valley, could fill a wall. Over the door a flat stone carried the rule, and beneath the carved words a row of worn sigils named the old ruling; no one alive could sound them, and everyone obeyed them.

In the hall above the hearth hung three portraits, each with a year cut into its frame. Two young men shared the first frame, 1988 beneath their shoulders: they had taught the founders to send orders instead of objects. The second, 2013, was a surveyor who taught the house to describe a country not by its stones but by one's distance from it — walk until the distance comes to nothing and you are standing on the shore. The third, 1974, was a stern schoolmaster who taught that the shortest road yields only to the disciplined, and that a letter arrives because every line of it was made to be checked. The keeper dusted the portraits each morning and let no one rehang them lower, though visitors rarely looked up.

On the longest wall hung a scroll in the house's finest hand, drawn long ago for the watchers the house sends out in flocks. It prescribed how a watcher must come home: each finding lying on a card of exactly six lines — the finding's number, what the watcher claims, what could steal it, what would prove it false, and two lines more in the same spare temper. Six lines, never a seventh — the very six on which the house's own memory was shelved, so that what the watchers brought could be filed row against row beside what the house already knew.

But the watchers came home talking. They gave their findings as long tales by the fire, rich with weather and cousinage, and a tale cannot be folded to six lines without wounding it, so no one folded. The cards stayed blank. The scroll stayed beautiful. The tales were heaped in the north corner, season on season, a soft dune of the unfiled. Twice that winter a man crossed the dune in the dark and fell, and the house bound his ankle and said nothing about the dune.

On her second day the keeper took Tilde down to the cellar, saying that what a house keeps in its cellar tells you what it is. On a shelf lay a blue book with a nominee's ribbon, unsealed. It was a register of the far teams: for each great engine in its own country, where it stood, how to wake it, and how to prove it was truly itself before work was trusted to it. The engines never came to the house. When a job needed one, the job travelled; the engine stayed where it stood and did the work at home. The book had been written years before and waited for the house to say yes, and the house kept not saying.

"Why is the scroll never read aloud?" Tilde asked. The keeper said the watchers love their tales. "And the ribbon?" He said the councils had been busy. She saw then that the house drew its instruments the way other houses paint their gods, and that an instrument never played hears no contradiction. The dune was the scroll's own silence returning, tale by tale, to lie in the corner like a guest left standing at the table.

That evening she took the oldest tale off the dune — a small one about a bridge in the eastern valley — and copied it onto a card in six lines. The last line cost her the cousinage; she kept the bridge. The keeper watched her shelve the card beside its kin and did not stop her. By morning one watcher had trimmed his own tale to fit, and winced as he cut, and the house, which had drawn the scroll so long ago, finally heard it read. The blue book kept its ribbon. The packs went over small, as always, and grew large only where largeness was cheap.

# The Folded Harbor

At the salt harbor, pictures travel folded. The guild of scene-writers took the habit from four dead masters whose names are still read off the flyleaf of the primer, each name followed by a small date like a nail hole: a carver who taught a hull to lie smooth however coarse the plank beneath it; a lamplighter who could price a beam of light before it was lit; a surveyor who stood in fog and could say, from any point in it, how far the shore lay and on which side of it he stood; and a walker who wrote down his road before he took it and never took a step that was not already written. From these four the guild keeps its trade: a whole scene — hulls, fog, lantern-light on water — pressed into glyphs on a narrow strip, folded small enough to ride in a boot.

Over the door of the waystation at the top of the pass a law is cut into the lintel, with a string of small cuts beneath it that the clerks use to name it. The law says what the mountain has always said: what is dear travels shut, and only what is cheap opens. Nothing is unfolded on the ice. Riders cross with the strips closed, and the unfolding is done in the valley, where ink is cheap, paper is thick, and the light holds late into the evening. A rider caught opening a strip on the glacier is fined for the weight, not for the words.

Joss read at night in the valley station. One evening a strip came down from the capital, and the senior reader unfolded it first. It was a festival: lanterns on the water, crowds on the quay, flags in the order the capital favors, and the senior reader saw it exactly so, lanterns first, and sent the capital a confirmation in the words the capital likes. The apprentice took the same strip and kept the quay and the lanterns but turned the crowd's faces out toward the water, away from the flags. And a stranger off a rival ship, given the strip overnight, read the flags as a bill of complaint and laughed until he had to sit down. All three held the same glyphs. The capital had folded a festival; the stranger's hands opened an indictment.

Joss wrote the capital a letter describing the stranger's laughter. It came back unopened. The return wire that runs beside the strips carries confirmations; it was never strung for laughter. So she folded the stranger's reading small and kept it, because someone in the valley ought to remember that one strip had held two scenes.

Along the shore the scouts walk their rounds and bring findings home on slips, and every slip carries six lines, never five: first the count, then the claim, then what the finder would take if it were allowed, then the trial that would shame the claim, then the remaining lines always in the same order as every slip before. The shape is the house's own. The great memory book in the back room keeps its entries in the same six lines, so any clerk who can read one page of the house can read a scout's slip without asking anyone the way in.

A drawer of new slips was printed in the spring, and the ink still smells of the press. Twice the caravan has left since; twice the drawer stayed shut, and the riders went out with the old loose letters, as their fathers did. The new slips lie in ranks like a bell that has been cast but not hung.

In the back room there is also a catalogue of far engines. Each entry names a distant city, says where the great stones lie there, gives the words that summon them down the mountain, and names the trial that proves a stone true. But no stone travels on a hope. The heavy part stays in the far country until a commission arrives that cannot proceed without it, and only then is the summoning spoken. The catalogue's cover carries a wax stamp the house reads as offered, not sworn.

Joss was given the night desk for good that winter. When a new reader comes up from the ships, she hands him a strip before she hands him the primer, and stands quiet while he unfolds it, because the scene is finished wherever it is opened, and the capital is very far away, and the fog is close.

# The Ribbon and the Valley Page

A counselor came up from the south to the picture-house at the river bend. He had spent his life writing in the margins of other men's books and trusted no sentence he could not try to break; he had heard that in this house pictures were made without hands. He came to see whether the thing was true or merely neighbor to the truth.

Nobody in the house painted. The apprentices sat at long tables making ribbons, strips of paper crowded with small marks, and at the end of the hall a brass press took each ribbon, read it mark by mark, and gave out a picture. The same ribbon through the press gave the same picture, every time. The warden would not let a picture hang unless the ribbon that begat it lay in the drawer beneath it; a picture without its ribbon, he said, was a rumor.

On a ribbon every mark stood for one whole phrase — no more, no fewer — written out at length on the wide pages of the valley book, where ink cost nothing. The craft of the house was the passage between the two: apprentices wrote phrase beside mark until they could fold a wide page down to its ribbon and unfold a ribbon back into its page. A mark without its phrase was struck off the ribbon; a phrase without its mark was struck off the page.

The house traced its descent to three portraits on the stair. The oldest was a ferryman from the marsh country; his one teaching was that a procession scattered on the road settles back into its own order without command from behind. Next came two partners in one frame, who had set the grammar of the ribbons: lights before surfaces, surfaces before distances, every picture speakable in marks. The youngest was a surveyor, painted within living memory, who taught the house to give a shape without drawing it: at every point write the steps to the nearest edge, counted forward outside the shape and backward within it, and the press will find the edge where the number turns. The warden named the three in one breath and would not choose among them.

The door into the press room was narrow, and crossing it was dear. Whatever went through had to be folded small; an apprentice who tried to carry a wide page across was sent back to the folding tables. Yet nothing in the house stayed folded: the warden would not file a ribbon until its wide twin was written out on the cheap tables, and the drawer held them together, narrow beside wide. Reading the doorpost, the counselor laughed and told of a man he had known, also a counselor, who had written in the margin of a borrowed book that he had found a marvelous argument the margin was too narrow to hold, and had gone to his grave with it folded. That evening he asked for cheap paper and wrote out, in full, an argument he had carried folded for thirty years, and slept better than he had in a decade.

In the porch the house meant to send out riders each season, readers who would go to other houses and bring back what held and what failed. Each finding was to ride on a card of exactly six lines: a number first; what the rider held true; what he had taken from another's holding; the name of the man sworn to try to break it; and two lines more, of errand and warrant, which the warden recited while the counselor counted. The books of the house's own remembrance were kept in the same six lines, so that a card from the road could lie down among them without a clerk's changing a letter. But the porch stood empty. The cards lay in a wooden box with two winters of dust on the lid, and the box had not been moved since it was nailed. The warden said the roads would open in spring; the counselor tapped the lid.

In a back room a keeper tended a catalogue of far foundries. For each, three things were written: the road to it, the words that would call its metal across the mountains, and the trial to be applied when the metal arrived. The metal itself never came on speculation; the house bought roads, not iron, and the heavy stock stayed in its own province until a task called for it by name. At the catalogue's corner hung a chalk mark, the sign of a thing proposed and argued at the winter table, defended, sworn by nobody yet. Asked what would become of it, the keeper said it would stand until someone proved it, or someone needed it.

On the last evening the warden brought the visitors' book, a wide page with room for anything. The counselor wrote: I came to see whether the pictures were true or only neighbor to the truth; they are neighbor, and they say so; I broke one card out of the box and could not break the second; I will send six lines of my own by the first rider, whenever the riders ride. He wrote it out in full, margin to margin, wasting the paper gladly, and in the morning he went south.

# The Six-Line Country

The picture-house stood at the edge of the dear country, where paper was weighed before it was sold and ink was kept under lock. Three portraits hung on its stair. The oldest, dated 74, showed a lean walker who taught that between any two places runs a shortest road, and that the road is found by ordering one's steps, never by hurrying them. The second, dated 88, showed a kiln-master who taught that a picture need never be carried at all: it could be spoken as a recipe — this shape, that light, a shadow here — and baked in whatever town owned a kiln. The newest, dated 13, showed a woman who had walked all her life in fog and taught that any point, asked, will confess its distance to the nearest shore; a blind walker who never stopped asking could find any shore she pleased. The couriers touched the frames as they passed, and everything the house wrote afterwards was written in the shadow of those frames.

At the door the house recited its saying: small where the road is dear, wide where the table is cheap. In the high camps the couriers carried their drawings folded to the size of a thumb, six marks that stood for a mountain; in the low city, where paper cost less than bread, the same drawings opened into sheets as wide as barn doors, and no one marveled.

For their findings the couriers used slips cut to six lines and no more. The first line carried the finding's number; the second, what the finder asserted; the third, what another courier might take from it; the fourth, the way it could be broken; the last two, where and by whom. A slip with seven lines was not a slip. In the cellar, the house's long book was ruled in the same six lines, and the cellar-keeper pasted the slips straight onto its pages without a seam; he had not rewritten one in twenty years.

On the workshop wall the master had chalked a rank of trays through which couriers could drop slips to one another without ever meeting on the road. The trays were not cut. The chalk said when the trays are cut; until then, every slip in the province traveled in Ilse's pocket, which was not what the chalk had promised, and everyone knew it.

In the hall stood a catalog of the great engines that lived across the border; no engine lived in the province. For each, the catalog gave three things: where it stood, how to have it yoked and hauled over, and how to prove it still turned once it arrived. The engines never crossed on speculation; they waited in the far country until a job truly needed one, and went home when the job was done. The catalog itself was unsigned; the guild had argued over it three winters and sworn nothing.

Ilse, who carried the slips, began to notice what the slips were doing to the couriers. They had started to find only what would fit. A canyon that wanted seven lines came down the mountain as a gorge; a shore that would not say its distance was reported as blank ground. And because the third line offered what might be taken, the couriers read one another's slips at the third line first, and admired a finding by the size of its take. The house kept an honest book — the most honest book in the province, people said — and every page of it carried a handle for carrying away, baked into the ruling of the page.

The apprentices told another tale. They had grown up on the catalog and spoke fluently of engines they had never touched, quoting hauling-times and turning-tests from memory. The winter a dyeworks truly needed one, the hall stopped work to watch it cross the border; the catalog's test proved it turned, the dye was done, and the engine went home while the apprentices talked of nothing else for a month. Their hands, in all that time, knew only recipes.

What no one used was the wide paper. In the low city the drawings opened barn-door wide, were read once, rolled, and shelved. Ilse began pasting the wide ones along the kitchen corridor, where the cooks and the dung-carriers passed with their shoulders. People who had never cut a slip stopped and corrected a distance from their own travels, and pointed at a pass drawn wrong. In one month the corridor wall found more wrong roads than the slips had found in a season, and it asked no one's permission and fit no one's pocket.

The trays stayed chalk. The catalog stayed unsigned. New couriers came, touched the three frames, learned the six lines, and were told the saying at the door. Ilse kept her pocket full and the corridor papered, and when the cellar-keeper pasted her slips into the long book, the seam never showed.

# The Folded Rooms

Marthe came to the great house in the month of repairs, carrying a notebook in which she collected accidents. She had been told the house was orderly, and it was, though nothing in it was ever commanded. When the west wing wanted a new hall, the steward wrote a small paper — pale fir, light falling from the left, a table set for twelve — folded it to the size of a thumb, and gave it to a rider going over the pass. In the valley below, where paper was cheap and the presses ran all night, strangers unfolded the paper and built the room exactly, and no plank ever crossed the mountain. The rule was old, numbered in the green book of acts, and every child could recite it: nothing bulky crosses the pass; travel folded, and unfold only where paper is cheap.

Where the habit came from, Marthe learned from the chronicler, a small woman who lived between three trunks and had nailed the first page of the oldest one to the copying-room door. That trunk held the great-grandmother's books. The great-grandmother had painted nothing in her life; she wrote out how a picture should be made — grind this, thin the blue until it weeps, layer that — and let strangers in far cities mix the colors, so that her portraits hung in houses she never entered. The second trunk belonged to the surveyor, who never walked the fields he could describe. He put a single question to every point in a field — how far do you stand from the nearest fence — and from the answers alone he knew the shape of the ground and the thickness of any wall, without touching either side of it. The third was the schoolmaster's, full of tasks cut into single strokes, each stroke honest, none turning back on itself, so that a child could check the work and a liar could not hide inside it. From these three the house had taken its hand, the chronicler said: it wrote finished things, small, and let the far side do the unfolding.

In the counting room, the house's memory was kept in rows of six lines. The first carried a number. The second said what the writer held to be true. The third said what the row would cost its author if it proved false. The fourth named the test that could break it. The scribes would not explain the last two lines to strangers, only that a row with fewer than six was not a row and could not be entered.

Every Friday, in the courtyard, the young riders rehearsed a new discipline for carrying news to the sister houses: every finding folded to six lines, in the same shapes as the memory rows. Marthe stayed to watch. The riders recited and corrected one another's sixth lines sharply, as though the sixth line were a horse that shied. Then the real post left the gate — long letters, sealed, in the old style. She stayed a second Friday, and a third, and filled more of her notebook than she had planned. On the third Friday a young rider, fluent now, said "as we sent last week," though nothing had ever been sent, and no one laughed, and the oldest rider looked at his boots. Marthe borrowed a rehearsal slip and laid it in the counting room beside the open memory book. Line for line, the shapes matched. The book was full; the slip was clean.

In the library she found the far-guest book, its pages stamped with a wafer of white wax that meant believed but not sworn. It listed every master who served the house without living in it: where he lodged, how he was to be summoned, and how to test the man who arrived against the man who had been called. It held one further rule in the chronicler's hand: a summons asks for the master and never for his chests; the crates stay in the far country until work is truly waiting, because a summons that hauls furniture over the pass pays the toll twice. Marthe read the rule, and then read it again.

She left on a Saturday, while the long letters were being loaded. The head rider asked her, the way caretakers do, what she had found. "Only that your tongues crossed the pass some time ago," she said, "and are waiting up there for the rest of you." Behind her, one of the young riders folded a rehearsal slip to the size of a thumb and put it in his pocket, where no toll could be charged for it. Nothing was sent. On Friday, the recitation would begin again.
