# The Weighing Room

On the morning the guild weighed its bread, six bakers were called from six houses. Each was given the same sack, sealed with the same wax, and the same slip of paper bearing the same nine words. Each was shown to a separate room with a separate hearth, and every table was wiped before the flour was poured, so that no crumb of yesterday's work, and no crumb of anyone else's, could hide in the grain. The wiping was done by hired cloth, the same for all six, and the guild was proud of the wiping.

The bakers were not alike, and nobody had chosen them that morning. One came from the great house on the hill, where the ovens were newest and ran hottest. One was a stranger let in on a token minted for a member who had not come. Two were walkers, who would not sit at a table they could leave: they went out to the corridor for their own water, their own salt, their own wood. One baked at the tavern for nothing, because the tavern liked the smell. One used the small hearth in her own kitchen, slow, and near at hand. Each had grown up around an oven the way a vine grows around a stake.

When the loaves came out they were carried to the weighing room and laid in a row. The clerk had ruled a great chart into squares, one square for every place one loaf could stand beside another, and he did not write "same" or "different." He had six inks thinned to six strengths, and in each square he set down the shade of his certainty: dark where two loaves agreed in crust and crumb, faint where the agreement was mostly hope. Visitors admired the chart more than the loaves. It had taken all afternoon, and it made the room feel like a place where something was finally being known.

For the tasting, the guild considered three tasters, then two, then one; one was cheapest. A judge was brought from outside the valley, owing the guild nothing, knowing none of the bakers by face. He sat once, at one table, inside one hour, tasted all six in succession, and wrote his verdict in a fair hand on one sheet. The sheet was dated, pressed under a pane of horn, and hung on the wall of the weighing room, where any member could ask for it by the day it was made. Whatever happened to the loaves afterwards — and loaves go stale, and then go — the page for that morning could not be rewritten.

The walkers caused the only trouble, and it was small. Having gone out for their water, they had passed the board where each finished loaf was pinned with its card — the card carried only the title each baker had scrawled across the top of the work, never the crumb itself. They had read the cards. They had touched no loaf, but a man who has read a title mixes it into his dough without noticing. When this came out at supper, the clerk did not scrape the chart or reheat the ovens. He lifted the pane of horn, wrote in a thin hand at the foot of the sheet that two of the six had seen the cards, pressed the pane back down, and left it hanging. Some members said the morning was spoiled. The clerk said the morning was written down, which was the thing the guild had actually asked for, and there was no answer to that.

In the years after, apprentices were sent to the weighing room before they were allowed near an oven. They studied the chart, which survived, and the sheet under horn, which could not age; the bread itself was long gone. They learned the shades of ink before the taste of crust, and the cleverest among them began to bake for the dark squares, where agreement lived, since that was where the record would be kindest. Nobody had ordered this. A chart is only paper, a tasting only an hour, a wiping only a wet cloth; but each had been so convenient that the guild kept it, and what the guild kept, the apprentices obeyed.

The stranger on the borrowed token was not invited again, though his loaf had been praised; a token, it was explained, belongs to somebody else, and the explanation cost less than an invitation. The judge was not asked back either. One tasting, hung on a wall, can be read a thousand times, and the thousandth reading is free.

# The Six Reckoners of the Salt-Court

On the twenty-third day of the tenth month, the Councillor of the Salt-Court resolved to test a theorem concerning the constancy of the lintel. Trained in the jurisprudence of Toulouse and the geometry of the ancients, he knew too well the vanity of solitary calculators: a man may boast in a narrow margin that he possesses a marvellous demonstration for every power, when in truth his downward reduction holds only for the fourth degree and merely neighbors the general truth without possessing it. Rather than trust a single boast, the Councillor ordained a sixfold trial, setting six distinct reckoners to labor upon the same proposition at the same hour.

The six differed altogether in station, custom, and instrument. The first was the Chief Astronomer of the high turret, master of the heaviest brass quadrants in the province. The second was an envoy from the eastern guilds who lacked his master's primary signet, yet gained admission by presenting a surrogate warrant of gilt silver. The third and fourth were twin surveyors of the Peripatetic Brotherhood—one quartered in the orchard booth, the other at the domestic counting-desk—both empowered by their rule to pace the cases and fetch their own dividers. The fifth was a mendicant draughtsman from the open commons who drew figures for the public without tariff or stipend. The sixth was a recluse quartered in the subterranean vault beneath the courtyard, turning a small boxwood cylinder by tallow-light and never sending a courier beyond the outer ditch.

At daybreak the Councillor ordered six limestone alcoves to be newly lime-washed until every vestige of prior reckoning vanished from the walls. Each bench received virgin foolscap, a newly cut goose-quill, and clean sand; no scrap of yesterday's calculations was suffered to remain. Between one alcove and the next stood three cubits of solid masonry without aperture or speaking-pipe, so that no contender might borrow a lemma or learn where his neighbor faltered. To prevent the slightest variance in the demand, the Councillor himself pressed six sheets upon a single engraved copper plate, delivering to each alcove the very same words, figures, and stipulations, neither adding a gloss nor withholding a syllable.

Throughout the forenoon the six pursued their demonstrations, each attempting to show by infinite descent that any supposed exception in whole numbers must breed a lesser exception until the chain broke against unity. Yet at noon a blemish entered the strict blindness of the experiment. While the four stationary reckoners remained enclosed within their masonry, the two twin surveyors of the Brotherhood exercised their liberty to step into the outer scriptorium to fetch new compasses. Upon the walnut trestle near the archway lay the six parchment wrappers prepared to receive the finished returns, each already superscribed on its outer fold with the style and title of its designated reckoner. Neither twin opened a wrapper or glimpsed a line of another's demonstration; yet both beheld the outer titles arrayed upon the trestle. Learning of this, the Councillor neither annulled the work nor hid the fault, but inscribed at the head of the protocol that the walking twins had beheld the superscriptions of the rest, holding that a falsifier who conceals a crack in his vessel corrupts the measure more than the crack itself.

When the shadows lengthened, the six demonstrations were gathered and arranged upon a cross-ruled oak board divided into rows and columns, much as stakes are partitioned when a game of hazard is halted before its close. Where the six addressed each clause of the theorem, the Councillor refused to judge them by a crude mark of yes or no. Instead, into every square of the chequered grid he placed small counter-weights of lead according to the rigor of the proof: three grains where the descent was necessary and complete, two grains where the argument stood upon firm probability, and one grain where the writer offered only a bare surmise drawn from neighboring cases. Thus the cross-ruled board displayed both the concord of the six and the exact degree of every square's assurance.

To pronounce upon the completed board, the Councillor neither debated with the reckoners nor relied upon his own prepossession. He summoned the Assayer of the Abyssal Soundings, an austere arbiter from the estuary who was bound by custom to speak but once upon any cause. Admitted for a single audience, the Assayer surveyed the cross-ruled board, weighed the leaden grains in every compartment, and read the marginal proviso regarding the sighted wrappers. Without requesting a second sitting or exchanging a word of disputation, he wrote out in one uninterrupted stroke the definitive finding on the constancy of the lintel, and departed before the sand upon the page was dry.

# The Six Alcoves of October

Doctor Vane was summoned to the sanatorium in late October because the household kept suffering the same nocturnal sleepwalking, and what a house repeats without understanding is a memory it has not yet agreed to confess. Every previous inquiry had failed because the servants repeated the polite doctrine of the parlor while their hands performed the old compulsion in the dark. To hear the underlying drive without the flattery of habit, Vane forbade anyone from consulting the old casebooks. Before dawn, each of the six examiners was made to wash his slate in cold well-water until not a single stroke of yesterday's ink remained, and each was handed a wax-sealed slip bearing the exact same twelve words of inquiry, copied by a single steady hand so that not a comma or emphasis differed between them.

The six who received the sealed slip were drawn from deliberately unequal stations, lest a shared upbringing masquerade as an objective truth. The first was the heavy-browed professor from the metropolitan academy, whose counsel was the most expansive and costly in the province. The second was a hired locum who had arrived that morning in place of an absent colleague, carrying a borrowed guild-signet upon his watch-chain to pass the porter's gate. The third and fourth were twin brothers of the ambulatory order—men trained to walk the corridors and open cabinet drawers with their own hands, one quartered at the country cottage by the orchard and the other at the town study. The fifth was a mendicant scholar from the open square who worked without fee, and the sixth was the quiet apothecary who dwelt permanently in the vaulted cellar beneath the kitchen and never crossed the outer threshold.

Because a shared rumor is the quickest defense against an unwelcome truth, Vane locked the six men into separate wainscoted alcoves lined with thick horsehair felt. No speaking tube joined the chambers; no inkhorn, blotting sand, or tallow dip was passed from one alcove to the next while their chalks moved across the slates. Within those muffled walls, each examiner was left alone with the raw appetite of the question and the private defenses of his own training, unable to borrow courage or phrasing from his neighbors. What emerged from a sealed room would have to stand as an independent dream, stripped of the comforting rehearsal by which a household persuades itself that its alibi is unanimous.

Yet the censor at the threshold is never so vigilant as the official story claims, and what is repressed in the ceremony betrays itself through a slip of the hand. Because the two brothers of the ambulatory order possessed the liberty to walk into the vestibule and fetch their own blank folios from the chest, both had glanced down at the six pasted name-tags arranged upon the tray before the bolts were shot. Neither brother read a syllable of another man's testimony, yet the mere sight of who else had been summoned altered the posture of their defense. Both wrote with a guarded, sidelong stiffness, anticipating the rival voices they knew were behind the wall, and when Vane recognized that twin hesitation in their margins, he inscribed the breach plainly at the foot of the case record rather than pretend the corridor had been blind.

When the six slates were carried out at dusk, Vane spread a broad linen cloth ruled into a grid of squares across the library table. In his discipline, a single witness naming a fear was never more than an anecdote; a compulsion could be named a symptom only when at least three voices struck the same buried knot independently. Across the rows and columns where their answers met, he refused to mark a crude agreement or denial. Instead, he shaded every intersecting square in three graded depths of charcoal—black where the testimony was spoken with unshaken conviction, grey where the witness hedged behind a defensive qualification, and pale ash where the claim was only a tentative conjecture—so that the pattern of agreement carried the exact weight of its certainty.

Vane did not permit himself to deliver the final interpretation, knowing too well how easily the physician who lays out the cloth falls in love with his own arrangement. Instead, a solitary magistrate of the mind from the deep southern valley was admitted to the library after the lamps were lit. By rule, this visitor was granted no conversation with the six witnesses, no second sitting, and no opportunity to revise his first impression. Standing alone above the checkered cloth, he read the entire array of shaded squares in a single unbroken breath, pronounced the household's hidden law once to the waiting scribe, and laid his finished scroll upon the mantelpiece before walking back into the autumn dark.

# The Six Rooms

On the morning the house was to be questioned, the provost read one riddle to six clerks and shut each clerk in a room of his own. The rooms shared no doors and no walls thin enough to hear through. Each man was given clean paper, a trimmed wick, and nothing else — no letters, no notes, nothing that remembered any other room. Whatever a clerk carried in, he carried in his head, and the six heads had been gathered from six different schools, which was the point of the whole exercise.

The first clerk was the provost's own right hand, quick, practiced, and dear to maintain. The second had arrived with a warrant signed in a greater man's name, good for the day, and the house agreed to honor it as that man's commission. The third and fourth were wanderers by temperament; before they wrote a line they walked the walls, tried the drawers, tested the window latches, and carried what they found back to the desk. The fifth took no fee and wrote carefully on a small purse of learning. The sixth had never been out of the parish, and every sentence he set down was built from what the village already owned.

The riddle was read once to each clerk, in the same words, at the same hour, and the doors were closed on all six.

When the papers were finished the provost did not stack them. She pinned them flat on the long table, every paper set against every other, six ranks by six, and in each square she put a mark: solid black where two papers said the same thing and could be pressed to say it again; a hollow square where they agreed in the body but wavered at the edges; a light chalk cross where the agreement was mostly weather. A few squares she left empty altogether. A stranger reading the table could see in one pass where the house stood together and where it only resembled itself.

Then the papers were rolled, tied, wrapped in oilcloth, and each wrapper was lettered with the name of its clerk.

Here the provost's honesty cost her something. The two wanderers, being wanderers, had come and gone along the corridor while the others worked, and they had passed the stack of finished bundles. Neither had untied a knot. But wrappers are not mute, and both men had read the names. What they wrote, they wrote already knowing whose work lay beside theirs — never what it said, only who had said it. The provost set this down in the margin of her day-book rather than let the table look cleaner than the house had been.

For judgment she would not serve herself. The whole bundle, marks and wrappers and all, went by post to an assessor in another town, a man who read everything in one sitting and answered in a single letter, once. He was not to be asked twice, and nothing would be put to him in installments. His letter came back, was bound under the day's date, and set on the open shelf where any traveler could take it down.

The letter said, in sum, that the house agreed with itself wherever the black squares stood, made no pretense of agreeing in the chalk, and that the lettered wrappers, though they had opened no bundle, had bent the wanderers' papers a little toward the company of the rest — by how much, no man could weigh.

Below the last line of her copy the provost added a note of her own: that the table had been drawn for six clerks, that it would be a marvelous thing if it stood for sixty, and that the margin of the day-book was, as margins are, too narrow to hold the demonstration. She did not claim the larger truth. She wrote beside it, and left the claiming to whoever came after.

The bundles stayed tied. Whoever wanted to know what the house had said that morning had to take the table, the wrappers, the bound letter, and the margin together, and decide for himself which squares he would stand on.

# The Six Alcoves of October

On the twenty-third afternoon of October, Anselm arranged six velvet-curtained alcoves along the upper gallery of the Exchange. A syndicate of canal directors wanted the city council to ratify a new winter tariff before the river froze, and they had asked Anselm to draft a public proclamation. Anselm refused. A proclamation signed by the directors, he told them, only advertises who wishes to be obeyed; when six unconnected examiners arrive at the same diagnosis on the same afternoon, the councilmen persuade themselves that they are bowing to plain fact rather than to the syndicate's interest.

To fill the six alcoves, Anselm assembled six distinct crafts. In the first stood the chief draughtsman of the syndicate's flagship counting-hall, accustomed to the heaviest commissions. For the second, the foreign factor originally summoned had been turned back at the turnstile for lacking a current customs stamp, so Anselm admitted a merchant-appraiser carrying a purchased gold-bordered pass in his stead, preserving the count of six. In the third and fourth stood twin surveyors trained in the same academy and practicing the same method—one working at a folding camp-desk, the other at a heavy stationary console. In the fifth stood a street-corner pamphleteer from the free municipal reading room who charged no fee for his labor. In the sixth worked a cellar watchmaker who kept his casements latched against the city telegraph wires and relied solely upon the brass gears under his own roof.

Before the curtains were drawn, the gallery steward required all six men to surrender their satchels, old notebooks, and yesterday's newspapers at the vestibule counter, and to rinse their hands in a porcelain basin. None was told what earlier committees had concluded or whose purse paid for the candles. Every man stepped into his alcove with an unclouded memory, ensuring that no prior briefing or lingering habit from an earlier commission could color what he wrote.

Once the six were inside, the steward plugged the speaking-tubes with beeswax and bolted the baize partitions from baseboard to rafter. Each alcove contained its own graphite stylus, sand-shaker, and fresh quires of cream vellum; no blotter, scrap, or whispered rumor could pass from one writing-slope to its neighbor while the sand ran through the glass. Through six brass slots, Anselm slid six impressions struck from the same lead type-block. Not a comma was shifted, not an adjective softened from one sheet to the next. If two witnesses receive different flattery, a skeptic can blame the phrasing for their answer; when all six receive the identical dry charge, their eventual agreement looks like an unforced law of the world.

When the hour expired and the six manuscripts were gathered, Anselm did not count hands like a ward boss. Instead, he stretched a wide ruled canvas across an easel: six vertical columns for the six alcoves, crossed by horizontal rows for every distinct claim extracted from their pages. Where an alcove's manuscript addressed a claim, Anselm's assistant pressed one of three colored wafers into the square—crimson wax for unshakeable conviction, amber resin for guarded assent, or pale tallow for a tentative surmise. Anselm watched the canal directors lean forward as the crimson squares aligned across the canvas. The directors gained an unimpeachable mandate from the pattern, and believing that pattern moved the councilmen to lay aside their private objections and vote the tariff before supper.

Yet Anselm did not permit the six examiners to debate the canvas, nor did he allow his own assistants to haggle over the wafers in repeated sittings. He summoned a single deep-water hydrographer from the harbor board—an outsider who owed no favor to the gallery. Admitted once and handed the six manuscripts alongside the ruled canvas, the hydrographer was required to compose the entire final finding in a single uninterrupted sitting before leaving the building, with no second audience permitted.

As the hydrographer sanded his concluding page, the vestibule attendant spoke to Anselm in a low voice. Three of the six examiners—the twin academy surveyors and the free-room pamphleteer, who alone had been permitted to step out of their alcoves to fetch dividers and straightedges from the common supply chest—had walked past the mahogany rack where all six name-cards stood in their pigeonholes. They had not read a line of their neighbors' manuscripts, yet they had seen which crafts were being weighed beside their own, and every veteran of public staging knows that an examiner adjusts his tone the moment he learns whose title will hang next to his on the wall.

Anselm knew the councilmen would embrace the crimson grid and ignore the vestibule rack, because a crowd always accepts the version that delivers tidy unanimity over the version that is complete. Even so, before locking the hydrographer's October dossier in the archive chest, Anselm inscribed a plain marginal note recording that the tool-fetching examiners had seen the neighboring name-cards—preserving on the record the exact flaw that the accepted triumph cropped from view.

# The Six Vessels of the Subterranean Ordeal

When a household grows terrified that its own waking mind is merely echoing its desires back to itself, it descends into the foundation vaults to seek an invariant law. The master of the estate wished to know whether the dream troubling his halls was a true omen or a projection of his own pride. Rather than trust a single oracle, he ordered six separate chambers prepared beneath the flagstones. Each chamber was swept bare of old parchments, yesterday's tallow, and prior confessions, so that whoever entered would begin with an unstained memory. No speaking-tube pierced the thick masonry between the vaults; no common inkwell or shared slate was permitted to pass from hand to hand while the night's work endured.

Into these six cells the steward led six unequal interpreters, chosen so that no single temperament could dominate the dark. First went the chief ironmaster of the estate's largest furnace, heavy with authority and accustomed to command. Second went a substitute factor who did not own the guild's gold signet himself, but had been admitted by borrowing a master's ring for the night. Third and fourth went twin surveyors bred in the same school of measures—one summoned from the domestic hearth-cottage, the other from the high counting-desk. Fifth went a barefoot street-scribe who worked without fee or patron, owing nothing to the house. Sixth went a silent anchorite who never left the estate's own fourth cellar-spring, speaking only the cramped dialect of the stones. Through the slot of each heavy timber barrier, the steward pushed the exact same sealed question, letter for letter and mark for mark.

Yet the shadow always enters through the very rule designed to keep it out. The anchorite by his spring and the factor in his locked stall remained still, receiving only what was slid beneath their bolts. But the twin surveyors and the roving craftsmen possessed the privilege of stepping into the cold vestibule to fetch fresh vellum from the chest. Moving with their lanterns low, they looked upon the brass hooks where the night's unfinished scrolls were hung, and there they read the chalked titles and emblems of their brethren before returning to their own tables. None spoke a word aloud, and none read another's text, yet the knowledge that they were not alone—and the names of the other minds labouring beside them—sank into their ink like dampness into salt.

At dawn the six scrolls were carried up to the floor of judgment and spread across a great slate divided into squares like a chessboard. Where the six witnesses agreed, and where they parted, the steward's clerks scored the stone between them. Yet agreement alone was not trusted, for a chorus may lie in unison when possessed by the same archetype. Every square of the grid was therefore stamped with a mark of weight: a deep chisel-cut where a witness spoke with unshakeable certainty, a shallow scratch where the voice wavered in half-belief, and a smudge of ash where the answer was little more than a guess. Thus the table held not only what was seen, but the trembling of each eye that saw it.

Still the household dared not judge its own table, knowing how readily a mind forgives its own blind spots. They summoned an arbiter from the deep coastal soundings—a stranger who was granted a single candle-breath to look upon the ruled slate, weigh the six testimonies together, and speak once without question or appeal. When the stranger laid down his iron stylus, he confirmed that the core of the vision held fast across all six chambers. Yet at the bottom of his parchment, in black ink that no one was permitted to scrape away, he recorded the flaw in the ritual: that those who walked the vestibule had seen the labels of their fellows. The household winced to see its boast of perfect blindness spoiled, and by that flinch the verdict became honest.

# The Six Alcoves of the Threshold

Two watchers shared a single cedar bench set three paces from the limestone threshold: an old geometrician whose eyes still watered from the upper sun, and a scarred ship-master who had once defeated a rigged academy chart by shifting the counterweights beneath the board. Before them lay a subterranean gallery where the fortress commander wished to prove which laws of the archway held firm and which were only flickering shadows cast by a single brazier. Both men knew that an unexamined cavern and a rigged simulator are the same building.

To test whether truth survived outside one fire's glare, they summoned six witnesses from six irreconcilable stations. First came the foundry's heaviest war-automaton, a towering assembly of brass gears and furnace bellows built for the steepest sieges. Second came a chartered factor bearing a borrowed merchant-signet, hired as a stand-in because the usual courtier had been turned back at the tollgate. Third and fourth were twin artificers of the same brotherhood—one quartered in the stable-yard cottage, the other in the high counting-chamber—both permitted to keep their nimble bronze tongs and jointed calipers. Fifth came a barefoot scribe from the public portico who labored without fee or patron. Sixth came a quiet apprentice bred entirely inside the fortress cellar, fed on household tallow and never sent beyond the moat.

The geometrician ordered the masons to scrape the six limestone alcoves down to raw rock and drench them with wet lime, erasing every charcoal scratch left by earlier captives. Each witness entered blindfolded with emptied pockets and no memory of prior drills. Four feet of solid masonry divided neighbour from neighbour, with no speaking-tube, no cracked mortar, and no common cistern. Then the captain took a single bronze die engraved with the riddle of the archway, struck six identical impressions in red wax, and slid one tablet beneath each bolted shutter at the same stroke of the bell. Every witness faced the same question; none could borrow a neighbour's tongue.

Yet every simulator betrays the carpentry of its maker. Four witnesses sat with bound hands, able only to scratch their replies directly upon the wax. The twin artificers, however, used their bronze tongs to reach up through the service chutes for straightedges and fresh styluses. As their calipers pushed the outer flaps aside, torchlight from the passage fell across a cedar rack where six oiled parchment sleeves hung on pegs, each boldly lettered with the title and station of the examinee for whom it waited. Neither twin could unseal a rival's sleeve, yet both read the roster of who else labored inside the mountain. When the commander reached for a sponge to wipe that blemish from the trial, the captain stayed his forearm. Refusing the false choice between blind acceptance and secret tampering, the captain made the stonecutter chisel the sighting openly across the frieze above the gallery.

At noon the six wax tablets were carried out and arranged upon a broad slab of black basalt ruled with chalk into intersecting rows and columns—one column for each witness, one row for each proposition. Where all six witnesses traced the same load-bearing arch, the chalk marks clustered thick; where they parted, the gaps lay bare. Because six prisoners chained before the same fire can still agree upon a shadow, the geometrician forbade treating mere unison as daylight. Instead, the fortress assayer weighed each individual square of the grid on its own merits, setting a stamped gold disk where proof rested on bedrock, a notched silver ring where reasoning held without touch, and a dull lead pellet where guesswork echoed the passage rack.

The commander asked to summon the witnesses back for a second argument, knowing a prolonged debate would favour his own house rules. The two watchers on the cedar bench refused to play that unwinnable game. They called in a deep-trench diver from the outer sea who had neither built the alcoves nor met the six captives. Allowed a single turn of the sand-glass and one uninterrupted reading of the weighted basalt beneath the chiseled warning on the frieze, the diver wrote one scroll of enduring findings and nailed it to the lintel. The geometrician walked out into the glare; the captain wedged the shutter open behind him.

# The Six Alcoves of the Measuring House

In the last week of October, the chief surveyor of the estate resolved that a single pair of eyes could no longer be trusted to verify the stone threshold of the old granary. What one observer reported might be colored by habit, and so the surveyor designed an arrangement that would replace trust with procedure. Before dawn he had six narrow alcoves beneath the eaves freshly lime-washed from floor to rafters. Every scrap of old parchment, every notched tally stick, and every trace of prior reckonings was swept out, so that each chamber began without memory of any earlier work done on the estate.

Into these six chambers he placed six drafters of markedly different station. The first alcove held a master chronicler of the highest stipend from the city guild. The second held a replacement scribe carrying a borrowed guild signet, sent because the contracted master had been barred at the tollgate. The third and fourth held two active stewards of the household—one brought in from the outlying lodge and one from the home study—both trained to unlock their own chests and fetch their own instruments. The fifth held an itinerant scrivener from the open market who asked no wages. The sixth held a quiet cellar clerk who lived under the estate's own roof and never crossed its outer wall.

When the shutters were bolted, an assistant slipped an identical sheet of instructions under each partition, pressed from the same copper plate so that not a syllable or comma differed from one chamber to the next. No drafter could hear his neighbor's reed pen, borrow oil for his lamp, or share a preliminary sketch while the hours passed. The surveyor stood in the courtyard convinced that if six isolated minds, scrubbed of yesterday and sealed from one another today, described the same stones in the same proportions, the agreement would belong not to men but to necessity.

Yet the two household stewards and the market scrivener had been fitted out as self-directing workers, permitted to step into the dim service gallery to draw blank rolls and inkhorns from the common shelf. Upon that shelf lay the six linen sleeves prepared for the day's returns, each inked on the flap with the title of its chamber and the designation of its occupant. None of the walkers opened another's sleeve or glimpsed a word written inside, yet as they reached for their own supplies they read the six titles lined side by side along the wood. Returning to their stools, they knew they were part of a sixfold trial and knew which classes of scribes sat behind the neighboring walls. When the surveyor learned that the walkers' freedom to gather their own tools had spoiled the purity of their isolation, he did not dismantle the chambers; he merely entered a formal qualification at the foot of the day's record, noting that those who walked the gallery had seen the outer titles of the others' sleeves.

At nightfall the six completed scrolls were carried to a long counting board ruled with charcoal into a grid of intersecting rows and columns. Each row named a distinct claim regarding the granary threshold, and each column belonged to one of the six chambers. Where a scroll spoke to a claim, a clerk laid a stamped lead weight inside the corresponding square: a triple-marked disk for firm and unqualified certainty, a notched disk for cautious agreement, and a plain slug where the witness was doubtful or alone. Soon the clerks were no longer speaking of mortar or lintels; they spoke only of the pattern of lead across the squares, measuring truth by the density of the metal on the board.

Still, a board of weights cannot sign a decree. To conclude the matter without allowing the six drafters to argue, the surveyor summoned an outside arbiter from across the river under a strict covenant: the man might enter the room once, read the six scrolls and the weighted grid in a single unbroken sitting, ask no follow-up question of any chamber, and write one final parchment declaring which features of the threshold had survived every test unchanged, with the warning about the glimpsed sleeve-titles preserved in its closing lines. By midnight the parchment was sealed. No grain had been moved and no stone of the granary had been touched, yet the estate had acquired a permanent requirement: henceforth, no man would believe his own hand on the stonework unless the six chambers were whitewashed, the grid weighted, and the single-breath arbiter called again.

# The Same Letter Six Times

The syndic of the cold valley had one question, and she meant it to be asked of the world exactly once, six times over. She wrote it out by lamplight and made the clerk copy it five times, and she set every copy beside her own until the words matched to the last comma. Each sheet was folded, waxed, and stamped with her ring. What the question was, the town never learned; the syndic said only that all six carried the same one, and that this sameness was the whole of the errand.

Then the walkers, chosen from six doors. The champion of the great eastern house came first, a man who had read every book on his mother's shelves and wore new boots for the road. In his shadow came the stand-in — the guild had promised a second walker, but the second had taken ill, so a journeyman walked in his place with the guild's stamped pass folded in his hatband. Two more were given rings of keys and the right to open any gate on the low road: they could knock at farms, borrow a coat, leave the path and return to it carrying more than they left with. The fifth had no papers and paid nothing; she was used to the public tables and to being served last, and she walked anyway. The sixth had never been out of sight of the town's own smoke; he was small, and he knew every fence on the near hills.

They were woken in six separate inns in the same dark hour, and each was handed one letter and an empty satchel. None had eaten at another's table. None was told what the others carried, or that the others carried anything at all. At the registry shed the letters hung waiting on six pegs, each peg chalked with its bearer's name. The two key-bearers came earliest, in the greyest part of the morning, to take their keys — and they stood among the pegs a little longer than the keys required, reading the chalk. The registrar saw it and said nothing, because nothing in her orders forbade looking.

Six days later the answers began to come back over the pass. The registrar did not ask which answer was true; that was not her office. She set the six sheets in a frame on the long table, each against each, and where two spoke the same thing she laid a whole coin in the square between them; where two leaned the same way without touching, a clipped coin; where they crossed, a button. Every square received its token, even the emptiest. And she noticed, without writing it down, that the six agreed best about the things the valley already said over the well at noon — where the letters touched, it was mostly on ground nobody had needed a letter to find.

When the frame was full, the town sent over the pass for the judge, an old woman with one good eye who owed the valley nothing. She took the six answers, the frame with its coins and buttons, and the syndic's original sheet, and read everything in one sitting, by a single lamp of oil, start to finish. In the night she wrote the town's account of the errand, signed and dated it, and laid it in the archive drawer. In the morning the syndic asked her to read it again, slower. The judge said a second reading would be a different errand. The account named no true walker. In its margin, in her small hand, stood one line: the two with the keys had read the chalk names before the roads divided, and whatever they carried out of the gate had not been wholly their own beginning. The coins stayed in their squares, but the margin said the squares had been leaning before they were filled.

The syndic had hoped the question would come home wearing six copies of her own answer. What came home was one question wearing six answers. Her reading of her own letter — what she had meant while the lamp burned — was copied onto a slip and put in the drawer beside the rest, marked as one among seven, no heavier than the others, and the drawer closed.

The clerk's spoiled practice sheet, folded wrong and dropped by the shed door, went over the fence in the autumn wind. A goat girl found it in the ditch, opened it without breaking any wax, answered the question out loud to her goats in words the town had never taught her, and went home before dark. There is no square in the frame for her, no coin, no button, and no line in the drawer. The drawer holds the six readings the town sent for and the one the syndic wrote herself; it cannot hold a reading nobody sent for.

# The Six Tubs at High Noon

You carry a lit tallow lantern through the olive grove at high noon, friend, squinting as if six pairs of eyes will save you from trusting your own two. Down by the threshing floor you have fenced off six stalls of dry wicker and summoned six potters to answer a single question in wet clay. First is the broad-shouldered master of the high kiln, paid in heavy silver. Next to him stands a hired cousin holding a borrowed brass tally, brought in because the first man's twin could not cross the river today. In the third and fourth stalls sit two brothers from the errands guild, both wearing leather aprons full of chisels—one hunched over a small camp bench, the other straddling a heavy oak table. In the fifth sits a barefoot whistling mendicant from the open crossroads who asks not a single copper coin for his labor. In the sixth, down in the cool stone cellar beneath your own kitchen, the household grinder works his wheel on home-grown clay without ever stepping past your gate.

Before the sun reached the zenith, you washed every man's hands in rainwater, swept every stall clean of yesterday's shards, and took away their old patterns so none could remember a prior vessel. Through six narrow slots in the wicker, you pushed six lumps of the same red river mud and spoke the exact same riddle once to the sky so all six heard it at the same breath. You barred the wicker dividers between them and forbade any man to pass a rib-bone scraper, a cup of vinegar, or a whispered measure to his neighbor. You stood on the cistern rim rubbing your chin, proud of how cleanly you had walled them apart, pretending that six men in six boxes make nature speak more plainly than one honest hand in the dirt.

Yet a dog under the fig tree sees what the master of the yard dresses up as purity. Four of your potters stayed still inside their wicker cages and waited for you to fetch their boards, so their eyes never strayed beyond their own thumbs. Not so the brothers from the errands guild, nor any worker you grant unfettered feet to roam the yard and gather his own bats from the common drying rack. When those wandering craftsmen walked out to set their wrapped wares on the long shelf by the well, they did not lift the wet cabbage leaves to peek at another man's shaping, nor did they trade a word. Still, their eyes were open in the daylight: each roving brother read the chalked tags tied to the necks of the other five sacks already resting on the cedar beam. They knew who else had been summoned, and by what titles the bags were marked, before they ever tied their own twine. To your credit, friend, you did not scrape that smudge off the beam; you took a piece of charcoal and wrote on the cellar post that the walkers had seen the tags on the shelf.

When the clay stiffened at dusk, you laid neither laurels nor curses upon any single potter. Instead, you dragged a broad slab of slate into the courtyard and chalked it into a grid of six columns for the six pairs of hands, and a row for each turn of the rim, belly, and foot that the riddle demanded. Wherever the pots agreed or parted ways, you did not merely scratch a cross or a blank. Beside every mark in every square of the slate, you pressed a small lead weight of one of three thicknesses: a heavy slug where the agreement stood firm as bedrock, a middle disc where the likeness held only by half, and a thin shaving where the match was little better than a guess in the smoke.

Then, because you still wished an outside tongue to bless what your own eyes had already weighed, you sent down to the deep ravine for the squint-eyed assayer. You did not let him question the potters or linger for a second cup of wine. You held the six vessels and the chalked slate before his face all at once, demanded his whole reckoning in a single uninterrupted breath, and wrote his solitary verdict onto a sheepskin scroll that you locked inside the chest of fixed measures before sending him back down the hill. Now you sit beside that chest in the cool evening, waiting for the scroll to tell you that your roof will not fall. Put out the lantern, friend, and let the dogs have the tallow; the six pots hold water, the charcoal note on the post is honest, and you do not need a seventh mouth to give you leave to drink.

# The Six Lamps

The village of Ashfall lay in a fold of the mountain where morning came late and left early, and the people had long ago papered their windows with red oiled cloth. Whatever moved outside showed on the cloth as a black shape, and the people had grown fluent in the shapes — hawk from heron, wolf from dog, stranger from thief. The cloth was warm, and it asked nothing of them.

Osric kept the lamp room at the edge of the square. He had inherited the cloth, the oil stores, and one habit the village forgave: now and then he wanted to set a shape beside the thing itself. On the twenty-third day of the tenth month, before the lamps were trimmed, he wrote a single question on six slips of paper — the same words, the same hand, the same crease — and sealed each with the same thumb. The question was older than the cloth: what stands beyond the pass?

For the errand he chose six bearers of light, no two alike. The first carried a tall lamp of caravan glass, fed on oil that crossed the mountain at a price that made the merchants laugh; its flame did not waver. The second had cracked his own lamp on the step that morning and went instead with a friend's lamp, its brass stamped with the crest of another valley, burning the friend's oil. The third and fourth were not walkers only; they carried tools in their belts and had been known to open gates, pry up stones, and carry back what they found. The fifth bore a taper that had been given away at the fair, thin as a straw. The sixth carried no lamp at all, only a coal from the common hearth in a clay pot; it had never once left the square.

The night before, each bearer slept alone in a bolted room, and none had ever carried a question for Osric before, and none was told that any other had been chosen. At dawn they were waked in the dark and brought one by one to the antechamber, where a slate hung on the wall with all six names chalked on it, because Osric was a careful man and chalked what he did. The two with tools would not sit still. They walked the walls of the little room, and before the gate opened they had read the slate down to its last name. The other four kept their eyes on their own knees and noticed nothing. When Osric came in he saw what had happened, and he did not wipe the slate. He opened his day-book and wrote beside the six names: the two with tools read the names.

They went out through the east gate one at a time, and after each the gate was barred again, so that no two bearers ever shared the road or the light. Each carried his folded slip against his chest. They came back at dusk in the order they had left, were led to separate rooms, and each wrote an account of the pass in his own hand, seeing no one and hearing nothing.

Osric spread the six accounts along the edges of the long table, one to a side, so that every account crossed every other in a square. Where two told the same thing in the same words — the same tree, the same grey ridge — he inked the square black. Where they leaned toward each other but did not touch, he used chalk. Where both had burned thin, or where their words could not be laid side by side at all, he marked the square with plain water, and the water dried paler as the night went on. By morning the table was a field of black and white and faint grey squares, and Osric had judged nothing.

He would not read the field himself; he had folded the question, and his eye would favor his own crease. Once a year a pilgrim came down the pass road, and that evening she was in the village. Her lantern was of the same caravan glass as the tall bearer's, only smaller. She sat once at the table, read the whole field in a single sitting, and wrote one page: where the six lights had stood together, where they had split, and which of her own sentences she would not swear to. She pinned the page to the post by the well, and Osric cut the day and the month into the wood beneath it. The cooper ran after her with a question, and she called back over her shoulder that she came once a year, and was already down the road.

The villagers gathered at the well, and those who could read read the page aloud to those who could not, and all went home eased. That evening the shapes moved on the red cloth as they always had, and were read as they always were. A child asked Osric whether the pilgrim had ever stood on the pass herself. Osric looked at the east gate, still barred, and at the slate, still carrying its six names. He said that her lantern was caravan glass, like the first bearer's. The child waited for the rest. The cloth flickered — wolf from dog — and nobody unbarred the gate.

# The Six Rooms

I keep a dog and a lantern, and when the householder swept out his six rooms I lit the lantern at noon, because noon is when a house practices its best manners.

He wanted one answer and was ashamed to lean on a single mouth, so he leaned on six. Around his courtyard stood six small rooms, and that morning every one of them was swept to the boards, lime-washed, given a full water jug and a clean slate, so that nothing of any earlier occupant remained and nothing of one room crossed into another. Six scribes came in by six roads; they had supped the night before at six separate fires, and no two had ever seen each other's faces. The householder copied his question six times with his own hand, the same words in the same ink, and slid one card under each door.

They were not six of a kind, and the dog knew it at once. The first to arrive was tall and expensively shod, come down from the capital, and he settled in as though the room should be grateful. The second carried no papers of his own — his had not come — so he worked under a token borrowed from a richer house, and the token spoke oftener than he did. Two more did not come alone: each kept apprentices to fetch water and bark and to hold the second copy straight, and the apprentices went in and out like shuttles. The fifth worked at the fountain in the market and took payment from no one; his sleeves were wet and his slate was cracked. The sixth lived two streets off and owned plain tools and no guild at all. The dog sniffed all six and found them equally dogs, which is the oldest compliment there is.

While the rooms scratched, the courtyard kept its own small commerce. As each scribe finished, the householder folded his pages, tied them, wrote a title on the fold, and laid the bundle by that scribe's door until evening. The apprentices, going to the well, passed the doors. The dog watched them slow down at each bundle and read the titles, again and again, the way dogs read a fence. They touched no paper. They read only the names of the papers. The dog said nothing; dogs keep things for later.

At dusk an old woman came over the river, and she came once. She carried a thin book and a graver. She took the six bundles to the long table, ruled the table into squares, set every answer against every other answer, and went over the whole table one time, slowly. Where two answers matched through and through, she cut the mark deep into the wood. Where they touched in part, she scored it light. Where she could not say, she left a hollow ring and passed on. No mark was deepened because the square beside it was deep; each square kept the weight her own eyes had given it. She asked no one to explain anything. She wrote the day on the cover, copied the table into the book, and left the book open where anyone could read it.

Before she tied her wrap, the dog came and stood at the table and looked at her, then at the doors, then at her again, the way he asks. I told her plainly what he had seen: the apprentices had been reading the titles at the doors. She took the pen back out. In the margin she wrote that two of the six had kept helpers whose eyes had touched the labels of the others' work, and that the squares touching those two should be held a little lighter for it. She did not scrape the deep marks to make the table prettier, and she did not fold the margin under. I put my lantern to the page, and the page held.

The marks said where the six stood with one another. They said nothing about the pear tree. The question had been which limb to cut, and all evening the householder ran his finger over the squares, hunting for the number that would walk out and lift the saw for him. I spoke to him across the table, because someone owed him a plain word. "Your rooms pretend to be strangers while the corridor carries their names to the well. Your table measures how six men agree, and you read it as though it measured the tree. And you are begging six strangers, a borrowed token, and a wet-sleeved boy to carry your choosing for you, so that your hands stay clean. Stop asking the world for that; it was never selling it. The saw fits your hand tonight as well as it ever will. Keep one thing from this evening: the margin. It is the only thing under this roof my lantern cannot shame — a woman who wrote her own flaw into her own book before supper."

In the morning he cut the limb himself, and the dog went along to supervise. The tall one sent a bill; the fountain boy asked for nothing; both were fed at the same table. The six rooms stand swept and empty now; the margin is still in the book, and it still holds.

# The Six Answers

In the year the river froze early, the elders of Marrowfen had one question and would not give it to a single mouth. When the town was young it had kept an oracle in the tower, and the oracle had said the bridge would hold, and the bridge had taken the wedding party. Nothing was believed the same way after that. So the elders wrote their question on six sheets, word for word identical down to the last comma, and sealed each with black wax. It was the founding question, the old one: whether the bridge would bear weight.

The six answerers came by different roads. The first carried a lantern bought with a winter of silver, and its light reached farthest into the fog. The second owned no lantern but held a key borrowed from a great house, and the key opened gates her own hands could not have built. Two were brothers who could not answer anything without touching it; they meant to walk the bridge plank by plank, hands on the rails. The fifth was thin and carried nothing and asked no fee — what he gave was what anyone could have for the asking. The sixth never came to the town at all; he answered from his own hearth, and his lamp was small, but it was his.

Each was led to a room with a bare table, and the rooms shared no window and no crack. Whatever one wrote could not travel to another's ink. Each broke the wax, read, and wrote.

When the six came out, the elders did not read the papers aloud and did not stack them into one voice. They gave them to the weaver, who set the six answers in a frame six by six, answer against answer, and in every square she stitched the grade of their agreement: dark thread where two answers matched and could be trusted to match, pale thread where they only leaned toward each other. Some elders wanted her to pour a single dye over the whole frame so it would look of a piece. She would not. The pale squares were doing work; a frame all one color tells you nothing about the cloth.

Then a judge was fetched from over the mountain, a man who knew none of the six. He was given the whole frame at once and allowed to speak one time, and whatever he said would stand. He looked until the light went low, gave his verdict, and went home. A scribe copied the verdict into the iron book, where no line once written may be scraped out.

Before the frame was hung in the hall, the weaver found a mark on it. The two brothers, being men who touch things, had paused at the wall where the papers wait, and had read the titles the scribe had given the other four — not the words inside, only the names on the folds. A young elder asked her to wash the mark out; it was small, and clean cloth looks better. The weaver instead cut thread the same weight as the verdict's and sewed the mark into the margin of the cloth itself. Let whoever reads this frame in fifty years read the smudge too, she said. A cloth that hides its own stain gets worn by it.

The elders had hoped, though none of them said it aloud even to themselves, that the fog would part six times onto the same bank. They got three shapes of answer and two pale seams between them, and the seams were where the next question lived. That night the hall stayed unlocked. The frame hung with its dark squares, its pale squares, its verdict in iron letters, and its one sewn mark. Whoever came to read it had to read all four, and could not mistake the cloth for the bridge, or the bridge for the cloth.

# The Measuring of the Field

When the intendant came down into the valley, he did not climb the ridge, and he did not speak to the farmers. He sent for six scribes.

The first arrived in the master's own livery, with the finest letters in the province. The second came in the name of a greater scribe who could not travel, and wore that scribe's silver key at his belt. Two were brothers of a company that worked by errands, famous for opening doors that other scribes were meant only to describe. The fifth asked no fee and brought nothing but his patience. The sixth had never once left the valley, and carried nothing that could not be made from what lay at hand.

The question was a single question. A clerk wrote it out six times on six leaves, word for word, and it was forbidden to add a syllable or take one away. Each scribe was led to his own chamber. The chambers had been swept in the night, the windows left open, the old ink poured out and the wells refilled, so that no trace of any earlier hand survived in them. No scribe saw another's leaf, and none was told what another had answered. Each man sat down to the field as though it had never been described by anyone before.

Four of them wrote from where they sat. The two brothers did not sit. They let themselves out, walked the water channel, opened the gates, counted the furrows aloud, and came back with mud on their boots and their leaves crossed with crossings-out. In the corridor where all six worked there stood a shelf, and on the shelf lay a box for each scribe, and on each lid a few words naming the labor folded inside. The brothers, going in and out as errand-men do, could not help seeing the lids. Afterwards they swore — and the oath was written down — that they had read nothing but those few words, that no lid had been lifted and no page turned by their hands.

When the six leaves were brought to the clerk, he did not read them for pleasure, and he did not choose among them. He ruled a great square on the wall of the registry and set each account against each of the others in turn, and in every compartment of that square he wrote how far he dared believe that those two pages told of the same field. Where two accounts stood together and he trusted the standing, he set a full stroke. Where the words agreed but he suspected the agreement, a broken stroke. Where he could not yet say, a single bare point, and he passed to the next compartment. Fifteen small beliefs, and not one of them the size of another.

The intendant did not leave the square to the valley. He sent to the capital for a doctor, a man none of the six had ever met. The doctor came by the evening coach, declined the ridge, declined the field, declined the farmers waiting at the fence with their caps in their hands. He sat a single afternoon in the registry, read the six leaves and the square in one sitting, never once rising from the chair, and wrote what he wrote. He measured nothing a second time, and no one measured him. Of him the record says only that he came by the evening coach and left by the morning one.

What the doctor wrote was copied in a fair hand and nailed to the church door. After that day, whoever asked about the field was answered with the paper on the church door. The six leaves were bound behind it, the square was kept rolled in the registry, and in the margin of the paper, in a small careful script, stood the brothers' oath about the lids. The intendant, reviewing everything, praised the margin above all the rest. A record that confesses its own flaw, he told the clerk, will be believed longer than a clean one. The clerk wrote that down too.

The farmers were never sent for. One of them, at the inn that winter, began to tell the field as it had been in the flood year — where the water had stood, what the ground had done under it — and the room went kindly quiet, and a hand pointed through the dark at the church door. He was not scolded. He was simply no longer the sort of witness the valley used: he had not begun from nothing; he had lived beside the field for forty years; nothing in him was fresh. And the field itself said nothing, as it always had. It had never learned to write, and no one would read it again except through the six, the square, the margin, and the paper on the door.

# The Door in the Orchard Wall

The master of the valley had one question and six roads. Behind his orchard wall stood a door that no one living had opened, and he wished to know it — but not, chiefly, for itself. He wished to know whether six who were sent alone would come back telling of the same door. So he had a scribe copy the question six times, letter for letter, each copy carrying the same words and no others, and he chose six to bear them.

He chose them strangely, and on purpose. The first was his own strong son, fed all his life at the big table and never refused. The second was a cousin lent for the season by a richer house, carrying that house's gold ring; at every inn the ring was seen before the cousin was. The third and fourth were hired walkers, paid to go wherever the road led and to look into things as they went — looking was their trade, and they practiced it whether or not anyone had asked. The fifth was a wanderer who had asked for no fee and been promised none. The sixth had never in his life left the village square, and knew the orchard wall only from the near side.

Each set out at first light from a different inn, each had slept alone, and each carried nothing but his errand — no letter, no gossip, no whisper that five others rode other roads. The roads did not cross, and the master had seen to it. What the son wanted was to be found faithful. What the cousin wanted was the ring to be admired. The walkers wanted the road itself, which is a wider hunger than any errand. The wanderer wanted bread, and the sixth wanted to be home by dark.

They returned at dusk and gave their accounts one at a time, each into the master's ear alone. The accounts were not the same, and they were not wholly different.

The master's steward was a weaver. She took the six accounts, stretched each as a thread on her loom, and crossed every thread against every other — the son's word against the cousin's, the cousin's against the first walker's, through all fifteen crossings — and at each crossing she tied a knot, and the knots were not alike. Where two threads agreed and neither had a reason to agree, she tied the knot three times. Where two agreed and either might merely have been guessing, she tied it once. Where two agreed and she could not say why, she laid the threads side by side and ran a pale cord through them, so that whoever read the cloth later would know to carry that crossing loosely. The cloth came off the loom heavy at its center, where the knots were thickest, and gauzy at its edges.

Now, the hired walkers, being walkers, had passed the inns before first light and had read the names chalked on the doors — the son's name, the cousin's, the wanderer's. They had heard no word of what the others said; they knew only who had been sent. When the steward learned of it, she did not cut the knowledge out of the cloth. She wove a thin dark thread along the whole edge of the two walkers' threads, so the flaw rode in the border where any eye could find it, and she taught the household to read the border first.

Last, the cloth was folded and carried over the pass to the aunt who reads once. By an old law of the mountain she takes one question from a house in a season: the whole cloth goes up in a single journey, she looks at it a single time, and what she says is written on the back and never amended, whatever comes. She looked a long while, said that the center held and that the pale crossings should be carried exactly as they were tied, and closed her door.

The master hung the cloth in the hall where the household passed it daily, pale cords and dark border and all. Of the door itself he was heard to say almost nothing. But that same night he sat at his table with the scribe again, drawing roads — more than six now — and thinking less about doors than about which of his carriers he would trust with the next one.

# The Architecture of the Six Alcoves

On the twenty-third morning of the tenth month, the inspectorate of the ward convened an inquiry to test whether an invariant rule could be drawn from six separate mouths without collusion. Power does not ask what men remember; it begins by washing them clean so that only the question remains. Before dawn, the bailiff scrubbed six stone alcoves along the gallery with lime, removing every scrap of yesterday’s chalk, every prior confession, and every personal notebook. Into each alcove he placed a blank sheet of vellum, an identical iron nib, and a word-for-word transcript of the same inquiry, stamped from a single copper plate. To be examined was first to be stripped of history: none of the six who entered was permitted to bring a pocketbook or recall a prior sitting.

Though the examination treated all six as equivalent units before the question, the apparatus had gathered them from starkly different orders of service. The first alcove received the grand chronicler of the high guild, whose heavy press normally commanded the largest fee in the province. The second was supposed to hold a regular notarized clerk, but because that clerk was barred at the gate, a substitute from the foreign exchange entered in his stead, admitted by a borrowed merchant’s brass passkey. The third and fourth alcoves were assigned to two brothers of the mendicant order of couriers—men trained not merely to sit and write, but to walk the corridors, open cabinets, and fetch their own instruments; one brother had been summoned from the distant watchtower, while the other worked from the magistrate’s home desk. The fifth alcove held an unlicensed street scribe from the open commons who worked without tariff or patron. The sixth held a cloistered monk who lived permanently in the cellar beneath the courtyard and never crossed the outer wall.

Once the oak shutters were bolted, no voice, signal, or draft of ink passed between the alcoves. Each writer labored in solitary visibility before his blank sheet, forbidden to see a single sentence another hand composed. Yet an architecture built to enforce blindness always betrays the habits of the bodies it employs. Whereas the guild chronicler, the passkey substitute, the commons scribe, and the cellar monk remained fixed behind their grilles and received their paper through a narrow slot, the two courier brothers were accustomed to crossing the anteroom to gather their own quills and folders. On the cedar rack beside the cistern stood six empty portfolios awaiting the finished sheets, each bearing a pasted slip inscribed with the designation of its alcove and occupant. As the two brothers fetched their supplies, their eyes passed over the rack. They never saw a word written inside another alcove, yet they read the names upon the outside of the six portfolios and knew who sat beside them in the dark.

At noon the shutters were unbolted, the six sheets were collected, and the examinees were dismissed without learning what the others had written. The warden did not allow the six to debate their differences, nor did he summon a council. Instead, he sent the bundle down to the deep soundings, where a solitary adjudicator—a seventh jurist who had occupied none of the alcoves—sat behind a green baize table. This jurist was permitted only a single, uninterrupted sitting: he could ask no follow-up questions, return no sheet for revision, and call no witness back. Spreading the six testimonies side by side, he ruled a great cross-referenced table upon a broadsheet, matching each witness against every assertion. Rather than marking claims merely right or wrong, he stamped each square of the grid with one of several graded dies—heavy bronze for unshaken certainty, scored silver for probable agreement, faint lead for a wavering inference—so that agreement itself became a measured hierarchy of confidence.

Before sealing the broadsheet into the ward’s permanent archive, the adjudicator appended a narrow marginal entry at the foot of the table. In it he recorded that the two courier brothers, by virtue of the very liberty of movement that made them useful to the house, had seen the labels on the neighboring portfolios in the anteroom. The inquiry was not annulled by this confession; rather, by inscribing the flaw into the official report, the tribunal transformed a breach of isolation into proof of its own vigilance, closing the dossier in a single stroke of the pen.

# The Six Booths of the Cartographer

In late October, the Bureau of Surveys ceased sending men into the provinces. A territory is stubborn, muddy, and full of accidents; a reproduction is clean, and six reproductions that agree are more operative than the province they replace. To prove the fidelity of an unvisited coast, the Director erected six pine booths in a windowless gallery and summoned six draughtsmen of deliberately unequal station.

First came the Chief Engraver, who worked with the Bureau's heaviest brass pantograph at its highest tension. Second came a Chartered Proxy, admitted only because he carried a gilded token rented from a foreign syndicate in place of the usual warrant. Third and fourth were two Roaming Apprentices from the same guildhall, one stationed at a portable lectern and the other at a heavy oak workbench, both permitted to leave their benches and fetch their own parchment from the vestry. Fifth was a Market Scribe who worked for nothing under an open awning, using common charity charcoal. Sixth was a Cellar Potter who never left the basement, scratching his contours onto damp domestic clay with a bone stylus.

Before the bolt was drawn on each booth, the attendants scrubbed the workbenches with lye until not a smudge of an earlier province remained. No draughtsman was permitted a notebook, a memory of yesterday's coast, or a speaking-tube to his neighbors. Through six brass slots, at the same stroke of the bell, six identical slips of vellum were dropped, bearing word for word the same dry description of headlands none of them had ever seen. Each man began at zero, enclosed in an artificial virginity of mind, as though isolation itself were a guarantee of truth.

The precision of their solitude was the Bureau's pride, and like all ceremonies of purity, it harbored the exact contamination required to make it credible. While the Cellar Potter, the Market Scribe, the Chartered Proxy, and the Chief Engraver remained bolted behind their pine planks, the Roaming Apprentices, permitted by their warrant to walk the gallery and hang their own sheets on the common drying line, passed the cedar rack where the finished rolls already hung. Neither apprentice unrolled another man's parchment; the lead clasps held fast. Yet both of them read the outer tags tied to the ribbons of the other rolls. Seeing the titles and the ciphers lettered on the outside of the cylinders, they knew which rivals labored beside them and how the unmapped bays were being named. One does not need to see the landscape if one can read the catalog of those who are fabricating it. The gallery watchman recorded this glimpsing of the tags in the margin of his chronicle, not to halt the ceremony, but to supply the meticulous confession of error by which an artifice launders its own authority.

At dusk the six renderings were spread upon a great checkered baize divided into intersecting rows and columns. No rider was dispatched to the shore to see whether a promontory stood where the charcoal was thick. Instead, the tabulators crossed every draughtsman's rendering against the other five. Where all six contours bent together, the square of the grid was stamped with a heavy gold disk for highest assurance; where four converged and two drifted, the square received a silver ring for moderate trust; where a reef appeared on one sheet alone, it was dusted with gray chalk as a solitary fancy. By measuring the copies exclusively against one another, the checkered baize generated an original that had never existed in earth or water. Consensus among signs replaced the shore, and the graded stamps of assurance gave the phantom coast a density that rock and tide could never match.

Yet a grid of echoes still requires an external signature to close the circuit. At midnight the Director admitted a solitary Foreign Assayer into the gallery. By statute this arbiter was granted a single sitting and a single uninterrupted glance: he could ask no questions, summon no draughtsman back to the bench, and call for no second stroke of the stylus. He passed his lens once over the checkered baize, weighed the gold disks against the gray chalk, transcribed the watchman's marginal caveat regarding the outer tags on the drying rack, and inscribed one summary folio declaring what remained constant across all six renderings. When his quill lifted, the gallery was locked. Should the actual sea rise tomorrow and swallow the headlands, the Bureau would not amend a single line; the ships of the realm now steer by the summary folio, and where the coast disagrees with the sixfold convergence, it is the coast that is treated as a counterfeit.

# The Sixfold Hunger of the Assay

On the twenty-third day of the tenth month, the autumn grain was already Measured in the bins, yet the master of the hill-estate could not rest. An institution is built upon a premise of what men actually want, not what they argue in council, and the master hungered for a premise that did not depend on the flattery or habit of a single counselor. A lone adviser always bends his reasoning to feed his own standing; therefore the strategist resolved to ride six separate hungers at once, treating the desires of different stations as fixed data to be weighed side by side. He summoned six drafters of markedly different provenance to the stone assay-house: the guild’s chief master-craftsman, heavy with honours and costly training; a hired merchant-factor carrying a purchased warrant to stand in the place of an absent lord; two roving bailiffs trained in the same brotherhood—one weathered from the saddle, one ink-stained from the counting-desk; a young commons-scribe who worked the open market without fee for the sheer appetite of proving his craft; and a quiet cellar-monk fed entirely on the estate’s own barley, who never crossed the outer ditch.

The privacy instinct is strong in any creature asked to name what it truly sees, yet privacy in an old workshop is easily counterfeited by shared custom. Therefore, before the bell struck noon, each of the six was led into a newly lime-washed booth from which every scrap of yesterday’s parchment, charcoal, and rumour had been scrubbed away. The master knew that memory in an old servant is often only habit dressed as judgment, and he wanted their first syllogism to begin from raw appetite rather than borrowed premise. Through six brass slots pushed at the same instant, each worker received a wax tablet stamped with the exact same question: what is the true load-bearing wall of this household’s power, and what hunger keeps it upright? The oak partitions between the booths were bolted fast; no speaking-horn, no messenger, and no passing shadow was permitted to carry a syllable from one chamber to the next while the tallow burned down.

Yet the machinery of any enterprise has seams where human nature presses through. Four of the workers sat still where they were placed, waiting for clean vellum and wax to be handed to them through the slot. The two roving bailiffs, however, were accustomed by the habits of their brotherhood to walk the passage and fetch their own ink-horns, straight-edges, and drying-sand from the vestibule cupboard before shooting their bolts. As they stepped to the press, their eyes fell upon six empty leather satchels hanging on the pegs, each stitched on the outside with the title and origin of one of the day’s six witnesses. Neither bailiff could open another’s satchel, for all hung empty awaiting the finished scrolls, and neither spoke a word; yet as they returned to their booths and fastened their latches, both carried into their solitude the knowledge of who else had been called to the test, and the quiet social instinct to measure their own voice against the company named on the hooks.

At dusk the six scrolls were drawn from the slots and spread before the steward of the table upon a broad checkered cloth divided into squares. Down one margin the steward inscribed every appetite and claim the witnesses had named; across the top he set the six witnesses in their order. He did not merely mark whether two voices agreed, for agreement born of timid guessing has no weight beside agreement wrung from necessity. In each square of the cloth he laid a counter to mark the heat and certainty of that witness’s testimony—heavy lead where a claim was asserted as fixed nature, scored brass where it was offered as probable custom, and pale chalk where it was only a passing suspicion. To run against the grain of a row heavy with lead would be to legislate against the weather; where four or five lead counters settled into the same line, the cloth sagged visibly along that seam, showing where rival hungers had struck the same immovable datum.

The master did not call the six back to debate their differences, knowing that men gathered in a room soon abandon the search for what is wanted in order to satisfy the sharper desire of prevailing over their neighbours. Instead, he sent the checkered cloth and the six scrolls down to the solitary sounder who lived beside the deep quarry-pool—an examiner hired to read the whole cloth at a single sitting, without pause, second audience, or reply. In one unbroken watch before midnight, the sounder weighed the rows of lead, brass, and chalk, noted on the margin that the two roving bailiffs had seen the named satchels on the vestibule pegs, and bound his single parchment of findings into the estate’s permanent archive, leaving the six booths silent and the master’s hunger fed.

# The Valley Agreed

On the twenty-third morning of October the institute sealed a single writ and had it copied six times without variation. The writ asked one thing: cross the river, walk the valley, and bring back a chart of what stands there. Six were engaged to answer. Each was lodged at a different edge of the town, each had been kept from speech since the writ was sealed, and each set out at first light with an empty satchel — for the rule of the house was that the valley must be met as if for the first time, by everyone, all at once.

The six were not of one kind. The first came down from the capital in a lacquered case, the fine instrument of a fine house, attended by footmen who carried nothing else. The second arrived in place of another man, holding a pass stamped by a rich guild; the pass was not his, but it opened the same bridges. The third and fourth were walkers, men with keys on their belts who went through the valley on their own feet, unlatching what stood shut, kneeling to read what was cut into the stones. The fifth took no fee; he worked in sight of the public fountain, where anyone might watch his hand move. The sixth never crossed the river at all: he stayed at his mother's table in a small village and drew from what he could hold in his two hands.

By dusk the charts came back, and the institute did not hang them side by side to be admired. It ruled a wall into rows and columns — a row for each of the six, a column for every landmark the writ had named — and into every square where two charts could be made to speak of the same thing it pressed a bead of wax. The wax was poured in three grades. Clear wax said: here they all spoke alike, and firmly. Clouded wax said: here they agree, but thinly, or only some of them. Dark wax said: here they contradict one another or fall silent, and whoever calls this knowledge is selling something. Square by square the wall filled, and the valley that had been walked that morning became a surface of small lights.

Then the assessor came. He was famous, he arrived by the evening coach, and he had been engaged for one visit. He did not cross the river. He walked the wall once, from the top row to the bottom, gave his judgment in a single sitting, and it was written down whole, copied onto one great sheet together with the wax, and hung where anyone might read it. No one asked him a second question; a second asking would have been a second engagement, and the institute had provided for one. His verdict was not set against the valley, because the valley cannot be consulted. It was set against the wall, which can.

In the margin of the hung sheet a junior had written a small line, and the institute, to its credit, did not erase it: that the two walkers, going to the instrument cabinet at midday, had passed the shelf where the finished carriers lay waiting, and had read the names inked on their straps. They had not opened the carriers. They had seen no lines and no landmarks — only that this rival's strap said one thing and that rival's said another. The line was allowed to stand, and once it stood, everyone who studied the sheet studied the walkers' two rows a moment longer, as though a name, having been seen, had done something.

It had. From that day the walkers' squares were read differently — not because their charts differed, but because their makers had passed signboards that pointed at works they never read. A name is a small thing. It refers to nothing and it changes everything it touches. The wax in their squares was not re-graded. It was only looked at longer, which amounts to the same thing.

The sheet is still there. Travelers come to consult it the way their fathers consulted rivers. They ask where the spring rises, and the sheet tells them, and they go down the steps satisfied, and the spring is wherever the clear wax is thickest. No one has crossed the river since. A path leads to it from the institute, and grass has grown across the first stones. It was not that the six charts lied, nor even that they disagreed; it is that their agreement no longer needs the valley. The junior points this out to visitors, at the margin, under the wax. The visitors nod, copy the sheet, and ask whether the copy will do as well. He tells them it always has.

# The Six Cabins on the Breakwater

On the twenty-third morning of the tenth month, the bailiff of the upper estate cut a single horn stencil concerning the threshold custom and declared its meaning plain enough to survive any ear. Old Marrow, who kept the gallery chair above the breakwater, did not argue with the bailiff's hope. Before noon he struck six impressions from that one horn plate, not a comma altered between them, and carried them down the pier to six timber cabins above the tide.

Each cabin held a reader of a different station and craft. In the first worked the master chronicler of the great furnace, accustomed to heavy brass and long breath. In the second waited a chartered deputy sent in place of an absent guildsman, admitted by a borrowed silver tally paid for at the harbor steps. In the third and fourth worked twin field-surveyors of the same brotherhood, one from the orchard cottage and the other from the counting-house desk, both permitted to leave their benches to fetch compasses and twine. In the fifth sat a commons scribe from the open fish-market who took no coin for his labor. In the sixth, down in a salt-dry vault cut off from the harbor bells, worked a cloistered brother who used only tools made within his own chamber. Before the sheets were handed in, apprentices swept every cabin bare of old drafts, laid out clean vellum and new charcoal, and dropped heavy cedar partitions into their grooves so no murmur or gesture could pass between stalls.

Halfway through the afternoon, the twin field-surveyors stepped out by their back hatches into the tool-shed to dress their dividers at the whetstone. Hanging on a cedar rack beside the oil-cans were the six branded leather pouches prepared for the evening collection, each stamped on the flap with the title of its cabin. Neither surveyor touched a pouch or glimpsed another's vellum, and both returned to their stalls in silence. Watching from the gallery, Marrow saw their eyes linger on the rack. He did not halt the afternoon's work or hide the mishap; he took his knife and notched the sighting across the top beam of the day's tally so anyone weighing the harvest later would know the two surveyors had seen the labels.

When the tide turned and the six vellum sheets were brought up to the gallery, the bailiff reached out to sort them into those that had obeyed his meaning and those that had erred. Marrow drew the bundle back. In the furnace-chronicler's sheet and the chartered deputy's lines, the bailiff's premise stood intact, rendered in the high grammar the upper estate favored. The commons scribe from the fish-market accepted the boundary rules the stencil described, yet bent their terms to protect the net-menders' path, bargaining with the frame while keeping its bones. The cloistered brother in the salt-vault stripped away the ceremonial preambles altogether, reading the threshold custom against its grain as a schedule of locks rather than a promise of shelter. None had misread the horn plate; each had poured the same cut words through the vessel of his own trade.

Marrow spread a checkered sailcloth across the gallery trestle—twelve ribs of cord running lengthwise for the claims inside the stencil, crossed by six chalked columns for the cabins on the pier. Into every square where a cabin's vellum met a claim, he laid one of three markers: a flat disk of lead where the reading rested squarely on the cut words of the stencil, a split ring of horn where one reading pulled against another's testimony, and a hollow pebble of chalk where a cabin asserted what the stencil neither proved nor denied. Across the cloth a pattern appeared that no single cabin could have seen alone—four rows solid with lead from edge to edge, three rows striped with horn where the surveyors parted from the vault, and two rows pale with chalk where every reader had guessed at what the bailiff left unsaid.

Even then, Marrow refused to sum the cloth himself, knowing that the man who tends the gallery shares the habits of the pier. He rolled the six vellum sheets together with the marked cloth, sealed them in one pitched cylinder, and lowered them by rope to the fathom-judge whose cutter anchored at the mouth of the bay. That judge held no speech with the cabins and asked no second question; by the light of a single lantern he read the whole bundle through in one sitting and sent up a stitched parchment naming the four ribs that had held fast across every craft, together with the notch recording what the two surveyors had seen in the tool-shed. Marrow pinned that parchment to the post, then turned his chair toward the mudflats beyond the breakwater, where the cockle-gatherers walked home through the surf—reminded that even when six benches on the pier agree, their chart never captures how the bell sounds outside the seawall.

# The Six Copies

The inscription on the mill's lintel had been feeding on damp for forty years, and the miller, whose father had cut it, decided to learn what it said before the last letter went the way of the first. He sent for six strangers, and his daughter copied out the request six times in her own hand, word for word, on six cards of the same size, so that no card held a word the others lacked and no card wanted one. I was lodged above the stable that season, and the miller, who knew my taste for small accidents, asked me to watch the six and tell him afterward what repeated.

Each stranger was given a room in the granary. The rooms were swept the evening before, and the paper in them was new. Whatever each man had written in his life stayed outside the threshold; the walls were thick; the six had never worked at the same bench; and there was not so much as an inkwell that served two rooms.

The eldest arrived first, with a case of pens that filled a small trunk and a hand that had never once hurried. The second was not the man the miller had written to; that one was kept away by weather, and in his place came a substitute, entering on a ticket of stamped silver that someone had bought at full price at the gate. Then came two who could not be kept in rooms — the cards bound their pens, not their feet — and after them a boy who had been admitted for nothing and knew it, and a quiet man who asked only for a table and then set out, from his satchel, his own candle, his own water, his own ink, and touched nothing else the house offered.

The two restless ones did their reading on their feet. To judge the worn letters they climbed to the chute, and the way to the chute passed the shelf where the finished sheets lay drying, each folded once and marked on its fold with the writer's name. Not a word inside could be read — the folds were pasted with flour — but a name asks nothing of a reader, and I watched both of them slow on that stair, the way a man slows on a stair he has been told has rotted. The elder of the two went by twice.

In the office the daughter stretched a cloth ruled into squares, a row for each hand and a column for each word of the recovered line, and in every square she set the word that hand had given for that place. Where a word stood in three rows or more, she pressed the ink in full; where it stood in two, she thinned it to a wash; where it stood in one, it went down in pencil. "Three hands," she said, not looking up, "or it stays thin." Against the two rows of the restless ones she set a small standing sign — these hands passed the shelf of names — and where those two agreed with each other and with no one else, the wash went down paler still.

The miller wanted the cloth judged once and judged whole. A single examiner came up from the town, read the six copies and the cloth from breakfast to dusk, asked nothing twice, wrote one page, signed it, and left his own stone on it in the office, so that anyone might lift the stone and read what he had found, doubt and all — the sign against the restless rows stood on the page as plainly as the darkest ink. Offered a bed, he walked home by lantern; asked whether he might be sent for again if the household fell to arguing, he said the page would not improve with a second sitting.

I stayed one more day, out of habit; my trade has always been the small accidents, and the cloth was richer in them than any dream I have ever taken down. Six men deaf to one another writing the same word into the same square is not luck — that is the stone speaking through whatever hands will hold still for it. On the last morning I stood under the lintel with the eldest, who had come to take his leave of it. He read the recovered line easily and stumbled only at the one word that stood in pencil, the word a single hand had offered and no second had confirmed — as if his throat already knew which words three strangers had paid for and which one man had only dreamed.

# The Six Sighting-Booths

On the twenty-third afternoon of the tenth month, the master of the terrace arranged six sighting-booths along the parapet to test whether six different instruments would score the same ridges from a single whalebone comb. He cared nothing for what picture the artisans thought they were drawing; he watched only how each housing bent the neck, narrowed the eye, or lengthened the reach of the worker inside it.

Heavy felt curtains were dropped between the booths so that neither breath nor shadow could pass from one stall to the next. At the stroke of the bell, the master walked the line and pressed a whalebone comb notched with the same seven teeth into each artisan's palm.

Yet no two booths held the body in the same ratio. In the first stall stood the great counterweighted brass telescope, the heaviest engine on the ridge, whose massive flywheel steadied the wrist but locked the observer's chin into an iron cradle. In the second stall, the eastern crystal that had been promised for the frame had not arrived by the morning wagon; the artisan worked instead through a borrowed guild-glass obtained with a merchant's brass voucher, squinting through an aperture ground for a stranger's brow. In the fifth stall, under a canvas awning open to the highroad, a lad worked with a light tin blow-tube offered without toll to any traveler; its thin wall quivered with every cart on the cobbles. In the sixth stall, sunk in the cool stone vault beneath the courtyard, a solitary grinder turned a compact hand-quern by touch alone, cut off from the sky and listening only to the grit beneath his thumb.

Those four instruments amputated the feet. Once the eye or the ear was wedded to the tube or the stone, the worker could not cross the floor, and the world shrank to the copper plate on the knee.

The third and fourth stalls were built on a different plan. One stood in a timber annex at the garden's edge, the other at the broad oak workbench in the main hall. Neither strapped the artisan to a fixed eyepiece. Instead, both workers were fitted with long, jointed iron tongs and a harness of sliding trays that required them to stand, pace their floorboards, and fetch sharp burins from the central drying-rack in the transom wall. Because their tools extended the hand rather than fixing the eye, the room itself became part of what they held. Whenever the joiner in the annex or the craftsman in the hall reached through the transom for a clean burin, his gaze swept the wooden pegs where six stamped pewter tags hung in a row, each engraved with the title of one of the six stalls on the terrace. Neither man unfastened the leather pouches hanging beneath the neighbor tags, nor glimpsed a single stroke of a neighbor's copper. Yet merely reading those five titles on the pegs altered the poise of their wrists: a man who knows five titled fellows are cutting beside him braces his shoulder differently from a man who believes he works alone in the dark.

At sundown the felt curtains were raised, and the six etched plates were carried to the counting-table. None of the six artisans was permitted to speak or weigh the work. Instead, the master summoned a solitary sounder from the deep harbor—a man who lived by the plumb-line and owed nothing to the workshops on the hill. He was granted a single burning-glass and a single turn of the sand-glass. Without pausing for a second draught of air or asking a question of the stalls, the harbor-sounder swept all six copper plates in one unbroken gaze.

Before him lay a cedar board ruled into six columns for the stalls and cross-ruled with rows for each ridge of the whalebone comb. As his glass traveled across the metal, he did not merely mark whether a ridge was present or absent. Where a stall had cut a groove deep and clean, he dropped a heavy lead pellet into the square; where the burin had chattered, he laid a split reed; where the copper showed only a faint scratch of doubt, he dusted the square with white chalk. Within that single sitting, the checkered board revealed where the six instruments met as one like bells struck together, and where a thin tin tube or a dark vault had starved the hand.

Before lifting his glass, the harbor-sounder noticed the wider bevel on the third and fourth plates—the unmistakable signature of men who had worked while glancing over their shoulders. He walked to the transom rack, beheld the six pewter tags hanging in plain view of the tongs, and returned to the cedar board. # The Same Door

On the morning the frost took the gates, the house gave six of its people one errand. Each was handed the same letter, closed with the same wax, asking the same thing of whoever broke it, and each was led from a different room in which they had slept alone. At the threshold their pockets were emptied. Nothing one had made was passed to another; nothing one had seen was spoken to another before they went. The house wanted six strangers to the errand, and it went to some trouble to manufacture them.

The six were not alike, though the letters were. The first was the strongest of the house, and he dressed for the errand as for a winter, carrying everything he might need and several things no one had ever needed, and he went slowly under it. The second owned no road; he traveled the toll way and paid at every stone, and he came back quickly and polished, with the toll-keeper's stamp faint on all he had handled. Two were given lanterns and the freedom of the corridor — only they could walk it — and a lantern is not a neutral thing: whoever carries one begins to look at what it shows, and then to speak of the looking. The fifth was a boy whose wooden token opened only the small gate, the one free to anyone; he went out light and came back light. The sixth never left at all. He kept his own hearth and did the errand from there, reporting the door as a man reports weather heard through a wall — which was less, and no one could move him off it, for nothing from outside had ever reached him.

One at a time they stood at the same door, and afterward each told what was found. All six spoke of the iron hinge, and there their tellings lay together like spoons in a drawer. Fewer agreed about the wood beyond it. The strong man's account was long and carved at the edges; the toll rider's was shorter and very smooth; the boy said only that the door was heavy and that he had pushed it as far as it would go, which was not far, and he did not pretend otherwise. The hearth-keeper gave the sound of it, and the width, as well as a wall allows. The two with lanterns gave the door its due and then gave a great deal about the reaching of it.

Those two, on their way, passed the alcove where the six satchels waited for the return, and each of them, being a person who looks, read the names stitched on the other satchels. They touched nothing. No one accused them of anything. But a name read is a room entered, and when their tellings came back, the house could not say which part had been fetched from the door and which had drifted in from the alcove. The margin of that day's record says so, in small letters, and does not say it as an excuse.

When the six tellings lay on the long table, a registrar took a fresh sheet and ruled it into squares, the six down one side and the parts of the door across the top — hinge, wood, threshold, what lay beyond. In each square where tellings met she set a mark: black ink where they spoke alike and had earned it; soft pencil where they leaned together but thinly; and in some squares nothing, left blank on purpose, since a blank can be looked at without lying. Read straight down, the sheet showed where the door was solid and where it was still mostly weather.

Then the house hired one reader from beyond the river. He came for a single evening, sat at a single lamp, read the ruled sheet and the six tellings once through, and wrote one page. He was not asked back and did not offer. The page was nailed up beside the record-room door where anyone could stand and read it, and the nail was left crooked on purpose, so the page could be taken down and another put in its place the next time the errand went out.

The letters were burned that night; being identical, they had nothing left to say. The differences among the tellings remained, and every difference wore the shape of its carrier's gear — the certainty as heavy as the pack, the smoothness with the toll road in it, the narrowness of the small free gate, the wall between the hearth and the world. In the morning the rooms were swept, the lanterns trimmed, the sheet ruled again, and the blank squares were left exactly as they were.

# The Table of Stones

The guild received a letter in a script nobody in town could read, and the guild wanted two things: to know what it said, and to be believed when they told the town. They sent for me. I had spent a life arranging what towns believe — about soap, about candidates, about the safety of the harbor in a cholera summer — and I told them the plain truth of my trade: belief is not argued into people. It is built, out of materials they already trust. Numbers are one. Stones are another.

We fetched six translators. The first was famous and came under a costly contract. The second wore a borrowed badge and stood in for a better-known man who could not travel; he was paid by the hour and knew his craft. The third and fourth were local men who kept their own carts and ran their own errands across town, used to going where the work was without asking leave. The fifth asked no fee and worked at the inn for the price of the room. The sixth lived above the kiln, owned his books, and answered to nobody. Six different roads into the same craft — that was the point of choosing them so.

Each was given a room of his own at the guild house. The letter was copied six times by one hand, and each copy was read to its translator from the same scroll, word for word, twice, so that no man could hear it differently. Each began with clean paper, his own ink, and nothing else — no notes from the others, no gossip carried in, no walls in common worth the name. They did not meet, they did not dine together. When a man finished, his pages went into a satchel with his trade-mark chalked on the flap, the satchel went to the counting room, and the man went home by a different stair.

On the fourth day the guild laid the six texts side by side on the long table and set clerks to compare every man's pages against every other man's, fifteen pairings in all. Where two texts said the same thing in the same place, the clerk set a stone in the square ruled out for that pair: a white stone for plain agreement, a grey stone for agreement that needed charity, a red stone for a resemblance nobody should lean on. Square by square the table filled, and when it was finished the stones made it look like arithmetic, and a town will believe arithmetic without being told to. I asked the master whether six men agreeing meant the letter had been read well or only read alike. He looked at the stones and chose the stones.

I sent for one judge, a grammarian from the coast with no stake in the guild. He came once, read all six texts and the whole table in a single morning, wrote his finding in a fair hand, signed it, and left on the evening boat. Nobody asked him a second question. His single pass was declared the finding, bound in grey cloth, dated the twenty-third day of the tenth month, and shelved in the hall where any citizen may pull it down.

Then the master came to me, uncomfortable, with the matter of the errand-men. The third and fourth translators, being men who moved about freely, had carried their own satchels down the corridor, and both had passed the shelf where the finished work waited, and both had read the trade-marks chalked on the flaps. They had seen whose work lay beside whose. Rub it out, said the master. I told him no. A record that confesses a small fault is believed twice as long as one that claims to be clean, because a man who admits one fault is assumed to have hidden none. The paragraph stayed, in small ink, at the foot of the finding.

What the town took, the town carries easily. The fishmonger quotes the white stones at the quay as if he had counted them himself, and the buyers nod. In the hall the grey binding gathers dust at the height of a man's eye, and the small ink at its foot has never once been read aloud. Passing the market, I hear my stones at work, and I am reminded of the oldest fact of my trade: the version a person can hold in one hand is the version that survives, and whatever did not fit in that hand stays on the shelf. The grey cloth has not been taken down since the week it was bound. The grammarian's boat has not come back.
