# The Bell and the Wall

When the bell rang, the people came up from the town, and none of them remembered why they had quarreled last time, or what they had agreed. A woman asked the man beside her his name; he gave it, she gave hers, and they shook hands like strangers, because they were. The bell-keeper had watched this every gathering of her life: the hall filling with neighbors meeting for the first time. Someone always asked about the ford, and she always pointed through the doors, down toward the square.

The wall stood where four streets met, shingled from corner to eave with painted tablets, each one a decision in the recorder's even hand. She kept the brush and the nails in a box at the foot of it, and no other hands touched either. A weaver once asked her to take down the tablet that named him badly; she reached for a fresh shingle instead, painted his grievance on it, and nailed it beneath the first. Nothing ever came off the wall, and nothing kept anyone from standing at it. Fishwives read at noon, children traced the letters, travelers set down their bundles and read. Each evening the stonemason's boy read the newest tablets aloud, and whatever he said, that was what the town had.

Inside, the hall arranged itself as it always did, though nobody remembered arranging it. Along the north rail hung the lanterns. They never left the building and never spoke; when a matter was put, the bell-keeper read the rail, and every flame that stood steady counted as yes. Below the rail sat the benches of the ten trades — the dyers beside the tanners, the smiths beside the coopers — each trade speaking through whoever it had sent up that morning. Nearest the door, facing all the rest, sat the five with covered faces, called only by their work: the one who kept the purse, the one who met strangers at the gate, the one who minded the works and the water, the one who kept the tale, and the one whose whole office was mockery. Each of the five had a second standing at the shoulder, sworn to find the flaw in whatever the mask proposed. When the keeper of the tale spoke, her second quoted the wall against her; when the mocker laughed, his second went hunting for what the laughter had missed.

Before any business, the bell-keeper recited the five laws. Four were old habit, about the hour of the bell, the counting of the light, the conduct of the trades, and the turning of the masks. The fifth could stop a morning: the bell could not call a gathering while the loft above the hall held fifteen empty jars or fewer. The loft had to keep more than fifteen empties, always, and so one market day, with the loft standing at fourteen, the five masks sat in the square with everybody else, waiting on the jars like anyone.

When the tanners fell out over the ford, they sent for the examiners. Old Petra, who had been crier for forty years and carried every gathering in her head as though she had sat through them all, stood up to swear to what had been said. The examiners thanked her and walked past her to the wall. They asked no lantern, no mask, no bench. They read the tablets, counted the nails, and gave the ford to whatever the paint said. A member's word was weather, they said; the wall was the only witness they would take.

One winter a man came through with a torch and told the square the town would be free of every old obligation if the wall went up in flame. The recorder did not reach for water. "Burn it," she said, "and nothing was ever decided. The ford belongs to nobody, the purse owes nobody, the trades never met, and you are standing in an empty street." The crowd smothered his spark under their coats, unhurried, and he left ahead of his own smoke.

Strangers read the wall differently, and in time the recorder stopped minding. The tablet about the ford was an order in the ferryman's eyes, and he ran his crossings by it. A farmer read the same tablet as a bargain he would keep by half; he took the crossing and paid no toll. A tinker from over the hill read a wall built to tell him where he did not belong, and moved on. All three had read truly. None of them had read what she painted. There was no arguing that any one of them was wrong, and she had learned what the nail teaches: the writing stopped being hers the moment it was hung.

At dusk the bell rang a second time and the hall emptied in a breath, everyone scattering without carrying so much as a grudge, the way water leaves a basin. The lanterns kept their rail. The wall held everything else, in the dark, saying it to whoever came.

# The Assembly of Strangers

A doctor came down from the capital to the river town to study how it ruled itself. He had spent thirty years listening to people who misplaced their own words, and he had heard that this town had arranged its forgetting in an unusual way.

When a matter could not be settled in the streets, a drum was beaten under the plane tree and the town gathered — and it gathered as strangers. Not strangers to the town; strangers to the matter, to every quarrel that had gone before. Whoever came, came new. A midwife sat beside the ferryman; neither knew what the last gathering had done, and neither was expected to. They heard the matter read aloud as though for the first time, because for them it was. The doctor attended three full gatherings before he believed it. In the capital he kept patients who lost each night whatever the day had settled; they wrote notes and pinned them to their sleeves, and he thought of them often in the square.

The gathering had a shape. On the raised stone steps sat the elders, who held their places by tenure and spoke first. Below them stood the speakers of the ten crafts, one voice for each trade, weavers to tanners. Nearest the fountain sat five figures in masks: one who counted the town's coin; one who kept speech with the far towns across the water; one who minded the bridges, the weirs, the waterwheel; one whose whole office was telling the town what had happened, and who read from the book and from nothing else; and one in a motley hood whose office was mockery, permitted to say aloud what no one else might say. Behind each of the five stood a second person, appointed and paid, whose employment was to prove that mask wrong. When the counter of coin declared the town could afford the new weir, his shadow declared at once that it could not, and unrolled his figures. The mocker's shadow was paid to take the jokes seriously and demolish them; the doctor noticed the town laughed longest at the jokes that survived.

The town's memory lived in one place: a book on the stone lectern by the fountain, open to the air, without a lock. Children learning their letters trailed fingers down its columns; a stranger off the ferry could read the town's whole account of itself, and the doctor found, in a careful hand, every decision the gatherings had ever made. One hand wrote it. The clerk of the fountain held the only pen that touched the page. One evening a dispute ran past midnight and a visiting scribe offered a second pen to hurry the record along; the clerk laid his arm across the book, and the gathering waited for the single hand.

A tanner came to the fountain with his cap in his hands and asked the clerk to take back a line entered against him. The clerk heard him out, drew a single stroke through the line, hard enough to bend the nib, and the words stood beneath the stroke as legible as the day they were written. The tanner read them again, put on his cap, and went home.

The doctor asked the teller of what happened whether the town had ever lost its book. In the grandfathers' time, the mask said, a lamp fell in the record-house, and the town learned what fire means there: everything it swallowed, the town had never decided. The treaty with the far town stopped binding, because nothing could show it had ever been sworn. The boundary quarrel took up exactly where it had stood thirty years before — word for word, the mask said, the same words. The doctor, who had heard such returns before, and in smaller houses, wrote in his notebook that night: the same words. The town convened anew, decided it all again, and the clerk of that year began the new book with an entry about the fire.

Twice a year an examiner came down from the hills. He greeted the elders with great courtesy, asked nobody what the town had done, and walked to the fountain and read. Once the oldest man in town began reciting the toll from memory; the examiner listened to the end, then read the toll aloud from the book, and it was the book's version that stood.

The doctor tried to count the town's laws and found five, written on the book's first leaf. Four governed how the town talked to itself; only one could be touched with a hand. That one lived in the granary: a wooden peg driven into the wall fifteen measures below the rafters, and the grain might never rise above the peg. In the fattest harvest the merchants cursed the empty air above the grain; the mask of the works stood in the doorway and pointed at the peg until they took their sacks away.

When a matter was decided and entered, the drum beat twice and the gathering dissolved. On his last morning the doctor asked the midwife, out of old habit, what the town had decided about the weir. She had seconded it herself, standing on the third step. She looked at him kindly and pointed at the fountain.

# The Hollow [-]

Ona came down from the pass at dusk and asked at the first lit window where the valley's business was done. She was shown a hall cut into the hillside, its mouth glowing. Above the door a stretch of cliff had been whitewashed, and on it huge shadows swung — a lifted arm, a banner — thrown by the single lamp inside. A knot of townsfolk stood in the cold, watching the wall. "Is the meeting out here?" Ona asked. "The wall is," a girl said, and did not turn. Ona went past them into the mouth, to see what made the shapes.

On the floor, things were smaller than the wall had promised. Down one side lay a row of flat stones, each cut with an old mark, and on each stone stood one person, with others waiting behind to step up. That evening a woman stepped off her stone mid-argument and would not go back, and the stone said nothing more that night; the hall grumbled at her, not at the stone. Across the aisle, benches ran beneath ten stitched banners — netmenders, charcoal-burners, and the like — and when a banner spoke, it spoke once, whatever the number of throats beneath it.

At the head of the floor sat five figures in woven hoods: one who kept the strongbox, one who carried words to other valleys, one who tended the water and the bridge, one who held the tales of hard winters, and one in motley who was permitted to laugh at the other four. Behind each hood stood a second person, bare-faced, paid from the same purse, whose whole office was to prove the hood wrong, out loud. The strongbox hood said the town could pay for a ferry; the bare-faced one read out the count of coin and showed it could not; both were thanked, and the ferry died where it stood.

Between the stones and the benches stood a lectern turned to face the door, a book lying open on it so that anyone who entered could read the last page without asking leave. One old man wrote in it, with the only pen in the hall. A drover swore his name had been wrongly set down; the scribe did not scrape the page but wrote a fresh line beneath: the line above lies in this matter; the true name is thus. He wrote forward only, never backward, and every line ever set down was still in the book, struck or standing.

When the lamps were snuffed the hall emptied in minutes. Outside, Ona asked the drover what the winter sitting had decided about the ferry toll, for a groom swore the drover had stood the winter through. The drover shook his head. "My legs, maybe," he said. "What stood on the stones stays with the stones. Ask the book." At the mouth the doorkeeper was barring the doors; the sitting, he said, was whoever answered the bell, and when the bell went quiet the sitting was no one, and come spring the bell would call whoever came.

In the morning a carter stood before the man the valley sent for when two people swear differently, about a road he said the town had promised him. The carter brought neighbors. The hood of tales offered his own memory of the promise; he had stood in the hall the night it was made; it counted for nothing. The sent-for man read the book from the last page backward, found no line with the road in it, and closed the cover. "No line, no promise," he said. "The town speaks once, and it speaks there." Ona asked what would happen if fire took the book entire. "Then the hill would be a room where people once stood," he said. "Nothing was ever decided in it. There would be a new book, and a first line."

A browned leaf sat near the middle of the book. A lamp had fallen on it years back, across the lines of a ferry toll, and from that day the collectors at the water took no coin, though half the valley remembered paying; a fresh sitting had to be called by bell, and the toll written again on a new leaf before anyone paid again.

Before any sitting could open, a warden went down the stair beneath the hall to the cold bins and counted the empty ones aloud to the doorkeeper. That evening the count was fourteen. The doors stayed open. No stone was stood upon, no banner argued, the five hoods waited on their bench, and carters rolled casks out into the yard until the warden called sixteen. Then the doors closed, and the hall began. Ona asked the one in motley why. "Sixteen sits better than fourteen," he said, and would say no more.

Near the door, cut into the jamb, ran five lines of law, no more, worn dark by hands. A tanner moved that a sixth be cut beneath them, and though his own brother stood that night upon a stone, not one stone took up the motion, and the only line the scribe wrote was that it was refused.

Ona left at first light. The whitewashed wall stood gray and blank in the daylight, the townsfolk gone from it, and the shadows had been taller than the arms that made them. She had been inside, though. She had seen the lamp, and the floor, and the one pen.

# The Book in the Square

In Alderford the bell, not the calendar, decided when the town would think. When it rang, whoever wished to speak came to the hall, took a gray coat from the pegs, and dropped their name into a basket at the door. Inside they were nobody in particular. The long table held twelve places that belonged to families and were never rechosen; ten stools stood for the trades — the millers, the coopers, the ferrymen, and the rest; and facing them stood five chairs that filled only when the officers came hooded: the counter of coin, the greeter of strangers, the keeper of the works, the teller of what happened, and the mocker, whose trade was to laugh at the other four.

A speaker in a gray coat who said "last time we agreed" was made to sit down. Last time was in the book or it was nowhere. Newcomers read before they spoke. When the bell ended the meeting, the coats went back on their pegs, the names were lifted out of the basket, and the members went home as strangers, carrying nothing of it but the mud on their boots.

The book lay open on a stand under the hall's porch, where the rain could not reach it and anyone could read. One scribe held one pen. The pages were numbered and stitched into the spine, and no leaf had ever been cut out; before writing anything new, the scribe read the last line aloud to whoever stood there. When the flood-plan of the hooded keeper of the works was proved wrong — it drained the wrong meadow — the plan was not scraped away. A fresh page was sewn in beneath it, and the error stayed legible above the repair.

Upstream, the town at the ford had let its book burn in a bad spring. The bridge tolls, the mill grant, the boundary stones all turned to shouting. The elders swore oaths over what had been granted, the oaths weighed what oaths weigh, and within a year the bridge belonged to whoever could argue longest. Alderford took the lesson into its sayings: burn the pages and nothing was ever decided here.

Each autumn the examiner came from the county. He heard the richest miller swear to his figures and did not lift his eyes. He heard the hooded counter of coin declare the toll-box honest and did not lift his eyes. When the scribe turned the stitched book to the line and set a finger on the ink, the examiner made his mark. "I take no man's word in this town," he said, "not even yours," and the scribe, who had spoken twice that day, nodded, because it was so.

After the fire at the ford, Alderford tried a second pen for one winter, a twin book kept by a clerk in the granary so that flame could not take everything. By midsummer the two books disagreed over a toll, a nine where the other held a six, and the examiner refused them both, and that year nothing could be shown to have been decided. The second pen was retired to the mill. It was the one contrivance the town ever unmade, and it took only a season.

Behind each of the five chairs stood a breaker, paid from the toll-box. The counter's breaker recounted every figure before it was spoken; the teller's breaker followed the recital with a finger on the pages; the mocker's breaker hunted for where the joke flattered. In the lean year when someone proposed sending the breakers home with the fiddlers, the hooded keeper of the works had his breaker undo the new flood-plan before the hall, and the coin for the breakers was found.

Five laws were nailed to the doorpost. Four concerned tolls, the night watch, true weights, and market days. The fifth said that more than fifteen ells of the drying-rail in the loft — where the wet pages hung — must stay bare. The year the barley came in thick and a clerk stacked sacks to the rail's far end, the ink ran, the pages smudged, the examiner refused the autumn count, and the town lived a season unable to prove itself. After that the farmers stored their grain proudly in the street-side barn with the rats, and cursed the harvest, not the law. Nobody could say who had first written the number fifteen. Someone asked the scribe; he turned to an early page and pointed at a line, and that was the whole of the answer.

Because the meetings began empty, they read before speaking; because they read, the pages thickened; because the pages thickened, the rail was lengthened; because of the rail, the barley went to the rats. Each remedy arrived quietly and stayed as a need.

At dusk the bell ended it. The hoods came off in the entry — the counter of coin was the tanner's daughter; the mocker, the old ferryman — and the breakers left separately, as breakers do. On the porch a child copied the market rules onto a slate, and under the eaves the shutter was drawn over the book, the last thing in Alderford anyone thought to shelter from the rain.

# The Assembly of Empty Vessels

Wren came down from her valley to the river town to learn how it ruled itself, because her own people were tired of quarreling from memory. She arrived on the eve of the convening. The grain hall was being swept, and the great book already stood on its stand beneath the east window, where the light fell on it and on anyone who came to read.

The pen belonged to an old woman named Osla, and it was the only reserved thing in the town. It hung from her belt on a cord. The book hung from nothing; it lay open to whoever wanted it, farmer, child, stranger, at any hour. Wren watched Osla sew new pages in. She never cut one out. When her own hand slipped, the slip stayed standing and the mending went beneath it, and both were read after.

In the morning the members came up from their streets carrying nothing. At the threshold a man who had spoken gloriously at the last convening tried to resume. "You agreed with me," he said, "before the frost." The hall did not argue. It waited while a boy fetched the book and read aloud what had been done before the frost, and it was not what the man remembered, and he sat down like anyone. Wren saw that in that hall a fine memory bought a man no more than a loud coat.

The assembly met in two parts. In the low hall stood members who were not people: a grindstone, a pump, a bell, a balance, and others of their iron kin, each with its keeper. When a question reached them, the keeper read the needle aloud, and the reading was the member's voice; one keeper was caught sweetening his needle, and he went out and did not come back. In the high hall the readers sat on ten benches, one bench to a trade — the dyers, the coopers, the tanners, and the rest, ten in all — and each bench gave one voice, whatever number of heads sat on it.

At the low hall's door the measurer worked with a rod notched at fifteen. He measured the empty room in each machine's bin before it could take its place. That day a new engine waited in the rain, tall and quick, its bin stuffed with its own hoard, and its keeper argued strength and youth. The measurer said nothing; he laid the rod against the emptiness, and the grain stood above the notch, and the door stayed shut. The first page of the book carried five founding lines, cut the year the town was begun, and the measurer's rod was one of them. Wren stood beside the keeper while he opened the bin and poured his engine's hoard into the mud, a little at a time, measuring, until the emptiness passed the notch and the door opened.

The five came in masked: the Purse, the Far-voice, the Works, the Long Count, the Laugh. Whoever wore a mask was nobody and the office both. At the threshold the Laugh tipped his mask up to drink, and the hall's eyes slid off him as off a stranger at a fence, until the mask came down and the office stood where the man stood. Each of the five kept a second figure at the shoulder whose whole trade was spoiling the first. When the Purse said the town could pay for the bridge, her spoiler turned the money out on the table and counted it against her, coin by coin, aloud, and the hall trusted the count and not the Purse. When the Works swore the old timbers would hold, his spoiler loaded them with stones until they groaned, and the Works thanked him and thickened his plan. Wren asked the Long Count why he fed so keen an enemy. "He is the other half of my face," the mask said.

A boundary quarrel rose between two farmers, each swearing his father's memory. The hall asked only one question, of each in turn: where is it written? One farmer had a line; the other had thirty years of remembering; the line won, though its owner was a newcomer who had never walked the field.

It was the bridge that got the book burned. The men whom the line had beaten carried it off by night and burned it on the mill steps, meaning to be quit of one decision. In the morning the hall convened and could prove nothing: not the bridge, not the midsummer debts, not the boundaries, not the five founding lines. Every quarrel in the town returned to its starting stone, and elders and newcomers stood level, equally empty. "I remember the vote," said the Works. "Then bring the bridge," said Osla, opening a new book, "and let it be voted before the pen." What the town still wanted, it enacted again, thing by thing, in front of her cord and needle; nothing else returned.

The bell ended the day and the hall came apart like water finding its channels, the members going out carrying nothing, ten benches bare, the low hall dark. The day's decisions existed in one place only, sewn paper under the east window, and the town slept around it the way men sleep near a banked fire.

Wren copied what she needed for her valley and left. On the road she passed the new engine in its field, bin still open to the sky, its keeper sitting on the empty sacks, letting the rain stay where it fell.

# The Strangers' Field

North of the river there was a field where decisions were made, and the strange thing about it was that nobody there knew anybody. Twice a season the horn sounded from the pole at the center, and people came in from the roads — millers, drovers, a man with a yellow dog — and the moment they stepped past the boundary stones they were, by the oldest custom of the place, strangers. Two men who had cursed each other all winter met inside the stones as men who had never met. When the evening horn sounded, everything they had said to each other went out with the light. The field kept no memory, and was proud of it.

What the field did keep hung on a post in the middle: a book, chained open under a little tin roof so the rain could not write in it. One scribe, and only one, was permitted a pen inside the stones — an old woman with ink to her elbows who wrote down whatever was agreed, line under line, and sewed every fresh page into the spine in front of the crowd. Nothing was ever cut out. A great man once asked her to unwrite a line that shamed his house. She said: I can sew another line beneath it; I cannot make a line un-happened. He lived with the line.

Every quarrel ended the same way, at the post. Two drovers came arguing about who had promised what, and the scribe did not look at either face. She read the line aloud, and the taller drover went home. Faces were weather; the line was the field's only fact.

One autumn a spark from a pipe caught the dry feathering under the tin roof, and the book burned — white ash, a smell like burnt hair. Standing around the warm post, the people found that the bridge toll had never been set, the water shares never split, the pardons never given: whatever the field had ever agreed was ash now, which meant it had never been agreed at all. They stood a long while. Then the scribe cut a new pen. Some call that morning the field's shame. The beggar who lived at the edge of it, in a clay jar too small for company, calls it the most honest hour the field ever had.

The beggar carried a lamp and lit it at noon. Asked what she was hunting, she said: a face I could believe. She never found one — not because every face lied, but because the field had long ago stopped counting faces. It had even made her game into an office: an examiner walked each gathering with a staff, asked every ring what it had resolved, believed nothing she heard, and settled the day by reading the post. When the beggar watched the examiner turn from a sworn oath to a page of ink, she laughed until the jar rang.

The gathering stood in rings. The inner ring was tables, one member to a table, and a table spoke only with its own mouth. Around the tables stood the hired voices, sworn to argue whichever side they drew by lot, honest the way players are honest; they fed under ten banners, and each banner kept its own fire. At the pole, five officers in masks ran the day: the Counter, who touched the coin; the Far-Voice, who spoke for the strangers across the river; the Maker, who kept the bridges and the works; the Rememberer, the only one permitted to talk about the old days; and the Laughing One, who mocked the other four and lost his post the season he got gentle. Each mask was given, at public cost, one enemy — a sober person with a single trade: proving that mask wrong. When the Counter declared the granary held forty sacks, her enemy went and counted them; the claim stood only when the enemy failed to break it. The beggar said the enemies were the only people on the field paid to be honest, and she tipped her lamp to them.

The field owned five laws, cut into a millstone by the gate. Four were argued over and recut most seasons. The fifth never changed: the ice-house — the quick cold room where fish and deeds kept fresh — must hold at all times more than fifteen tubs of bare room; emptiness counted on purpose, fullness a fault. A law that measured what was not there. The beggar heard it read at every gathering, and every time she wiped one eye, because it was the most honest law she knew.

The last stitching the scribe made was done at the black end of a day, three breaths before midnight, by lamplight, the five masks in a row and their enemies standing behind them like shadows with pay. That same afternoon a fat merchant had given the field a hundred carts of gravel and demanded to be remembered for it; he shouted at every ring and paid the children to sing it. At the evening horn the members scattered down the roads as strangers, and his song died where the roads forked. What kept the gravel was one sewn line on the post — eleven words, no gratitude in it, no fat man in it. He came back next season and asked the strangers at the boundary stones whether they remembered him. They did not; that was the whole arrangement. The beggar called after him down the road: you could stop asking. The book already doesn't care, and it is the only thing here that keeps anything.

# The Town Between Two Bells

A traveler came to the town in the dry season, having heard that it governed itself without remembering anything. The congress met in the long hall above the market. When the morning bell rang, the summoned walked in — a dyer, a surveyor, two widows, a farrier — and became, for that day, the congress. They argued, weighed, agreed, refused. When the dusk bell rang they walked out, and not one of them could have said what had passed. The next morning the bell rang again, and the ones who came in were strangers to the day before, even in the same coats. Whatever the congress was, it lived only between the two bells.

The traveler expected disorder and found instead a lectern in the open market, under a glass hood, lit by a lamp at night. On it lay one book. An old woman with ink to the wrist was the only hand permitted to write in it, and she wrote while the congress spoke, line after line; when the pages filled she sewed in new ones, and nothing was ever cut away, washed, or struck through. She wrote in the open air, where any passerby could watch the lines appear, and boys copied passages to sell. Once a cobbler leaned over the rail and added, in his own hand, a claim about a debt owed to him. The wardens wiped it away before the ink dried and fined him — not for lying, but for writing. Anyone could read; only one hand could make a thing have happened.

One winter a spark took the hood and the book burned to the rim of its stand. The town did not mourn it; it discovered it. The bridge toll had been set in those pages, and with the pages gone no toll had ever been set, and the carts crossed free until a new line was written. A man who had been declared a thief in the burned leaves was let out of the stocks, since he had never been declared anything. The elders said aloud what everyone already knew: burn the pages and nothing was ever decided. The scribe was given a new book the next morning, and ten years of decisions were remade one line at a time, or not at all.

Each spring an examiner came from across the river. He asked no member what the congress had intended; intention had walked out at the dusk bell and gone home to forget. He asked the book. Two merchants disputed whether a rate had been fixed; the examiner went to the lectern, read the line aloud with its date, and the dispute ended — not because the book was honest, but because in this town honesty had no other address. Where no line existed, he ruled that nothing had occurred, and no memory, however vivid, could plead against the absence of ink.

Inside the hall the traveler counted three orders. Along the back wall stood the bronze delegates, iron figures that could not speak; when a question was put to them each dropped a white or black stone into a trough, and the stones were counted, and the count went to the scribe. In the middle were the ten choirs, each with its own manner — the one that sang of causes, the one that sang of costs, the one that sang of ruin — and they pulled the day's question to pieces. But only the five masks beside the door could put a question at all: the Mask of Coin, the Mask of Far Speech, the Mask of Works, the Mask of Memory, and the Mask of the Fool. When a mask was lowered over a face, that face could propose; and behind each masked speaker stood a second person whose whole office was to break the proposal — to prove it false, to find where it would drown. The Fool's breaker was said to be the gentlest and the most feared.

On the door of the hall five laws were nailed. Four concerned tolls, weights, and market hours. The fifth was the strange one: the cold cellar with the swift shelves, where the town's quickest goods lay ready to hand, had to hold more than fifteen barrels of emptiness, and each dusk a warden went down with a rod to measure the void. When the emptiness shrank to the mark, the morning bell stayed silent; the town spent the day clearing the cellar rather than deciding anything, for the congress could not convene over a full one.

On his last evening the traveler noticed the woman who sat against the square's wall, whom the children called the remembering-woman. She had attended every convening for forty years and carried all of it — every rate, every pardon, every face. She spoke freely to anyone who sat beside her. But no examiner had ever crossed from the lectern to her wall, and when she offered her memory at the hall the wardens let her talk only after the dusk bell, as amusement; her words entered no line and made nothing happen. She was the most complete archive in the town, and by the town's single rule nothing she had ever said had been said. The traveler bought a copy of the pages he had watched being written and left at first light, and by dusk the town had forgotten him.

# The Horn at the Wellhead

I passed a week in the town below the chalk hills, riding circuit for the parlement, and I set down here what I saw of their custom, as the margin of my notebook allowed.

A horn sounds from the wellhead at no fixed hour, whenever the warden judges there is matter. Whoever hears it may come; at the door of the long shed each comer draws a pebble from a jar, and the pebble gives him his bench. Enemies draw side by side; a stranger may draw between them. When the torches are quenched the sitting ends, and it ends utterly: the pebbles go back into the jar, the people leave by different gates, and no sitting is ever called back. A weaver named Jeannette once began, "as we resolved in the autumn—" and every head turned to her as to a woman describing a dream. What the autumn sitting resolved lay in one place only, and no one's head was that place.

Behind the shed wait the banners. Ten trades keep the town — the weavers, the smiths, the tanners, and seven more besides — and each comes in behind its painted cloth, speaking through whichever of its own hands it has chosen that morning. When the torches die, the cloths are folded, and a trade holds nothing over from one sitting to the next except what stands written.

Five go masked between sittings, and no one asks which neighbor wears a mask this year; a quarrel falls on the office, and the face under it is never invited in. One keeps the coin; one carries the town's words beyond the chalk; one tends the bridge and the waterworks; one reads the old sheets aloud and keeps the story of what was done; one goes in motley, whose office is to laugh at the other four. Behind each walks a lean man carrying a slate, the shadow. Whatever a mask intends, his shadow first tries to ruin it. One works-day the bridge-mask brought his plan to the wellhead, and his shadow called out the flaw he had hunted down in it — the timber count ran short by nine — and the plan went to the scribe mended. What no shadow can break goes in as spoken.

The scribe is Perrin, at an iron stand in the square, and on the stand lies the roll: sheets sewn end to end, longer each year than a man is tall. A cord ties the quill to the stand, long enough to reach every line of the day and too short to leave with. Whoever wins a grant or a right speaks it aloud to Perrin, and Perrin alone writes, and the square hears it twice. Anybody may turn the roll back — the tanners send a boy to read the year's lines at their door of a morning. A draper once, impatient of his turn, took the quill and set down his own grant in his own hand; ever after the town read that line as it read a stain, the readers' voices passing over it, and because his grant stood nowhere else, it did not stand at all. Nothing is ever cut from the roll; when the sheets fill, new ones are sewn on.

Master Gaultier, who had lost a water-right, came one night with a torch, and the watch took it from him gently and told him what fire would buy: not the water-right, but the end of his own mill-title, his father's grant, every judgment ever given him — all of it lived in the sewn sheets, and the sheets burned would leave a town in which nothing had ever been decided, himself a squatter on his own mill-floor. In a festival year a spark did catch the corner of the first sheet, and the town held everything from that scorched span as never having passed: two roads went back to being unowned, and the parties came before a fresh sitting and began again from silence.

Disputes are not settled by memory there, nor by rank. Old Arnaud swore before the whole square that he had heard the mill-grant given otherwise; nobody argued with him; the crowd walked to the stand, unrolled to the year, and read the line aloud, and the line was believed over the man, his oath weighing what a dream weighs. Asking after a road-title, I saw them descend: they stood the claim on the line that gave it, and that line on an earlier line, and so down the sewn years, link after link, until the chain either touched the first sheet or ended in air; and what ended in air was left lying.

Five lines are cut into the wellhead stone. The strangest governs the horn. Before any sitting the warden goes down to the vault where the blank paper lies and sets a marked rod against the clear floor, and unless better than fifteen measures stand open — the rod carries its mark, and fourteen and a half is nothing — the horn stays silent. One spring the town waited eight days in the square while flax was beaten and dried; no horn sounded, and nothing could be decided, because there was not room left to decide it in.

Of all this I saw the horn, the roll, the rod, and the descending chain with my own eyes. Gaultier and the draper I have from the innkeeper, and I give them as they were given to me. I have the custom by neighboring, not by possession; I could set down the whole proof of why they chose to forget, but this margin is too narrow to hold it, and I would rather leave the theorem whole and unproved than set down half of it.

# The Minutes

I came to the congress on a day when it did not exist. The hall stood open, the benches were dusted, and the doorkeeper told me the members had dissolved at dawn and would convene again at dusk; between the two bells, she said, there was no congress to visit. I asked where the members waited out the day. Nowhere, she said. When the evening bell rang they would arrive new, every one of them, and would learn who they had been by reading a single book.

The book lay open on a stand in the corridor where anyone could turn its pages, and it had one writer only, a scribe seated behind a cord. I watched him work. When a member misspoke a figure, the scribe did not wipe the page; he drew a single line through the words and set the correction beside, and the wrong words stayed legible underneath, so that the book was less a record of what was true than a record of everything, the true seated next to the struck. I asked whether the book could ever be made shorter. He looked at me the way one looks at a man who has asked to see yesterday.

The doorkeeper showed me a gap on the shelf between two fat volumes, wide enough for a third. Years ago, she said, a stove overturned and the seventh volume burned entire. I asked what that season's congress had decided. Nothing, she said. Not that the decisions were lost — nothing was ever decided. The session had no proof it had occurred, and so, for the house, it had not. Members who had stood in that room and carried it in memory were not permitted to remember it aloud.

I saw the rule enforced. Two members argued at the assessor's table over a vote to open the north road. One swore he had stood in the room and heard it pass. The assessor did not ask him to swear again; she found oaths weightless. She sent a boy to the corridor, the boy returned with the book open, and she read the page and said the road stays shut. But I was there, the member said. You are not evidence, she told him — no one in this house is; we are all too fresh to trust. Only the page is old enough.

At dusk the bell rang and the hall filled in order, an order painted on the outer wall where strangers read it before entering: who may speak first, who checks whom, who counts, who only listens. Nothing about precedence was kept behind doors. First came the tall elders of iron, who never explain themselves and answer only yes or no. Then the ten banners — the dyers, the carters, and the rest — one speaker to each, entitled to talk until breath ran out. Last, at the table's end, sat five masks: one who keeps the coin, one who speaks to far houses, one who keeps the works in repair, one who keeps what happened, and the Fool, who keeps nothing.

Each mask carried a shadow, a thin companion in gray whose whole office was ruin. When the keeper of the coin proposed lending out the north road's tolls, his gray one recited, without heat, three winters in which the tolls had failed, and the proposal died in the reciting. No one called it disloyalty. A mask without a ruin at its shoulder, the doorkeeper told me, is only a mask quoting itself.

Over the door five statutes were cut. Four were plain. The fifth was read each evening by a boy with a lantern at a gauge on the cold store: the free room within must stand above fifteen measures. One night the gauge showed fifteen exactly — fifteen and no spoon more — and the doors stayed shut, the elders went dark in their places, and the question set for that night was never asked again. It had no night to belong to. I waited for grief and found none; the house cannot mourn a session that never convened, and by morning no one could say what the question had been.

Late, on the last night, I saw the visitors in the corridor. They had stopped watching the hall. They ringed the open book, following the lines as the scribe laid them down, and the hall behind them could have emptied to its walls without their noticing. I understood then that I had never once seen the congress; I had only seen the book being made true. At dawn the members dissolved mid-sentence, as they always do, the scribe finished the line, and the book stayed open on its stand — the only place the congress is continuous.

# A Congress of Strangers

Each morning the town of Vey raised a pavilion of grey canvas in the arcade, and each dusk it took the pavilion down. The people beneath it were drawn by lot at dawn — a fishwife, a wheelwright, a boy too young to shave — and at dusk they walked out through the ropes and became private again. What they had said under the canvas did not follow them home. A woman who spent Tuesday arguing for the new weir drew a lot again on Wednesday and stood against it by noon, because nothing of Tuesday lived in her; she argued Wednesday's side with her whole chest, and the town called this ordinary.

For the town kept its mind in one place: the long wall at the north end of the arcade, cut line under line by one mason with one chisel. A grain merchant once paid the mason to smooth away a line that shamed him. The mason took the coin, cut a new line beneath the old one saying the old one was mistaken, and left the old one standing — so the merchant paid twice and was shamed three times: once in the first line, once in the second, and once for asking. Beggars earned their bread at the wall, reading it aloud to anyone who could not read. Travelers copied it for amusement, but a copy was paper, and paper had never governed anything in Vey.

Under the canvas, the town arranged itself by furniture and by masks. Along the east side stood the ancient benches, older than any family and owned by no one; they were counted as voices in their own right, and whoever rested on one that day merely lent it a throat — a bench, whatever else could be said of it, never said one thing on Tuesday and another on Wednesday. Along the west side stood the day-speakers of the ten trades — the dyers, the coopers, the tanners, and seven more — each trade with one clear voice for the day and nothing carried over.

Between the halves, on a low stage, five masks hung on five pegs: a coin, a far shore, a hammer, a closed book, and a grin. Whoever drew a mask wore it and spoke for it — the coin for the town's money, the far shore for strangers, the hammer for whatever must stand, the closed book for whatever had already been, the grin for whatever the others had forgotten to doubt. Beside each peg stood a breaker, paid by the town to ruin whatever the mask said. A mask's word counted for nothing until its breaker had swung at it and missed; only then did the mason's chisel get involved. The breakers were liked by nobody, which was the point of them, and they ate well.

Five laws were cut at the top of the wall, above every other line. Four were older than the arcade and concerned blood, bread, bridges, and strangers. The fifth, the newest, said that the cold room beneath the arcade — the fast, cold room where the town kept what must be reached quickly — must always hold more than fifteen pails of emptiness. A boy measured it at dusk with a marked pole. Once, after a fat harvest, the emptiness shrank to fifteen pails exactly, and men carried cheese out into the yard and left it in the rain until the pole called the air lawful again.

The small trades loved the wall best, and for the plainest reason: a great man's promise used to shrink as it traveled, and a cut line did not. Every eye in Vey went to the wall first and to a face second. Believing stone was cheaper than believing neighbors, and the town had paid out the difference too many times.

In the autumn a captain came up the river with forty spears and a paper sealed by a dead king. He stood at the edge of the pavilion and announced that the town was his. The strangers under the canvas looked at him, then past him at the wall, and waited, because in Vey nobody was anything until the wall said so, and the wall had not said so. The coin-mask offered him bread; the grin-mask offered to read his paper aloud for fun. He stayed a week and left with his spears.

One spring night lightning cracked the wall through three courses of stone. The town came out in the rain and stood with lamps along the crack until morning, because an old breaker, speaking to no one, said what everyone was thinking: a spear takes one life, but a wall coming down takes every yesterday a town owns, and there are men behind hedges who would pay to push. The wall held. In the days after, the mason cut the crack itself into the wall, so that the wall now carries an account of its own wound, where anyone may come and read it.

At dusk the masks went back on their pegs and the day-speakers went home to their trades. The strangers walked out through the ropes and forgot the day on purpose; forgetting was the town's oldest courtesy. The boy measured the cold room — fifteen pails and two fingers over — and the mason cut the last line by lamplight. Then the pavilion came down, and Vey slept well beside the one thing it owned that remembered anything at all.

# The Town That Forgets at Dusk

The stranger came down to Vell at the hour when the lamps were snuffed, and watched the whole council walk out of the hall and scatter like ash. No farewells; the day's speaker embraced no one. Each left by a different gate, and by the time the bell finished striking, none could have named the others.

At dawn they gathered again in the same hall — the same bodies, and not the same. They came up the steps blank as unwashed plates, and before anything could be argued, an old woman with a single pen led the youngest among them to the glass case where the town book lay open, and read the town back to itself: what it had decided, whom it had forgiven, what it had forbidden. Only then did their eyes catch light. Vell slept clean every night and was born old every morning, and the book was the only thing in town that grew.

The book had one hand in it. The old woman wrote, and no one else; when a councillor reached for the pen to add a note in his own cause, the others drew his arm down without anger, the way one moves a child back from a well. And the book never lost a page. A grain merchant judged a cheat waited until the hall was empty, licked his thumb, and scrubbed at his line. The ink would not lift. In the morning his scrubbing was a new line, in the one hand, and he walked the square for a year under that wet thumbprint like weather. What the town refused to carry, the book carried; and what the book carried could not be drowned.

Twice a year the auditors came down from the pass, grey twins who spoke to no host and accepted no bread. Men stood in their path offering testimony; the twins lifted their palms, went to the case, and read, and read nothing else. A woman swore before the whole square that her neighbor had moved the boundary stone, and the twins waited at the glass until the page turned up her father selling that ground. She had believed her oath completely. Believing completely is the mark of the thing one has not looked at.

The upper room of the hall belonged to things, not to people: a pump, a bell, a plough, a kiln, each standing where a person stands elsewhere, each asked its mind through a reader who laid a hand on it and gave its answer to the room. Below stretched the long hall of talk, where the town's speakers sat at ten long tables — the diggers at one, the weavers at one, the tanners, the smiths — and nothing was said to the whole hall until each table had first said it to itself.

Before both rooms, at every session, stood the five who covered their faces. The first counted; his fingers moved even in silence. The second had learned the far valleys' tongues and spoke for Vell to strangers. The third had raised the bridge. The fourth carried the town's story back to the town. The fifth wore bells and said the thing the room was walking around. Behind each stood a second figure, bare-faced, with a single duty: to break what the first had said — to bring the count that undid the count, the crack in the new bridge, the story the fourth had skipped. The town paid these breakers from the same purse as the five. When the stranger asked why a town would pay to be contradicted, the old woman only tapped the glass case.

Five laws were cut into the doorpost. Four changed with the seasons. The fifth never did: the cellar under the granary must hold no fewer than fifteen empty jars, counted at dusk. Not grain — emptiness was the law. A magistrate had once filled the last jar to feed a hard spring, and the town turned him out; not for the hunger he had answered, but for the fullness, because when the next wagon failed on the pass there was nowhere to put what little it carried.

In the stranger's last winter, a man the book had marked three times stacked brush against the case and burned it in the night, and the hall filled with the town's whole decided life going up. When the ash cooled, nothing was false, because nothing was. The forgiven were unforgiven; the judged were unjudged; the boundary sank back into the field, and two brothers met at the stone with the old dark in their faces. The burner had meant to erase one line, and had erased the line that kept his own son's name clear. He went through Vell begging someone, anyone, to remember. Everyone he begged had been born old every morning for years; they looked at him with blank kindness. The old woman sat down at the burned case with her single pen, waiting for a town to be decided again from the first word.

The stranger left on the thaw. On the road he passed a boy carrying a jar down from the granary — empty, held level, as if it were full.

# The Fifteenth Measure

In a town between two rivers, the hall bell was hauled on no fixed day. When it rang, the market was emptied by lot: whoever drew the marked beans climbed the steps — the tanner one week, a drover, a widow who sold eels — argued the town's business until dusk, and went home empty. Not empty-handed; empty. By supper the drover could not have told you what he had argued; by spring he would swear he had never climbed the steps at all. The town counted this no affliction. It kept one servant for whom nothing faded, a scribe who sat beneath the bell with a wide book across his knees, and whatever was decided under that roof went down under his hand, line after line, and no line ever came up again.

The book had enemies and admirers but only one hand. A draper, anxious to be seen deciding something, sent his clerk at noon to add a flattering sentence in the margin while the scribe ate. The whole town came out to look at the sentence as at a wound. The scribe could not lift it out — nothing is lifted out — he could only write beneath it, "this line is none of mine," and the two lines lie together still, and the draper sold his shop and moved to the coast. At nightfall the book was carried to an iron stand in the market and chained open, and the beggar read it first if the beggar arrived first.

Two neighbors once fell out over where the boundary ran. Both had sat in the hall the day it was walked, and both remembered it clearly, one of them falsely, and it made no difference: the assessor asked neither man anything. He had lived too long in that town to ask a body to be a page. He sent a boy to the stand, the boy read aloud, the boundary ran where the reading said, and the man who remembered falsely paid for the reading.

In the fever year a man who had lost the vote on the toll came by night and fed the book to his stove, and he got the whole of what he wanted. Through that winter the town had decided many things — a pardon, a boundary, the price of salt — and it had now decided none of them; whatever had lived only in the pages was never decided at all. The pardon dissolved like smoke. Nobody could even name his crime, since the only witness the town would hear was ash in his stove, and he walked free into a town that had quietly begun again from line one.

After the fever year the hall was set in order, and the order stood on three legs and five laws. In the high room the benches voted, each bench one voice, older than any sitter; whoever the beans set there in the morning spoke with that voice, and next day a different body sat in the same place and the bench said its say in a different mouth. A man once grew vain of his bench; he was reminded that it had said its say since before he was born and would go on saying it after. On the floor stood the ten troupes of speakers, each keeping to one fixed manner — one troupe told everything as a mourning, another as a sum — so that you never learned the mouth but always knew the music. Nearest the door hung the five masks: the Counter, who minded the purse; the Greeter, who met the strangers at the gate; the Keeper, who watched the works; the Teller, who carried the long story; and the Laughing One, who mocked the other four. Whoever took a mask down put on its wanting — the purse kept safe, the gate kept warm, the bridge kept standing, the story kept whole, the joke kept at everyone's expense — for the town had noticed that its five standing wants outlived every particular wanter. Each mask kept a shadow, and the shadow's whole office was ruin: when the Keeper pronounced the new bridge sound, the shadow loaded it with stone until the timbers cried, and only what could not be ruined was called the mask's word. The shadows were paid the same whether they broke the word or could not, and nobody in the town thought the money wasted.

The five laws were read from the first page before any talking, every ringing of the bell, for no head that climbed the steps had carried them up. One of them concerned the cold cistern under the hall, which was to hold better than fifteen measures of clear water at all times; the year the spring shrank, the town let the hall stand dark on fourteen rather than ring once against the law.

What the members kept of their service was the appetite only. They came down the steps unable to say what they had voted and certain the day had gone better for their being in it, and toward dusk you could find them at the iron stand, reading for their own names in the lines. The scribe, who had watched this hunger longer than anyone, gave each of them a name at least once — a crumb to every appetite — and the town called it kindness, which was also the oldest trick in town, and it worked daily. The book alone wanted nothing; everything that wanted ended by quoting it.
