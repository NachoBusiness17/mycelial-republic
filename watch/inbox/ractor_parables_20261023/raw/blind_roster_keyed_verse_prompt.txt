# The Held Note

The woman who wrote the song lived above the flood meadow, and she wrote it the year the river rose twice. She copied it out six times, and the six copies were exact: the same marks, the same words, the same dark line at the top. She did not choose the best voices in the valley. She chose six singers — in the flood town, the mill town, the salt town, the orchard town, the town under the cliff, and the town at the ford — and what she chose them for was that they would sing what was written and add nothing.

A courier carried the pages down the valley, one to each town, and the pages agreed with one another completely. In the second stanza and again in the fifth, the same six quick notes came down the same steps and landed on the same low tone. Every line ended in a word that chimed with another word farther down. In the middle of the song, one note carried a mark that meant: hold, to the end of the bell. And near the end there was a measured silence, after which — the mark said — each singer was to leave the common tone and take a note of her own, and no two were to take the same.

When the flood bell rang at noon, the wind took it up the valley, and the six singers began together, each in her own town. A traveler on the ridge that day heard one song coming from six directions. Through all the stanzas it stayed one song. Then the long note came — the same low tone the six steps had landed on — and it was one pitch everywhere, held while the bell's hum rose, leaned, and finally stood still. In the quiet after it, the six voices left the note one after another, each onto a different tone, the low town deepest, the orchard town highest, and from the ridge the one song came apart into six and went on as six.

On the woman's desk lay a book no larger than a hand. Beside the six quick notes she had written: six towns, one page. Beside the long note: what does not move. Beside the measured silence and the parting of the voices: the crossing, then the fields. Some entries sat beside pairs of chiming words instead of notes; beside the last two words of the song she had written: a song like this one.

The courier asked, once, whether the book should ride down with the pages, so that the towns could read what they were singing. She wrapped the six copies in oilcloth and told him the pages traveled light. He did not ask again, and the book never crossed her threshold.

The singer in the orchard town, who was young, copied her page against the rule and wrote a small guess in the margin beneath the long note. A clean page came back up the valley with the next post, and nothing else with it. She sang from the new page, kept the old one folded small in her sleeve, and sang nothing that was not written.

One winter the woman struck out the words beside the long note and set different words in their place. The six pages did not change. At noon the singers held the same pitch at the same hour as every noon before; the note was the note, what the book said beside it was new, and no one in the valley was told.

She died in the spring the water took the lowest field. The book passed to her granddaughter, who read it through once, sent nothing to anyone, and put it in the drawer with the spectacles. The singers sang at noon as they had been taught. The song kept its shape without her, exact in six towns, and nothing in it asked to be understood.

In the sixth year a traveler stood on the ridge at the appointed hour and heard the long note come from six directions at once, one pitch over the whole valley while the bell's hum stood. Afterward he came down into the flood town and asked the singer there what the note meant. She held it to the very end of the bell before she answered, and then she said: it is the same everywhere. That was all she had been given, and she gave it.

# The Book That Stayed Home

In the winter of the flood, Maren wrote one song and sent it out to six towns. She copied it six times in her own hand, and though the copies travelled by six different roads — the river road, the ridge road, the road under the pines — every page carried the same marks, down to the smallest comma of ink. Riders were stopped and searched at each boundary, in case the song was going out with anything tucked behind it. It never was. What each town received was the song, and only the song.

The song had a stubborn shape. Twice the melody climbed the same six tones, and twice it fell to the same low tone at the bottom, like a stone let down twice into one well; the two climbs even ended on the same rhyme, which everyone took for art. The mill town decided the six tones were six mules on one rope. The fishing town heard six oars in one boat. The hill town, having less water to think about, heard six brothers. Maren sent no note with the song and answered no letter about it, so each town kept the meaning it had made in the room where the song was first sung, and no town's meaning was any emptier for being homemade.

Near the middle came the bar no town argued about. Every line that had been wandering its own way came down onto one tone and held it, all voices together, six times over. When the choirs met at the spring fair and sang the song side by side, that was the only bar they did not rehearse; it arrived whole in every mouth. A choir that shook on it was asked to sing it again before anyone would trade with them. A choir that held it could change whatever else it pleased. The fair gave no prize for the best reading of the song. There was no such prize to give.

After the held tone, the song fell silent — one long rest, longer than seemed polite — and when the notes returned they scattered, climbing away from one another until no two were within reach.

The oldest people in the towns remembered what that winter had actually been. A single notice had to reach all six towns before the water did. It crossed the old arch at the ferry, where the ferrymen turned back any load that swung and passed only what hung steady on its frame, and on the far bank it was copied six times and given to the six roads. The last line of every copy — the line naming the hour the water would arrive — was forbidden to differ by so much as a breath. The song had been written the week after, and the towns had assumed it was about grief, or about the mules.

If Maren ever learned what the towns had made of her song, she left no sign of it. The drawer in her house held the winter's meanings and not one word about the mules.

Maren died in the third winter. A young woman came up from the capital to catalogue what the water had spared, and in the drawer under the north window she found a small book in Maren's hand that no rider had ever carried anywhere. It was not a diary. Beside each figure of the song it set a short plain note, the way a cook labels a jar; beside the long rest it said only, the arch. The young woman sat with the song and the book and read the winter back out of them, bar by bar. The two climbs and the two falls were the six roads and the one notice. The tone every voice held was the last line that could not differ. The scattering was the far bank, the copying, the many roads going their own ways. She could even tell which road had been sung first and which last. The song had been the record all along, complete without the book, and the book only said what its figures had stood for, for whoever came looking.

Before she left, the young woman copied the song six times in her own hand and sent it up to six new towns that were building on higher ground. The riders carried nothing else. The book went back into the drawer. That autumn the song was sung in six new rooms, and it became about six new things — a well, a wedding, a debt forgiven — none of which anyone had sent.

# The Song and the Slender Book

Maren came down from the pass in the dust season, when the singing house opened its six alcoves to the wall. She had left the town years before as a singer and had walked far enough to lose the habit of walls; the daylight had taken the edge from her sight, and she had paid it gladly, though it made her slow now in bright doorways, shading her eyes like a beggar.

The house was unchanged. Six alcoves faced a long stretch of pale plaster, and lamps behind brass grilles threw the song onto that wall in rows of small marks that climbed and settled, climbed and settled. Six singers sat with their backs to one another and sang the marks as they arrived. They never turned around. Maren asked the tall girl in the third alcove what the marks meant, and the girl laughed and said they meant today's song; she had sung them since she was seven, as her mother had, and it had never occurred to either of them to ask what the marks had been before they were thrown. When Maren asked whose wall it was, the girl said the house's, as if that settled it.

Behind the singing wall ran a corridor, and at its end hung a curtain stitched with the founder's motto: know the trial before you fight it. The town still told how the founder, in her youth, had been given the old trial of the house, the one no singer had ever passed, and had not passed it either. She had instead gone through its rules line by line, shown the judges every place it could not be won, and had the thing rewritten. The house had sung differently ever since. The tall girl told this story the way children tell of a flood.

Behind the curtain, the cantor kept a room with one table, one lamp, and a slender book bound in gray. The book never left the room. This was not guarded as a treasure is guarded; it was simply the arrangement, the way the river is under the bridge. No alcove had ever held it. No singer's hand had ever turned its pages. When the song went out at dusk, the book stayed, and the cantor followed the song with one finger, and where a figure came back on itself, the finger stopped and rested.

Maren asked to read the gray book. The cantor said no — not from cruelty; the book had never gone anywhere the song went, and its one rule was older than both of them. So Maren asked to sit and watch the finger instead. She sat a season of dusks, noted where the finger stopped, and said nothing.

The season's songs were plain. In the second line and again in the third, six marks climbed to the same height, and the six voices, fed from the same thrown shapes, sounded one above the other and came down together on a low round tone the oldest singers called the anchor. Each song was otherwise unlike every other — different in pace, in sadness, in length — but the anchor arrived the same every dusk, and all six struck it, and while they held it the marks on the wall stood still.

Near the end of each song the marks went dark for the length of a breath, an emptiness held on purpose, longer than any singer's habit, and when the voices returned they came scattered, flung wide, the high and the low at once, as though the song had gone through a narrow door and burst on the far side of it. The young singers loved that moment and threw their arms out when it came. They called it the burst. Not one of them asked why the silence had to come first, or what the scatter would become without it.

On the last evening the cantor closed the gray book and said, unasked, that the finger stops where the song speaks of this house — and would say no more. But Maren had watched a season of stops: the six marks at one height, the anchor struck by all, the held breath before the scatter, and the final page, which the finger touched last and longest, after the song was over.

The tall girl asked whether Maren had come back to sing. Maren said she had already been out; the daylight was hers, and it had shown her this room. She stayed by the lamp, between the wall and the book, facing neither, and did not leave.

# The Song Given to the Roads

Sela made one song in her life, and she made it the way good bread is made: for strangers to eat. At the crossroads in spring she handed it to six singers, one copy each, and told them to sing it in every bathhouse and tavern down their own road, to let it be copied, mis-sung, worn smooth by foreign mouths. The more hands it passes through, she said, the better it works. The six took their copies and went six ways, and each one, as porters do, mistook the load for a title.

Sewn into the cuff of her coat she kept a small grey card, crowded with her shorthand: half-lines of the song down one side, plain words down the other, written by one person for one person. That she gave to no one. Not to the singers, not to the landlord who fed her through a winter, not to the rich man who offered a house for it — she asked him whether he also bought ears at the market, since he clearly shopped for organs he lacked. The song went everywhere; the card went nowhere. That is the whole architecture of the trick, and it is not a trick, only patience: a thief in a port town once copied the entire song off a drunk singer, every note and every rhyme, and walked away owning a pretty noise he could not spend.

When the six came back in autumn they met at the same crossroads, and their copies carried the same furniture. In the second line, and again in the fifth, six notes climbed in the same order twice — identically, twice — and both times the phrase walked down and landed on one low tone and stopped dead on it. A child who had trailed the singers asked why the same walk ends in the same place twice. The proudest of the six said it was grief for the drowned, and invented a drowning, with names in it. Sela, on the fence, said nothing. She set six pebbles in a row on the flat stone, then laid one hand over all of them at once. That was her entire commentary.

Then she asked them to sing the low line together, the line where one tone is struck six times — the same tone, six times, every voice on it at once. The youngest, clever in the throat, curled the last strike into a little ornament, the way clever throats do when plainness frightens them. Sela flattened her hand in the air, and he sang it plain. A tone that every mouth holds together belongs to no mouth; make it pretty on one road and plain on another, and the song becomes a liar with six heads.

Up the hill stands a mill with a habit: its door rises only for a level tone — a note held steady, neither climbing nor sagging, unwavering as long as it takes to walk through. Five singers threw their trembles at it and it stayed shut. The sixth held one tone flat and still, like water that has stopped pretending to be a river, and the door rose. And in the song itself, just after that figure, comes the part the child loved: a silence exactly one breath long, then notes flung wide — high and low at once, no two near each other. Sela kept her palm flat over the pebbles through the silence, then swept all six off the stone in a single splash. After a door that admits only what stands still, everything scatters.

A man came through at noon carrying a lit lantern and asked each singer, in full daylight, what the song meant. He received six confident answers, all different, and nodded as one nods at six counterfeit coins from six proud mints. Porters, he said, bragging of porcelain they have agreed to call bread. The song only ever asked you for your feet.

Her last winter, Sela took an apprentice and never told her a single meaning. Learn the shapes, she said; the shapes keep their word. By spring the girl could hear a strange song once and name the road it had walked. They say the last entry on the card is the song itself — a rhyme about a rhyme, the ballad that carries its own grammar in its bones. Where the card went after, no one can say. It was never seen again, which is exactly what never giving a thing away looks like from the outside.

And you, the one holding the cuff — mark what you are still asking the world for. You give the song to every road all day, then stand at the threshold of every ear, waiting to be admired as its only reader. You could stop asking today. The card is not your title; it is your chore. Give the song away, keep the ear, and go home.

# The Song and the Sheet That Stayed

In the year the river took the low bridge, the old master of the singing house set down a verse of nine stanzas, and he would not let anyone call it old. At the head of every copy the ink-boy was ordered to write *made, not found*, and when a copyist once set *ancient air of the valleys* in that place, the master made him scrape the page and begin again. A thing might be sung falsely, he said, but not dated falsely.

He boasted of the verse in the margin of the fourth volume of the house book, in a hand smaller than the print: that it carried its whole meaning inside its own figures, and that the margin was too narrow to hold the proof. Then he folded a single sheet into that margin, and the sheet was the proof, and it did not leave the house again while he lived.

Six copies went out on one morning, one to each of the singing houses in the six valleys, carried by six riders who had all drunk from the same jug before leaving. Each rider carried the verse and nothing more — the notes, the words, nothing written under or between. Before they left, the east gate was tried against them. That gate lifted for no key a man could carry; it rose only for a knock given in step, six fists, one beat, and it had been known to stand shut all day against a ragged hundred. The six knocked as one, the gate rose, and on the far side the riders broke their line and spread over the hills, each to his own valley, and did not ride together again.

In the autumn the third valley wrote back: we sing it wrongly, send the meaning. The master sent another copy of the bare verse and nothing else, and when the valley begged a second time he sent the verse a third time, and the sheet stayed folded where it was.

I saw it once. He showed it to me for the length of one poured cup and no longer. The line-ends that chimed were paired there, and against each pair stood a few words in his small hand. Where the verse set the same six notes in two lines, one under the other, and both lines settled on the ground tone, the words said: the six, one morning, one letter. Where every voice was made to sound that ground tone at once — all the singers on the single pitch, neither more nor less — the words said: what holds. Where the singers fell quiet for the length of a heartbeat and then flung the notes wide, one to each string, the words said: after the door, so, and never before it.

On the feast of the frost he bade the six houses sing the verse back to him on one morning and write him their final tones. Five ended on the ground tone together. The sixth ended a step above it. He did not blame the valley. He went down instead: from the singing house to the copy on its stand, from the copy to the rider who had carried it, from the rider to the ferry, where the page had taken damp and a hurried crossing-keeper had re-inked one note higher, to better it. Step to the step before, all the way back to the one morning. The bare verse went out again; the sheet did not; and the next winter all six houses ended together.

A composer in the north town wrote that no such sheet existed and that the verse meant whatever a singer pleased. The master answered: take my verse, change one note in your house, sing it back, and I will name the note you changed. Letters crossed; he named the note from the endings alone; the north went quiet about meanings after that.

The master died in the spring. The fourth volume keeps the sheet, and I have not held it since; I can stand next to that meaning, but I do not own it. The verse still rides out every year, still bare, six copies on one morning, and the houses still end together on the ground tone. Anyone who wants the gloss must come with clean hands to the book, because in all these years the meaning has never once traveled with the song. And this is the strangeness of it: sung whole and read beside the sheet, the song tells the story of its own journey, figure by figure, and yet no rule is ever spoken aloud in it.

# The Bell-Note

The collector came up the river in the wet month with a commission from a city society: bring back the meaning of the valley's flood-song. He had read fair copies of the words and found them nothing — a miller, a sparrow, grey water, a lantern — the sort of thing a washerwoman sings without noticing that she knows it. He booked a week at the inn.

The town was rehearsing. At the fountain stood six couriers, and the widow Iva gave them the song by mouth, the same mouthfuls to each, no one permitted to bend so much as a syllable. The collector offered his services and was set to copying; he made six pages, no two differing by a hair, and she approved them the way one approves weather. Checking his pages against his ear that night, he found the second stanza was the first stanza again — six notes, the same six, in the same order — and that both stanzas came to rest, at their close, on the note the flood-bell gave. He asked a courier why the thing was said twice. "The water goes out all the spillways at once," the courier said, and sang on.

Before the six left, they did a thing no transcript had mentioned. They sang no words at all — only the bell-note, one sound out of six throats, held until it stopped being six sounds. The sexton told the collector the custom: if the six could not make that single tone, the song stayed home that year. There had been a fever year. The song had stayed home, and the flood had not waited, but that was the custom, and the town had kept it anyway.

All the roads out of the valley passed through one stone arch in the neck of the gorge, so narrow that the couriers went through it single file, and the custom was silence inside it — the song was carried through the Eye, never sung in it. On the far side the roads forked and spread toward six horizons, wider and wider apart. The collector walked out to watch them go and heard, from the last house, a child clapping the song on a fence rail: a long held stillness in the hands, then strokes flung wide, low to high, farther apart each time. In his notebook he wrote: the song and the road are the same drawing.

He fared worse with the paper. The town spoke of a sheet folded small that Iva kept under the tea tin, ruled in her own hand, where the song's rhymed words sat in facing pairs with other words beside them — flood against blood, water against daughter — and what the pairs meant, only she read at her lamp. She showed it freely and lent it never. In her girlhood a flood had put her on a raft to the next valley, and the sheet had stayed behind on the highest shelf in a tin box while she went without it. "The roads take the song," she said. "This stays where the lamp is." A young courier had once changed lantern to candle, for an easier breath, and she had made him learn the whole stanza new; the rhyme had tied two stanzas together, and loosening one had loosened both.

His interviews went badly, and delightfully. The miller was the frost, said one farmer; the tax man, said his neighbor; both were content, and neither sang better or worse for it. What the town had was not meaning but shape. The couriers walked in the song's measure at market; they fell quiet together in doorways; in flood years the whole valley moved the way the song moved — hushed at the arch, scattered after, everything filed through the one gap and then wide. A boy who could not manage words at all tapped the rhythm true on a pail and was taken on as an understudy.

On his last evening the six sang it end to end, and he heard the final stanza plain for the first time. It told of six singers who leave a valley through a narrow stone mouth in silence and scatter wide on the far side, all carrying one song, and of the one who stays behind at the lamp. The song had been carrying a description of its own carrying, and he had copied it out twice without seeing. Iva was not watching the singers. She was watching the road, to see that the shape of the thing held.

He went down the river at dawn, still humming, and could not stop. At the second bend the ferryman caught it.

# The Six Copies

Before the fair, the song-maker wrote one song for six valleys. She copied it six times in her own hand and laid the sheets side by side on the long table, and they were so alike that the messengers could not tell which was which. Each sheet carried the same words and the same small marks above the words, showing which tone to hold and how long. With each copy she sent nothing else — no letter, no word of meaning. The last messenger waited at her door and asked whether there was not more to carry. She said the sheet was the whole errand, and he went up his road.

In the song, two lines were twins. Each climbed the same six steps of the scale and each came down on one plain tone in the middle, the sound a door makes when it settles into its frame. A singer might dress the rest of the song as her valley pleased — flatten this phrase, sharpen that one — but the twins were not hers. Every sheet in every valley carried them exactly, and both landed on the same settled tone, so that whoever heard the song twice heard the same arrival twice.

At midsummer the six singers came down to the fair and sang it together beneath the old arch. Where the twins ended, they held the settled tone, six voices on one sound, and held it. If a single singer leaned, the hum in the stones shivered like water and the song went no further; the arch gave them nothing until the tone stood still. So they held it, breath after breath, and when the hum at last stood — not rising, not falling, only standing — the arch let them through.

Then came one full measure in which nobody sang. The six stood in the quiet with their mouths shut, and when the quiet was spent they broke apart across the scale: one voice climbing high, one sinking low, the others threading between, note set far from note, six journeys widening out of the tone they had held. Whatever a valley changed elsewhere, it kept the quiet before the widening, because that was where the widening began.

In the song-maker's drawer lay a card folded once, in her own hand. On it she had written what each figure was for: at the top, what the whole song itself was — a lesson that could be learned only by being sung; beside the twins, what they carried; beside the tone six voices held, what it proved; beside the standing hum, what it opened; beside the quiet before the widening, what followed it; beside every pair of rhyming line-endings, the thought the pair had been chosen to hold. The card had never gone up any of the six roads.

Men came for the card the way they came for grain. A buyer from the city set a full purse on the table and said he wanted to be the one who knew. She sold him a copy of the song instead, and he found it bare: lovely, singable, and silent about itself, a song that belonged to anyone who learned it and told nothing to anyone. Some years the singers asked her to explain the twins, and she sent them fresh copies. One valley tried to better the twins so that its version would be the true one, and the other valleys did not follow; they kept the six steps and the settled tone as these had come.

She grew old, and the song traveled farther than her name. Every valley changed it somewhere — a word here, a bend there — but the twins held, and the held tone held, and the quiet before the widening held, so that a stranger who heard all six valleys in one summer could mark what never changed and guess that the unchanged parts were the errand. What the errand was, the stranger could not say, and the valleys could not say either, though they sang it.

When she died, they found the card in the drawer, folded once, unsent. It was read aloud in the square at the fair, standing where the arch could hear, and the six valleys learned at last what they had been carrying all those years. At the bottom of the card, in small letters, she had written: sent up no road. They sang the song that evening exactly as before, and afterward the messengers' children asked for copies, and were given copies, and nothing else, as before.

# The Sixfold Song

The cove kept its harbor behind a door of rock, and the door opened for no key. It opened at a certain hour for a song, and the song was sung from six towers standing along the cliff, each tower within earshot of the next, all of them within earshot of the water.

Every morning the keeper, an old man named Ivor, copied the day's song from his slate onto six cards. He copied the words first, then the notes above them, and he copied each card from the slate itself and never from another card, so that no slip could travel from tower to tower. A young singer named Corin, who had the finest voice in the cove, asked leave to add a small turn at the close of the second line — only a turn, the kind that made the fish-wives look up. Ivor struck it out and handed back the plain note. "Sing to the gulls if you want to be wept at," he said. "The door is not an audience."

The six cards went out at dawn, one to each tower, carried by six runners who never swapped. The singers sang at the same hour, and the song had a shape everyone knew by heart and almost no one knew the meaning of. Twice in the middle it walked the same six pitches, step by step, and both walks came down together on one low note. When the six towers reached that close as one — one note from six throats, no voice early, none late — the sound did not pass along the water; it stood on it, leaning on itself the way a wave in a tub leans on its own reflection and climbs. That standing tone was the only thing the rock listened to. It swung.

Then a silence, exactly as long as one held breath. Then the bells, released, scattered — each tower taking its own line up and out and away from the shared note — and the ships came through and fanned across the open water toward every point of the compass.

The rule the children learned first, because they could sing it before they could say it: the door answers six as one. Corin proved the other half one windless evening by singing alone from his tower, better than the six had ever sung it, and the door sat in the cliff like any other stone.

In Ivor's room above the fish-hall lay a thin book, and the book never left the room. No runner carried it; no tower held it overnight; no copy of it had ever been made to travel. When a singer named Sela begged to know what she was singing for, Ivor read to her from the book but gave her nothing to take. Where the line walks its six steps twice, he read, that is the six of you, each given the very same; where you come down together on the low note, that is the thing that must not change; where you fall silent and then scatter, that is the door and what comes after it; and the song itself, learned whole and sung true, is the lesson that teaches its own singing. Sela climbed back to her tower with the words in her ears and nothing in her hands, and by the top step some of the words had gone soft, which was also part of the design. The towers held the song. Only the room held the meaning.

In the autumn a man came to the cove with a wagon painted gold and a voice like warm oil. He said the old song was grey and the cove deserved better, and he taught a new close in the square by candlelight, free to anyone. He kept the sixfold walk — he was careful to keep that — but where the six notes should have come down together he set a triumphant rise, his own invention, easier to cheer. Believing the new close, people felt the door owed them an opening, and the feeling was so pleasant that they sang it to their children. The man in the gold coat put a rope around his candlelit square and began charging at the rope.

On the fog-day, with the fleet outside and the fish running, the square sang the gold close with real love. Three towers, taught by their mothers, sang it too. Three sang the old card. Over the water the two closes beat against each other and died, and the rock did not move, and the man at the rope kept the coins. The people had paid for the feeling of the door opening, which he could supply nightly, and not for the door, which he could not.

The cove went back to the cards after that, and the fog lifted on its own terms. Corin still sang his turn at the close, but only to the gulls, on the far side of the cliff where the water could not hear him. And when visitors asked Ivor why the rock trusted six ordinary voices and not the finest one in the cove, he answered as he understood it: the rock has no ear for quality. It opens for nothing but agreement.

# The Cellar Book

Every evening the six singers took up six sheets in six rooms along the cold corridor and sang the same song from the first bar to the last. The sheets were copied each month by one hand, and no singer ever held a paper different from any other. The old master had written the song before he died, and the house had sung it since, in the same order, at the same hour, without one variation the walls had ever heard.

Twice in the song came the figure the singers called the flocking: six notes of one height, set in two lines that ran side by side, both lines settling at last onto a single tone. That tone was the one thing in the house no one softened. A singer might hurry a phrase or weep through a rising passage — the house forgave all of it. But once, on a night of frost, the newest singer let the tone lean, only a finger's width, and the inner door between the corridor and the master's hall sat dead in its frame, and no lamp in the passage would light, and nothing in the house answered until he sang the six notes clean again and held the last of them, with every voice upon it, until the air in the doorway stopped trembling. Then the door swung of itself, as it always did, and the song went on.

Past that door the song fell silent for one beat — the held breath, the singers called it — and on the far side of the silence the chord broke open wide, each voice taking a different distant note, the six of them climbing away from one another like seed thrown from a single hand.

No singer could say what any of it meant. Asked, they laughed the way workers laugh at a question about a doorknob's weight. It opens the door. It is sung. The master wrote it so.

Below the singing rooms, down a stair most of the house had forgotten, lived the keeper, the one who copied the sheets. He had been the master's copyist while the master breathed, and he had kept one command past the grave. The master had written two things in his life. The song went up to the rooms. The other, a thin brown book, went nowhere; on its first page, in the master's own hand, stood the words: to no room. For thirty years the keeper copied the song six times a month, carried the brown book nowhere, and the house above him sang.

A woman came in the autumn to tune the old clavichord. She worked with the corridor doors open, and by the third evening she had stopped working to listen, because the shapes came round too deliberately to be decoration. The six-of-a-kind, twice. The single tone that stilled the air of a doorway. The beat of silence, and the wide breaking chord. She asked the newest singer what the flocking meant. He said he had never asked. She asked the keeper. He looked at her the way a man looks at a stair he has decided not to climb; then, because she did not look away, he took her down.

The brown book set each rhyme of the song against a meaning, in the master's cramped hand. Six of a kind, in two rows: the six rooms running one errand. The tone that must not bend: the thing that stays true in every mouth. The held breath before the scattering: the doorway, passed. And on the last page, against the song's closing round, a line she read three times: a telling that keeps its own rules folded inside it, unspoken.

She had gone down expecting a dead secret; she found the house's other half, awake and keeping time. The song had ruled the singers all the more surely for being nameless, and the buried book had loosened its grip on no one. She had watched men serve a dead father's voice before; the voice grows louder the longer the father is in the ground.

She carried one page as far as the top of the stair, and the keeper did not stop her. In the corridor she read it to the newest singer, who stood with his sheet in his hand. When he understood, he asked her to leave the passage. That evening the six rooms sang exactly as before — the six notes of one height, the tone held until the doorway stilled, the silence, the wide chord — and the newest singer varied nothing, in the voice of a man who has looked at something and closed the door on it slowly, from the inside.

The brown book stayed below, for the dead man's word still governed the paper. But that winter the keeper took a pen and added a second line beneath the first, in his own slower hand: — but not to no one.

# The Drawer That Sang Nothing

Each autumn the examining house issued one song to six stands. The stands stood in a row along the wall of the practice hall, each with its own lamp, each facing its own corner, and every morning the registrar laid a fresh sheet on every one of them — six sheets, struck from a single plate, so that no singer could claim a different page. On the sheets was a verse: rhymed lines with a melody stitched above them, and the singers were instructed to sing what was written and nothing else. What the verse meant was not a question the house put to them, and they had long ago learned not to put it to the house.

The meaning lived in another room, in the registrar's drawer, on one slip of paper ruled into two columns. Down the left column went the figures of the song — each rhyme, each turn of the melody — and down the right, the thing each figure stood for. The drawer was locked. The slip was never copied, never posted, never carried out to any stand. A young singer once asked to see it, and the registrar told her, not unkindly, that the house does not explain its examinations to the examined, and that a proof explained is a proof spoiled.

Yet the house examined without pause. Two readers sat behind the stands with slates, marking every deviation. Each night the night-clerk collected the slates; the door-warden countersigned the clerk's tally; and twice a year the warden's own log was gone through by the registrar, in a room whose door the singers could see from their stands. The line of gazes closed into a ring, and no one inside it stood beyond being looked at.

The verse itself was plain about one thing only. Two of its lines were built alike: in each, the same six pitches, one after another, stepped down and came to rest on a single G — and that G was taken by all six voices at once, together, on a sign. The readers listened for it the way a magistrate listens for the name in a will. If the six did not land as one — one early, one flat, one holding past the others — the morning was void, and nothing sung before or after it counted as anything. That unison was the house's one article of faith, and the singers held it as tenants hold a wall.

After the shared G came a silence, a full measure in which no one breathed on purpose, and then a wide chord: the six flung apart, high and low, each taking its own interval for the first time in the song. Here, and only here, difference was no fault. The slates stayed quiet through the scatter; the readers had been taught that the silence was a threshold, that past the threshold the six were meant to part, and that a parting attempted before the silence was the same as no parting at all.

One winter an outside ear came to visit — an examiner from a distant house, with leave to ask the forbidden questions. She asked the registrar what the verse meant, and the registrar, to everyone's surprise including her own, unlocked the drawer. Beside the two built-alike lines the slip said: the six stands are fed from one plate. Beside the sixfold G: this must hold, or nothing after it is evidence. Beside the silence before the wide chord: past this they may differ. The outside ear read the columns twice and understood that the song was not a song. It was a drawing of the room — the stands, the plate, the threshold, the scatter — folded into notes and rhymes so that it could travel as music and be denied as a drawing.

She saw, too, how the room was cut. The singers had the verse and could not read it; the registrar had the reading and could not sing it. Whoever held the slip named what the unison meant; whoever stood at a stand supplied only the sound that proved it held. And when the outside ear asked what became of singers who read the slip, the registrar brought out the file. Years back, one had copied two lines of the right-hand column onto his cuff and sung his knowledge into the G. It landed perfectly — and perfectly wrong — and he was sent to a house that keeps no drawer.

The drawer was locked again. On her way out, the outside ear passed the six stands, where a new verse was being struck from a new plate: the same shared G, and, in a place of honor, a longer silence before a wider scatter. Behind the stands the slates filled; behind the slates the clerk; behind the clerk the warden; behind the warden the registrar, whose own drawer the record shows was audited that same season by the examining house — the same one, the file says, that had sent the outside ear to ask.

# What the Valley Repeated

Every year, on the first cold evening, the messenger went down the river with six folded sheets in his coat, and at each of the six villages he left one. The sheets were copied by the schoolmaster and checked line against line, so that no singer held a single note more than any other. The singers never met: a ferryman, a midwife, a goatherd, a carter, a weaver, a schoolboy. At dusk each stood at the edge of a village and sang what was written, from the first mark to the last, changing nothing.

I had come up the river for my own rest and stayed for the valley's. My trade is listening; the carter could not sleep, and he slept better in the third week of my stay, which he attributed to my draughts and I did not. It was through him that I borrowed a sheet.

Two lines near the middle of it wore the same six notes, note for note, like twins walking, and both lines stepped down at the end onto one plain tone, neither high nor low, the color of fog. Near the close there was a small square of silence, drawn like a held breath, and after it the notes flung wide, from the cellar to the roof of the voice, a dozen sounds in one throw, spreading the way seed spreads. The words rhymed in pairs, always in pairs, and turned year upon year on the same few sounds. I asked the weaver, who had sung longer than anyone, what the fog tone meant. She said it meant it was evening. I asked what the twin six notes meant. She said they meant the singing was nearly over. I asked why the wide throw came after a silence, and she looked at me as one looks at a man who has asked why rain follows cloud.

On a still evening I rowed the length of the river and heard all six villages arrive at the fog tone in the same moment — each in its own country voice, and the tone itself the same in every mouth, the same, the old men said, as long as there had been sheets. Long ago a clever carter had curled the tone into something finer. No one reproached him. The next year his sheet came back with the tone plain and a small cross beside it, and he sang it plain the rest of his life; he told me the note had simply come back to him, the way a name comes back to you at a door.

I noticed that the tone did not stay inside the singing. The midwife hummed it over her kettle without knowing that she hummed. The ferryman's oar knocked it out against the current. Children who could not yet read clapped it in the road — four small claps and the fog note, four small claps and the fog note. Whatever the sheets were carrying, the valley could not stop producing it, though nobody could say what it was for.

At the head of the river lived the widow of the man who had written the sheets. In her drawer lay a thin grey book tied with a shoelace, and no messenger had ever carried it. When the elder of the third village asked her to send it down the water so that everyone might know what the songs meant, she put her hand flat upon it and said that the post was for the singing, the book was for her, and it had never gone out and would not begin with him. He went home offended, and on the next first-cold evening his village sang as loudly as any. Once a year, on the morning after the singing, the widow took the book down, read the sheet against it with her finger — and her finger, the messenger told me over wine, rested twice as long on the rhymes as on the notes — then made one line of ink in the margin of her chronicle and tied the lace again. He had carried sheets for twenty years and had never seen inside it, and had stopped wanting to.

The words of the sheets, each year, told some small story: a sower who waited for a wind before he threw; a mill-door that opened only between two bells; six sisters setting the same table in six houses. The farmers did not know they had learned anything. But they had taken, within living memory, to waiting for the wind before sowing, and to passing the mill only at the turn of the bell, and no one had taught them by word. It is done, they said, and shrugged.

The widow died in the spring. The valley feared the songs would die with her, but she had left the book to her granddaughter, with instructions the girl would repeat only in part: send the sheets, never the book; read the morning after; one line, no more. On the first cold evening the six villages sang, the fog tone held in six mouths at once, and up at the head of the river a lamp burned past midnight and a pen moved for the length of one line.

# The Verse on Six Roads

Alder wrote songs the way other men of the river country wrote contracts, because paper mildewed in the wet seasons and tunes did not. Each year at the same tide he sealed a new verse, one copy for each of the six barges, and the six left together up the six canals toward the six cities where his words would be sung before the ice. The song went out. Nothing else ever went with it.

The verse of his last year was short. Its first two lines climbed the same six notes, step for step, note against note, and settled together on a low G. Then all six singers took that one G at once and held it as long as breath allowed. After it the page kept silence for one full measure, and when sound came back it came scattered, one note then another across the whole width of the voice, like seed thrown wide. The end-words of the third line and the fifth chimed, and no others did. The cities found it plain and sang it all winter.

What Alder never sent was the page in the drawer of his desk. The key to that drawer hung on a cord against his skin and went nowhere that he did not. The page was written small and gave each figure its burden: the six notes walked twice and landing as one meant the six hulls and their one cargo, the same words on every deck; the G held sixfold meant the thing that must not differ from itself, whatever else went under; the measure of silence meant the lock at the head of the canals, where the six waters narrowed to one and every hull waited, and where the gates opened only when the water in the chamber stood flat, neither rising nor rocking; the scatter that followed meant the letting-go, the fan of wakes on the far side; the chiming pair meant the two winters a verse stayed good, and no more. The last line of the page was about the verse itself: this song is the shape of its own reading. He never copied the page, never spoke a word of it, and refused flatly the one collector who offered a horse for it. "Six hulls, one song," he told the man. "A page would be a seventh."

He died in spring. The cord and the drawer passed to his niece Iva, who had tuned his instruments all her life and asked him nothing.

By then the verse no longer needed him. The singing houses of the delta had improved it — a brighter turn at the close, an extra echo in the second line — and the improved kind traveled back up the canals, so that the six cities now learned the song from the delta's copies rather than from Alder's barges. The annotators of the delta printed a commentary that gave every figure its meaning: the sixfold tone was the memory of a drowned king, the silence was his tomb, the scatter was his inheritance. The book was detailed, and the detail gave it credit. Pilgrims came on the first night of ice to hear the six choirs of the delta take the low G together, six processions converging by water, and if you asked any of them who had first said to gather, no one could tell you; it had simply always been the custom, from three winters ago.

Iva went down once, with the cord under her coat. In the booksellers' row a man offered her the original, meaning the delta edition, finely stitched and twice the length. From the back of the hall she listened: the two lines still climbed their six steps, note against note; the G still fell sixfold; the silence still held before the scatter; the third and fifth still chimed and nothing else did. Everything the page in her drawer said was still true of what six cities sang, and none of them had ever seen the page. She bought the commentary for a small coin, rode home, opened the drawer, and added one line below the last: the copies keep the shape by themselves now. Then she locked it, wore the key, and sent nothing.
