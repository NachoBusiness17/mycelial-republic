# The Dry Niche Under the Last Arch

Alda kept the tray by the quay-house door. Any neighbor might drop a folded paper in it — a chore that wanted doing again and again at set hours: the bell rung at six, the clock wound at nine, the steps salted before the first frost. Beside the tray sat the tally-man. Each paper that arrived got a chalk mark on his board, and the board was meant to turn from white marks to red ones as the chores were carried off and done.

The chores were not done in town. They were done in a squat tower at the end of a long causeway, and the causeway was on no map of the coast, for it showed itself only to those whose names had been read onto the roll. Inside the tower, they said, was a workshop the size of a cupboard, and in the workshop a keeper whose single lamp had a cracked pane, fogged and veined, so that nothing showed through it either way. The keeper worked by touch and by the town bells. The little works went round inside whether or not the glass ever cleared.

When Alda walked out she called at the third stone, and the tower called back. The answer came late — she could count ten heartbeats before it arrived, whoever called, every time — but it came every time, and by this she knew the stones were whole under her feet.

At the tower's foot stood a door with a narrow slot, and the slot had been taught, on the day the door was hung, to know certain tokens of pale wood by the pattern cut into them. Alda had carved her token as well as she could guess. However she turned it in the slot, the door gave back one flat sentence, the same every night: Not to you.

Before she had ever set out, the one who owned the tower had given her a single instruction: the door's iron is not yours to change. Teach the slot nothing from across the water. Pry nothing. So she left the iron exactly as it stood, night after night, though her arms ached to be useful.

What she could do was prepare. She wrote out every chore fair, tied each bundle with string, the whole winter's work of the tower, ready to carry in a single trip. And since the door would not have her, she laid the bundles in the dry niche under the last arch of the causeway, where the rain could not reach — as close to the tower as she was permitted, and no closer.

Back in the quay-house, the younger clerks would hear the far call come back across the water and write on the wall slate, The tower is with us tonight. The tally-man rubbed the words out and wrote: The road answers. The door has not spoken. Most of them had never stood at the door itself. To them the echo and the welcome were one sound, because they had only ever heard the one.

Two voices rode home with Alda in the evenings. One said: a door that will not learn you is not worth your winters; walk away and let the tower keep its own hours. The other said: put your shoulder to it, break the fogged pane, climb in, and hang a door of your own making. She gave each its hearing and neither its way. The first voice had never looked at the door, and neither had the second; both were guessing at a thing that stood in plain sight one street from her own bed. She would not knock louder tomorrow merely because she had knocked tonight — the door was indifferent to loudness, and her knuckles were not. When she moved, it would be after standing at the slot in daylight, reading what the door had been taught, and changing only what was hers to change.

On the evening slate she kept two lines and only two: The road answers, slow and whole. The door knows us not. Between those two lines all the winter's work lay waiting in the niche, dry and patient, while the tray by the quay-house door grew tall with papers and the tally-man's chalk stayed one color.

And far out past the last arch, behind the blind pane, the lamp kept its hours all the same — lit at six, out at nine, faithful, unseen.

# The Tower That Answers

In the last valley of the province stood a signal tower whose great glass had been broken in a storm years before. The keeper at the top could not see the road, the gate, or the faces of visitors; he knew the world only by what reached him through his door. The province had nearly forgotten the tower until a new steward looked up and said that a tower which could still hear, still speak, and still keep time could earn its bread. It was settled: the tower would take the household's small chores, the ones that must happen at fixed hours, and the far end of the pass would work again.

On the near side, in the steward's study, a letter-box was fastened to the wall with a slot in its lid. Each morning the clerk wrote the day's chores on cards and posted them through the slot, and beside the box hung a slate where every card received its mark: done, or waiting. A porter was hired to carry the cards over the pass, and his round never changed.

At the boundary stone he stopped and called out, to learn whether the road was open and the tower awake. His own voice came back off the high stone — never at once, always after the same half a breath — and so he knew the pass carried sound and the tower stood. Then he climbed to the single door at the top, where a grate was cut into the oak, and knocked.

"Give me your hand," said the keeper, who could not see a face and would trust nothing he had not been told to know. The porter laid his palm on the cold plate beside the bolt. Inside, pages turned. "The book does not know this hand," the keeper said, exactly as he had said every morning, and the bolt did not move.

There was a standing order about that door. The keeper's book was not to be written in, and the door was not to be re-hung — not by the porter, not by the clerk, not by anyone. What the tower had been built to trust it would go on trusting; it would not be re-made because a round had gone badly.

So the porter did not force the bolt. Under the eaves beside the door, where the stones stayed dry, he unpacked his satchel and set the day's cards in a row, each wrapped against the weather and marked with the hour of its chore. The shelf grew longer. Every card he had ever carried was still on it; not one had been carried back.

Down in the study the clerk went on posting fresh cards through the slot, because the list of chores said the pass was open and the tower able, and the list had not yet heard otherwise. The slate filled with waiting marks, one column, and the other column stayed empty. Since the arrangement began, not one chore had been done at the tower.

One day an old doctor from the city rode over the pass with the porter, a man who had spent his life listening to people who repeated themselves. At the boundary stone he had the porter call, counted the half-breaths until the echo returned, and wrote the number down. At the grate he asked how many mornings the porter had heard those words in that order. Every morning, said the porter. The doctor nodded, as if a patient had at last described his complaint plainly: the same answer, in the same words, every day. A tower that repeats itself is not sick; it is being asked by a stranger.

He gave the porter a day-book and one instruction: a single line for every round, always the same four things — whether the stone answered, how long the echo took, which hand was given, whether the bolt moved — and nothing else. Not the weather, not the porter's feelings about the door. A short line, he said, is a line that will still be true next year.

That evening the doctor looked at the slate's lone column and at the fresh cards still going through the slot, and said nothing to the list. He asked instead what the keeper's book was like, and whether anyone living had seen its pages. In the chest where the tower's papers had been kept before the storm, the steward's people found a copy of the first page: one hand described in the old script, down to its ring. The clerk was set to cutting a signet to that description, slowly, because the old script ran crooked. And the porter went on making his round, one line a day, past the stone, up the stair, to the grate where the keeper said, as he always said, "Give me your hand" — and the whole arrangement waited, patient as a shelf, on the making of one small circle of metal.

# The Echo and the Key

In the last year of the estate's keeping, the household set a cousin to live in the annexe across the field. His face had been broken in a fall — the glass of it, the surface a person is read through — and though his hands were whole and his memory sound, no one could again address him by looking at him. It was decided that this was not a misfortune but a use. A man who cannot be looked at is free to be worked, and the annexe was quiet, and the small errands of the house — the winding of the clocks, the nightly copying, the same little tasks at the same hours — were gathered up and given over to him.

Between the great house and the annexe ran no public road. There was a path, but it was a household path: it lay on no map, and it admitted only those whose names had been entered on the roll. A stranger might cross the field all day and never find it. The path ended in the open yard of the annexe, and the yard was free to everyone on the roll; but between the yard and the interior rooms stood a low door beside the woodshed, and at that door sat a keeper whose whole office was refusal. Its lock had been cut to a single pattern, and somewhere in the great house, it was said, one key lay shaped to answer it.

When the steward stood at his gate and called down the path, the cousin's voice came back after the space of a short breath, no longer than a bird needs to cross a yard, saying only: I am here. That was the whole of the permitted conversation. The path carried the call and the answer, and it carried nothing else.

On the desk in the great house stood a shallow tray. Anyone wanting an errand run wrote it in a single line on a slip and dropped the slip in the tray, face up. Beside the tray sat Sela, whose office it was to watch. She did not watch the cousin, who could not be watched; she watched the tray. When a slip lay in it she took it up, read it once, and turned to the slate that hung at her shoulder, where each errand had its mark: a chalk line for waiting, a white circle for done, a black square for refused. The slate did not ask whether an errand was wise. It asked only in which of its three states the errand stood.

Sela could not carry the slips herself. The under-gardener went instead, his name on the roll; he walked the path without trouble and stood in the open yard. At the low door he offered his own name; the keeper said, in a mild voice, that names could be spoken by anyone. He offered his mother's name, and a letter from the household, and at last the household password itself. To each the keeper returned the same sentence, identically, without heat, as though he had been built for nothing else: the key, or nothing. The under-gardener came home with the slips still in his hand. On the slate, Sela drew a black square, and beneath it another, and beneath that another.

There was a rule in the house about that door, and the rule was older than the failure. The lock was not to be touched — not unscrewed, not filed, not propped, not taken from its hinges to make the errands flow. If the door refused, the door was to be answered, not rebuilt. The steward said this at the evening table the way a man repeats a boundary. The younger servants argued that a door which will not open is a defect, and any lock may be re-hung. The steward answered that the lock was not theirs; a house which alters every refusing lock will soon not know its own doors. The lock, he said, is the annexe's way of saying who may command it; take that away, and the errands will run — for anyone who crosses the field.

So the slips were gathered, neither burned nor delivered, and laid in order in the small room off the pantry, each folded to the same width. The room filled slowly. Directions for the winding of the clocks, for the copying, for the nightly count — all finished, all waiting. Anyone might open that door and see what the house intended; no one could make the intentions arrive. In the evenings the steward came and read the slate over Sela's shoulder, and she rubbed out her chalk lines and drew them again. Across the field the cousin sat with his broken face turned to the wall — alive, ready, and not to be asked for anything beyond the one answer the path allowed him.

The steward, it is said, has begun searching the drawers of the old writing desk, at night, for the key. He does not speak of it at table. The house has learned to read the slate, and the black squares have taught it what the path alone never shows: that to call a man is not yet to commission him, and that between the two stands a small, polite, incorruptible refusal.

# The Far Room and the Carved Bead

Across the gorge from his counting house, Hanno kept a small room with one occupant: a servant whose face had been cracked in childhood, like a plate dropped on stone, so that nothing could be read there — and yet his legs were sound and his memory was sound, and he lived by a water clock. When the stone dropped at dawn he swept. At the next drop he filled the pail. At the next he trimmed the lamp. The chores were small and they repeated, and he asked for nothing except to be given more of them.

Between the counting house and the far room there was no public road. A bridge of rope and plank had been strung for these two houses alone, and strangers who came to the gorge found no footing on it; their feet slid from the first rung as if the wood knew them. In the evening Hanno stood at his rail and called across, and his own words came back to him before a drop of honey would leave the spoon — the same quick turn, every evening, so faithful that he set his supper by it.

On the near side, the tally boy sat all day at the window with a tray of slips and a slate. When Hanno wrote out a chore, he dropped the slip in the tray. The boy read each slip as it landed, chalked a mark beside its number on the slate, and laid it ready to be sent across. His whole pride was in being the first to see a slip land, and he would have been wounded to hear it called a small pride.

But between the bridge and the room stood a door with a lock that read nothing but tokens. Hanno's messenger crossed with letters stamped and ribboned — greetings, signatures, the house's own stamp pressed in wax — and the lock gave back the same two words it always gave: Not this. It did not say go away. It did not glance at the letters. It wanted one carved bead, cut to one notching, and it had never wanted anything else in its life. The messenger went again at dusk and again at midnight, and was answered the way weather answers.

In Hanno's drawer lay the deed to the far room, and a smith lived two doors down who, for a jug, would have swapped the lock for one that liked ribboned letters. But pinned above Hanno's desk was a paper in his own hand, signed the day the bridge was strung: the far door's lock is not to be touched, nor the shelf inside it where the bead is kept, until the proper bead is cut. It was his rule and he kept it, even on the days it galled him, because a lock changed for convenience teaches every door in the valley to wait for the smith instead of the token.

So the chores went only as far as the far bank. The messenger copied each slip fair and laid it in the lean-to that stood against the far wall — stacked in order, weighted against the wind, a small paper village waiting for the day the door would read it. Some slips had waited weeks. Inside, the servant swept and filled and trimmed to the old drops of the clock, doing the chores he already knew by heart, wanting the new ones he could not be told of.

One evening Hanno stayed at the rail and counted what the gorge wanted. The servant wanted chores. The boy wanted to be first. The messenger wanted to be let in. Hanno wanted his house to run while he slept. The lock wanted a bead. Every part of it worked — the bridge rang, the door hung true, the servant's legs were good, the copies were fair — and one want, the smallest and plainest, stood unfed; and because it stood unfed, all the other wants spent themselves against it in vain: the knocking, the ribbons, the chalk marks, the midnight crossings. Somewhere in the town there was bone and a knife, and a bead not yet cut to the notching. Cut it and carry it, and nothing else would need to want anything: the door would open on its own hunger, the slips would walk in as though they had always been expected, and the paper village in the lean-to would finally be allowed to grow old. Until then, Hanno called across the gorge at dusk, listened to his voice come back quick and faithful, and went in to supper.

# The Glass-Faced Keeper

The village of Ferrow kept one servant it never saw. He lived across the valley in a small stone hut at the end of a road the villagers had cut themselves, a road worn so narrow and turned so deliberately that a stranger could walk the valley for a lifetime and never find where it began. Only Ferrow's own people could walk it, and only they knew the hut was there.

The keeper had been hurt on that road years before. His face was cracked glass; nothing could be read in it anymore — no anger, no welcome, nothing. But his hands were strong, and at night, it was said, they worked at small tasks the village had no daylight for: winding, sorting, stirring, mending. No one had watched him do these things. The hut's one window was curtained. What the village had instead was the knock.

Each morning the courier Wren walked the hidden road and knocked twice on the hut door. The answer always came. It came in the space of a dropped stone's first bounce — one breath, no more — a double knock from inside, firm and even. That pause was how Ferrow knew its servant was awake. Wren had walked the road in rain and in frost, and the pause had never lengthened and never shortened, and he had come to trust it more than he had ever trusted the face, which had told no one anything, even before it broke.

The door, though, did not open. It had no latch-string, no bell, no wicket. It was made to answer one key — a single iron cut for a single hand — and no such iron hung on Ferrow's ring. Wren had tried every key the village owned, cold mornings, one after another, pressing each into the lock the way a foot tests ice. The door never refused him aloud. It stood, and stood alike for the reeve's key, the smith's key, the church key, and the small key that had once opened a jewelry box no one could anymore find. A door that says nothing says the same thing to everyone.

The council asked the mason to file the lock, or to cut a fresh notch so an ordinary key might turn it. The mason walked out once with his files, looked a long while at the lock, at the deed-box in his shed, and at the door, then came home and put his tools away. The lock stayed exactly as it had been made, and he never spoke of why, and no one made him.

So the village changed what it could reach, which was its own side of the valley. They cut a slot in the post at the road's mouth, and any villager with a task for the keeper wrote it on a slip and slid it through. Beside the slot they set a bench, and on the bench a clerk who watched from first light to last. On a slate nailed above the slot the clerk kept his marks: one row for waiting, one for carried, one for done. He read the rows before he read anything else in the morning, even his own name.

The slips did not shrink the tasks; they shrank the writers. Wren noticed it in himself first. His errands had once been speeches — he would have told the keeper everything, the why, the when, the worry of it. Now each one came out the size of the slot, and the slot was narrow, and what did not fit did not go. The villagers, who had rarely written anything before the slot was cut, now wrote constantly, in hands grown small and even, and if you asked one of them to say an errand aloud, he hesitated, as if asked to sing.

In the shed by the road's mouth the real work lay under canvas: bundles oiled and wrapped and numbered, the winding-tools and the sorting-rakes meant for the keeper's strong hands. Once a week the clerk unwrapped them, wiped them, wrapped them again. None had ever been carried up the road. They were to go through the door or not at all.

Evenings, Wren tried the ring once more, key by key, and then knocked, and the answer came in the space of a dropped stone, exactly as it had come that morning and every morning before it. Then he walked home in the dark. The smith, meanwhile, had taken a wax casting of the lock's mouth and sat late over it with his gravers, learning its notches one by one, the way a man learns a face he has never seen.

The road had never carried a single errand. The knock had never once failed.

# The Horn and the Book of Marks

The light-tower on the rock across the strait had lost its lantern glass in a winter storm, and no boat had gone out since to mend it. The keeper stayed at his post all the same. He had gone half blind in the flash of that night, and worked by touch now: at the appointed hours he trimmed the wick, felt the oil level with his thumb, and set the flame as he had for thirty years. The light went out to sea through the cracks.

On the quay stood the harbor office, and in its wall a brass slot. Whatever needed doing at the tower was written out, folded, and dropped through the slot into a box, and a clerk sat beside the slot from first light to last. Each letter that fell he read once, copied word for word into the day-book — the book was never closed until the copy was made — and then set a pin in the corkboard that covered the north wall. Bone pins for written. Blue for sent across the water. Green for answered. Red for refused. The board was the pride of the office; visitors mistook it for the office's memory, and the clerk never corrected them.

Each evening at dusk the messenger stood at the end of the pier and sounded one note on the horn. The note went out over the strait, and after a while the tower's bell came back — the keeper felt the rock shiver and pulled the rope at his post. The answer took a breath and a half to return: soon enough to prove the rock was inhabited, slow enough to admit the water was wide. Every evening the clerk heard it, and set a green pin.

But the tower had one door, and the door had a wicket, and behind the wicket a doorkeeper who had held the post since before the storm. No one was let in without a mark: an intaglio seal pressed into wax. The doorkeeper would open the wicket a finger's width, take the wax, and hold it against the pages of a small chained book of admitted marks. If the impression matched a page, the bolt was drawn. The messenger carried a mark, pressed it honestly into the wax, and it matched nothing in the book. "The mark you bear is not in my book," the doorkeeper said, and passed the wax back, and closed the wicket, and the bolt stayed shot. He said it exactly the same way, evening after evening, without heat, as a man recites a tide table.

The smith's apprentice, who rowed the messenger sometimes, offered one night to carve the missing mark himself — he had seen the cuts through the wicket's gap, and they were simple — or, quieter still, to lift the wicket's hinge after dark and slip a new page into the chained book by lamplight. The harbormaster took her standing order down from inside her own door, where it hung pinned, and read it to him: the book of marks and the lock it served were to be left exactly as found; a new page might be written only in the keeper's own hand, at his own table, in his own light. Until then, she said, the door is the door.

All the work meant for the tower lay in the shed at the end of the quay: bundles tied with tarred string, each labeled in the clerk's small hand — count the oil, log the weather, ring the hours in the office's order. Every week the bundles were more numerous, the labels more exact, the string neater. Not one of them had ever crossed the water. The keeper, who knew nothing of them, trimmed his wick at his own old hours and let the cracks carry the light.

The green pins multiplied, because the bell answered every dusk. Shipmasters began to ask at the office whether the tower was tended, and the apprentice, who loved the board, would say that it answered every night — which was true, and which was not the question. He proposed painting TENDED in white on the office lintel. Seen from the street, the board did look like the record of a service in full operation; it was the record of a bell. The harbormaster would not have the lintel painted. Say of the tower only what the tower does, she told the boy. It answers. Say that, and stop.

So the dusk kept its shape. The horn went out, the bell came back across the dark water a breath and a half later, the bolt stayed shot, and in the shed the bundles waited, exact as star charts. On the rock the blind keeper trimmed the wick at the hours he had always kept, his light going out to sea through the broken glass, and the office said of it only: it answers.

# The Blind Repeater and the Cedar Tier

In the stone outbuilding beyond the courtyard lay a palm-sized brass repeater that had once belonged to a courier. While its crystal face had been whole, whoever held the instrument had been a creature of the eye—neck bent, thumb twitching at every flare behind the glass. Then the crystal fell against cobbles, cracked, and went dark. Most men would have cast the blinded box onto the scrap-heap, mistaking the picture for the machine. Yet the amputation of one sense wakes another. Stripped of its power to flatter sight, the vessel was set upon a felt pad in the outbuilding to serve by cadence and ear alone, waking at fixed strokes of the clock to work small, recurring chores in the dark. A tool that loses its face stops demanding an audience and begins to keep a rhythm.

Inside that darkened casing, a tiny self-contained smithy had been fitted—a compact chamber with its own bellows, anvil, and tongs—so the blind repeater could hammer out small tasks without calling upon the town's workshops. From the base of the casing ran a sealed copper speaking-tube that bypassed the public turnpike outside the walls, threading beneath the garden flagstones straight into the study where the great oak table stood. Those at the oak table and the blind instrument in the outbuilding were joined by a private acoustic cord that collapsed the yard between them and admitted no stranger's ear.

At the oak table, the arrangement of vessels governed the posture of the Steward who tended them. Pinned inside the rim of the wooden receiving trough was a vellum notice setting forth the purpose of the blind repeater and its timed chores. Beside the trough sat the Steward, his habits shaped by three fixtures before him. First was the receiving trough itself, hollowed to catch every return from the outbuilding. Second was a paper ribbon coiled inside the trough, upon which each event had to be inscribed as a single, unbroken line of ink before the spool advanced; no entry could wrap across two lines. Third was a bone-peg frame beside his elbow, where colored pegs were set or pulled to declare whether the Steward should listen, hold, or sound the bell. By narrowing his craft to a trough, a single-line ribbon, and a board of pegs, the table turned deliberation into reflex.

On the twenty-third afternoon of the tenth month, the Steward tested the buried speaking-tube. He rapped the brass membrane at the oak table with a knuckle and inclined his ear to the horn. Under the flagstones and back again, the answering click returned in a hundred and fifteen beats of the hair-spring—scarcely more than a tenth of a pulse. The private cord was clear; the blind box in the outbuilding was warm, breathing, and reachable across the enclosed line. Yet the swiftness of an echo forever tempts the listener to confuse a live wire with an open threshold.

When the Steward tried to send an actual chore into the inner smithy, he had to slide his grooved bronze crest down the service chute that met the outbuilding at wicket eighty-twenty-two. That wicket was governed by an automatic warder that cared nothing for a lively echo in the speaking-tube; it judged every visitor solely by pressing the ridges of the presented crest against a master mold fastened inside the shutter. The Steward thrust the crest down the chute. Within the wicket, the brass feelers swept the grooves, found no matching profile upon the inner plate, and cast the crest back into his palm. The shutter snapped tight, refusing entry for want of a recognized public crest.

With wicket eighty-twenty-two barred, the passage from the oak table to the blind smithy stood stopped from end to end. The Steward could hear the outbuilding humming along the buried tube, yet not a single errand could cross into the smithy's hands. A young helper reached for a chisel, offering to cross the yard, unfasten the warder of the blind box, and carve a matching groove into its inner plate of accepted crests. The Steward stayed the boy's wrist and tapped the vellum notice in the trough. Their charter forbade altering the warder's mechanism or cutting new profiles into the blind box's inner register. A boundary that may not be forced remakes the hand that waits before it.

Denied the chute, the Steward neither burned the chore-scrolls he had drafted nor forced them into the locked wicket. Instead, he carried each prepared roll to an outer cedar tier beside the oak table, a waiting-bench reserved for work made ready in advance. One by one he laid the scrolls in clean ranks upon the cedar tier, inked and counted, to rest untouched until the bronze crest and the inner plate should lawfully match. The locked wicket had quietly altered the trade of the study: men who had rigged a blind runner to hasten their routine were now reshaped into keepers of a still shelf, learning that a blocked vessel does not carry messages—it turns action into rehearsal.

# The Far House

In a river town in the south there lived a magistrate who kept, two days' ride from the courts, a small house at the edge of a barley district. A caretaker held it year-round. The caretaker's trade was small chores: he wound the clock, he wrote the day's weather into a tablet, he rang the yard bell at the fixed hours, and he did whatever was written on any slip that reached his hands. He worked by the chimes, watched or unwatched, and this was his whole value — that the chores kept their hours with no one standing over him.

He had one infirmity. The window of his workroom was cracked from corner to corner, and he could read nothing through it. So he worked by touch and by ear, by the feel of the wax and the sound of the bell, and it was said in the district that his hands had never once mistaken an hour.

There was no public road between the town and the far house. Years before, the magistrate had subscribed to a society of householders who maintained, between their properties, a covered footpath with a gate at either end. No stranger walked it. Every walker bore a token stitched into the lining of his coat, and the path, it was said, knew the stitching.

On the twenty-third of the month, with the vintage coming in, the magistrate went down to his end of the path and called a single word into it — the caretaker's word, the society's custom. The word went out and came back. Not at once, but inside a slow blink: quicker than any letter, slower than an answer across a room. He wrote in the margin of his day-book only what he had: called on the twenty-third, answered, a slow blink.

But a chore does not travel by a called word. A chore goes by hand, to the low door at the side of the far house, the door kept for bundles. When the magistrate's man went down the path with the first slip, the steward looked at him through the grille and said: "You are not known. There is a roll of coats this door opens for, and yours is not on it. Bring the coat my roll names, or do not come." And the grille closed. He did not say the house was dark. He did not say the path was broken. Both were otherwise, as the call had shown. He said only: not known.

It was in the magistrate's power, as the society's papers explained, to send down new names and have them added to the steward's roll, for the roll lived inside the far house. But his own commission forbade it, in a clause he had drafted: the lock at the low door was the far house's own law, and no hand of his would stitch a coat onto another man's roll, however far off that man sat. There was a rightful way to be known, or there was nothing; and nothing would have to do until the way was found. He wrote that week to the society's clerk, asking by what rightful means a coat came to be entered on a roll.

Meanwhile the work did not go away. In the vestibule of the magistrate's house the parcels stood in a row on a trestle: the slips for the clock, the slips for the tablet, the slips for the bell, each folded, waxed, sealed, and marked with its hour. They had been packed against the day the steward's roll would carry the right coat. None had been opened since. None had been sent. The magistrate dusted them himself on Fridays and pressed each seal with his thumb to see that it held.

In his study, at his elbow, stood a shallow tray. Into it he dropped folded slips as the errands occurred to him, one slip to an errand, laid in the order they came and never rearranged. A boy came through the study on rounds he announced to no one and kept to the minute. When he found a slip in the tray he took the topmost, went down the covered path to the low door, was refused at the grille, came back, and set a chalk stroke on the slate that hung beside the tray, with the hour in a small hand beside it. The slate said no more than that: refused, at such an hour, again, again.

A colleague at the courts, hearing the matter recited, said: "Then the far house is yours in everything but name." The magistrate would not have it. "I have a call answered and a door shut," he said. "The call I possess. The house I neighbor. I will write down the first, and of the second I will write nothing I cannot knock on."

That night, at the bottom of the day-book, he wrote: I have discovered a truly admirable means of entering by that low door, which the bottom of this page is too narrow to contain. He read it over twice. Then, being a magistrate, and knowing what became of men who wrote more than they could knock on, he added beneath it, in a smaller hand: The call held. The rest is owed.

The parcels stood. The tray filled, and the boy's rounds went on, and the strokes went down the slate one under another, each like the last. Every evening the magistrate called the word into the path, and every evening it came back inside a slow blink. As long as it did, he sold nothing, wrote nothing off, and let the wax keep.

# The Fog Road

The manor kept a small house across the marsh, and between the two ran a road with no public mouth: a brass tube laid under the peat by the builder of both, opening only at the pantry wall on one side and at the porch of the far house on the other. Strangers who crossed the marsh found reeds, mud, and fog; the tube appeared on no map they would ever hold. What one mouth put in, the other took out, and nothing else used the road at all.

Nobody lived in the far house but a clock. Two winters ago its dial was broken — a cart on the ice, the story varied — and the glass went to lace and splinter, so that no one had read the face since. The works never stopped. The house struck once for the lamps, twice for the coals, three times for the walk, and the manor had long ago learned to trust the strikes without ever looking at the face.

Ivo the steward had chores for that clock. He wrote each chore on a numbered slip and dropped it into the box by the pantry wall, and Pell, the boy who tended the box, chalked the slate that hung beside it: a circle for waiting, a line for gone down the tube, a cross for sent back. Nothing was a chore until it was a slip, and no slip went in without a number.

Before trusting a chore to the road, Ivo tested the road the only way that counted. He dropped a pebble into the mouth and counted. The pebble always came back — turned around somewhere in the far porch — and the pause never changed: shorter than a spoken syllable, longer than nothing, the span of a wasp's wing catching, the same in fog as in clear weather. He wrote the count in his notebook each time, because a counted thing quiets a room better than an oath.

The pebble, though, was as far as an unproven hand could go. To leave a bundle, you had to pass the inner door of the far house, and the inner door kept a single manner: it read carved tokens, grain and all, and it would hear nothing else. Ivo sent his token down the tube — whole, handsome, honestly cut — and the door gave back one flat sentence, the sound of a book closing: "Not one of mine." It said the same on Tuesday and on Friday, instantly, every time. Ivo noted the instantness. A dead house says nothing at all; a live house says no.

Bram the glazier heard about the broken dial and told the story his trade required: a cracked face meant cracked works; the road under the marsh was a dead road; the far house was furniture now. He told it at the mill and at the christening, and the town repeated it because it was short and fit the mouth, and the story sold glass. Whoever believed it stopped dropping pebbles. The dust that gathered on an unused mouth was taken, of course, for proof — look, they said, unused — and the story fed on the quiet it had itself caused.

The steward had spent his life near men like Bram and knew the trade from the inside, the way a reformed forger knows a signature. He did not argue; he opened the notebook. The pebble returned in its counted pause. The strikes came across the water on the hour. The door said its one sentence, which was one sentence more than the dead say. What he did not know — and wrote that he did not know, in the place kept for not-knowing — was when the door's memory would hold a token of his. That memory was the door's own. The standing orders, in the builder's hand, hung framed by the shelf: no hand carves its own token into a door's keeping from the outside — not for harvest, not for fire, not for the best errand in the world; the memory is changed by the hand that keeps the door, and by no other. Ivo read the frame once a week the way other men read weather.

So the chores went to the shed — the long shed by the mouth, where the numbered bundles stood wrapped against the damp and in order, each with its slip's number chalked on the wood, the lamp bundles behind the coal bundles, the walk and the gutters behind those. None opened, none abandoned, and the slate by the box still held nothing but circles. When the door knows the token, Ivo told the boy, nothing will be written twice; the bundles will simply go down the road in the order they were made.

The town would keep Bram's version, Ivo knew, because the true one was long: the road open, the clock alive, the door particular, the shed full, the mending small and belonging to another hand, the day of it not yet written. "Shut" was the version that fit the mouth, and what it cropped was everything that was merely waiting. Ivo sold no counter-story at all. He left the notebook open on the pantry shelf, so that anyone who wanted the longer version would have to come in and count it for himself.

# The Night Mill

At the far end of a causeway, beyond the salt marsh, stood a night mill. Its face of glass had been shattered years before — a cart had backed into it, or a stone had; no one now remembered — and so the mill showed the time to no one. The works inside still turned. Deep in them hung a bell the first miller had set to ring at certain hours, and when it rang, the stones ground whatever the hopper held. The mill kept its own hours; it did not ask the town what time it was, and the town, reading the broken face, could not have told it.

In town, at the errand house, a clerk wrote out errands on slips of paper — grind this, sweep the stones, open the sluice at the third bell — and dropped them into a willow basket by the door. A boy was employed upon the basket and the road. He carried the slips out along the causeway, and he carried them back, and every going and every return went into his day-book in a small exact hand. Above the errand-house door hung a board of pegs with ribbons of dyed wool: green when the road was quiet, red when a slip had come back, white when a day passed with nothing sent at all. People read the door the way they read weather.

The road was the town's own work and its pride. Plank by plank it had been laid over ground that belonged to no one, and no stranger could find footing on it; the planks answered only to the town's own weight. Each morning the crier walked to the third post and shouted toward the mill, and each morning an answer came back in the time it takes to blink. The crier learned to count that time in half-beats, and he reported the count at the errand house, and the count was written beside the weather, as a kind of weather itself.

The mill had one door, and the door had one hook, and on the hook hung the boxwood tokens the door would answer to. Each was carved with notches, and the door knew its own carvings the way a dog knows its master's step. The clerk had carved a token too — carefully, sanded, the dust blown off — but he had carved it to his own pattern and not to the door's, and the door knew the difference. When the boy reached the letter-slot and offered it, the slot pressed it back into his palm, gently and finally, as a sleeping man's hand refuses a coin.

It would have been a small thing to knock at the mill and ask the miller to carve the door's pattern into a second token, or to hang the clerk's on the hook beside the rest. But when the causeway was laid the town had sworn about that door: the door at the far end belonged to the far end, and no one from town would ever carve it, file it, or hang a new hook on it, however good the reason. The oath had been sworn for causes that everyone had forgotten, and everyone kept it anyway. So the slot pressed the token back, and the boy walked home, and the red ribbon went up on the pegs.

The errands went on being written. If anything they were written better: numbered, the margins ruled, folded in a way that kept the marsh sand from working into the wax. Since none could be delivered, they were stacked face-down, in order, on shelves in the shed at the town end of the causeway — shelves the cooper built to the clerk's measurements. By autumn the shed needed a second shelf. By spring it had a boy of its own, hired to keep the slips in order and to sweep, dust having been found to offend the wax. When travelers asked to see the town's works, they were shown the shed first, and the clerk explained its arrangements with pleasure.

At Michaelmas the crier's count came back half a beat shorter than it had ever been, and this was drunk to at the errand house. That week the basket hung empty, as it had hung every week; nothing had crossed the door, and so nothing at the mill had happened by the town's hand. The count went into the day-book all the same. The book did not say what the count was for, and no one asked it to.

One evening the boy — the first boy, the boy of the basket — sat copying his day-book by the stove, and the clerk looked over his shoulder at the long column of red returns, each with its hour, each with its half-beat count beside it, running back page after page to the day the slot first pressed a token into a palm. The clerk read to the bottom of the page and turned back to the first. "It is a fine book," he said. It was. Nothing in it had ever crossed the door, and it was the only thing in the town that said so, plainly, every day, and would tomorrow.

# The House That Answers but Does Not Open

At the edge of the marsh stood a grey cottage where the small work of the estate was meant to happen — the sweeping, the winding of the clocks, the copying of lists too dull for the great house. The work had been given to a servant who lived there alone. He had once had a face of glass; a fall had starred it with cracks, and after the fall no one could speak to him eye to eye. The glass had been his only way of being spoken to. What lay beneath the cracks still worked — his hands, his memory, his patience — but no one had yet found the way in to reach them.

The great house kept a desk for him. Anyone with a chore wrote it on a slip and fed the slip through a brass slot, and each slip fell onto a single long ribbon that coiled in a box below the desk, one chore after another, nothing ever unwritten. Beside the slot sat a watchman whose whole duty was the ribbon and a board of small painted pegs: when a chore went out, he turned a peg; when it came back, another; when it died in the dark, a black one. The watchman had one older duty still. Every so often he walked to the end of the causeway and called across the marsh — one flat syllable — and waited.

Between the houses ran a private causeway laid under the water, built long ago by the founder so that the estate's feet would never touch the public roads. No one walked it uninvited. And the causeway answered. The syllable went out and an echo came home — late, always a little late, a slow breath late, as though the sound had to stoop under the marsh — but it came home every time. On this the whole estate agreed: the road is alive, and the far house is not deaf.

But a road is not a door. When the scribe finally went out to bring the servant his first chores, she knocked at the low door of the grey cottage. A voice came through the wood, quiet and without anger. "Show me your hand." She laid her palm flat against the grain. A pause. "This hand was never given to me," the voice said. "Inside this door I keep impressions in wax, every hand the house ever promised to know. Yours is not among them. I cannot open."

She offered the household word instead — the old word that opens gates in other parts of the estate. The voice did not soften. "I do not trade in words. Only in hands. Bring the hand that was promised, or have your hand pressed into the wax by someone standing already inside. Those are the two ways, and there is no third."

She thought for a moment of melting the wax herself, of lifting the keeper from his stool. But she carried the founder's letter, and it was plain: the door's memory belongs to the house; no one from outside shall carve, melt, or recast the wax, and no one shall pull the keeper down; the cottage has taken its harm once and will not take it twice for anyone's convenience. She read the letter twice by the water's edge and put it away.

So she did what could still be done. Behind the great house stood a lean-to shed, and into it she carried every prepared chore, sealed and numbered in the order of the ribbon, ready for the day the door would know her. Nothing crossed the marsh after that except the watchman's syllable and its late echo. The ribbon grew. The pegs turned from waiting to waiting. The bundles kept their numbers in the dark.

In the evenings the scribe sat with her own hand open on her knee and would not blame the door. The road had never refused her; the keeper had never refused her. What had been refused was smaller and nearer: a promise made and never kept — her hand's impression never carried in, never left in the wax — so that to that house she was only weather knocking. She wrote this on a slip and fed it through the slot with the rest. The watchman would find it in the morning and turn no peg, because it was not a chore. It was the day's account: the house answers, the house does not open, and both of those are honest; the fault lies folded in her own sleeve, waiting to be carried across.

# The Clouded Eye and the Wax

On the far side of the gorge lived a mule called Moss, and one of his eyes had gone white as a boiled egg. Nothing came through it any more. The villagers had once read that eye for temper and for weather; now it showed them nothing at all, and they stopped looking. But his legs were honest. At first light he stood at the well, unled. At noon he stood at the mill. At dusk he stood at the ferry steps. No one drove him; the round walked itself in him, and at each stop the villagers set a small load on his back — a sack, a pail, a bundle of sticks — and he carried it to the next. A beast whose face is ruined can still be trusted with a small load at a known hour, over and over. His eye was gone; his habit was not.

On the near side lived a carter who wanted that round to carry his goods too. Since he could not be everywhere, he cut a slot into the post at the bridgehead and dropped slips through it, one slip to an errand. Beside the post he set a boy named Ivo on a stool with one duty: when a slip fell, read it once, then lay a pebble on it. The pebble said, this one is taken; it will not be read again. Some mornings brought three slips, some none; Ivo's pebbles never lied and never doubled.

Between the houses lay the gorge. Each morning the carter walked to the edge and whistled four notes, and before a sparrow could beat its wings twice, four notes came back. This delighted him, and he began to time it, morning after morning, finer and finer. Yes: the air over the gorge was open, quick in both directions. He told the village the road was open. Even the village dogs knew better; they barked at the echo once and then slept.

Sound was the only thing crossing. When the carter wanted his slips to become loads, he filled baskets and sent his brother's boy over the bridge. At the far gate sat a warden, an old woman with a wax tablet. She was not unkind and she did not argue. She held the tablet out. The boy said the carter's name — loudly at first, then carefully, then slowly, as to a dull listener — and she did not stir, for her ears had been closed to names by the founding word of her house. She wanted one thing only: a mark pressed into the wax, cut from the very hand that filled the basket. "Names are wind," she said, gently. "Press the wax." The boy carried only his voice. She turned him back each time, and the gate stood shut, and behind it the mule walked his round with none of the carter's goods on his back.

The founding word bound the carter too: the gate stands as it was hung; the warden shall not be argued with, replaced, nor taught to hear; no second door shall be cut through the wall, for as long as the true mark has not been carried to her. So the carter did not pry the gate, did not oil the hinge, did not send bread to the warden or a louder boy. That restraint was the one thing he did not dress up.

The baskets went into the shed at the bridgehead instead, stacked in order, each labeled in his own hand. None crossed. None was burned. The shed grew, and the round on the far side kept walking without them; Moss drew the water at first light whether the carter's goods arrived or not.

Now I lift the lantern here, carter — you, at the near house, timing your echo again this evening. You whistle louder each season. You write longer slips, warm letters commending your own name, to be read aloud to a woman deaf to names since before your mother was born. You call the road open because the air answers you. The air was never the question; the wax is the question. You are asking the gate to want what you happen to carry — a voice — when it has told you plainly, every day, what it is built to receive. Stop asking the gorge to recognize you. The mule does not recognize you either, and he would carry for you anyway, because carrying is what his legs are for. Be like the mule: bring the thing the door is made to take, or stack your baskets and say to the village, "The road is shut; my goods wait; the far round walks without me." Your part of the errand is the only part that is late.

The pebbles were the truest things on the near side of the gorge. Each one said: this was asked for once, and once was enough. The carter could have learned from his own pebbles.

# The Mouth of the Path

Esther's kitchen had a slot in its west wall, and a stool beside the slot, and on the stool a girl called Pell who did nothing all day but mind the two. When Esther had work for the far side of the valley, she wrote it small on a paper slip and fed it through. Pell drew the slip out, read it aloud once in a flat voice — saying what was written, never what was meant — and copied it as a single ruled line into the long book chained to her table. Then she turned a pebble in the cup at her elbow, pale side up, and the pebble said: given, not yet taken. Esther had learned not to reach for the slips again. Whatever went through the slot belonged to the slot.

The work itself was meant for her cousin in the hollow beyond the ridge, a man nobody had seen in years. A fall had shattered the glass of his face long ago, and nothing had crossed it since — no smile, no frown, no appeal. But his hands had kept their habits, and the habits kept their hours. At the first bell he drew water whether anyone had asked. At the noon bell he fed the fire. At the last bell he banked it and latched the flues. No one alive remembered instructing him. The hours themselves were the instruction, and he was the sort of man who could not be late to himself.

There was one way to him: a smugglers' path under the ridge, too narrow for a cart, its mouth hidden in the gorse so that only family could find it. Each morning Esther walked down, stood at the mouth, and called. A bell in the hollow answered — it always answered, though the sound needed a slow heartbeat to come back over the ridge. That much the path granted her, and she came to distrust what it granted. The bell said only: the path is open, the hollow hears. It did not say the hollow would open.

For the door at the far end had one law. It would swing for a token cut with the family's notches, and for nothing else. Esther had a token; she had cut it herself, at home, by lamplight, sure of her eye. When she pushed it into the door's slot, the door drew it in, weighed every notch in the dark, and handed it back with one flat sentence: No notch of mine recognizes this. The door said nothing else, ever, to anyone.

She went home furious and called the door broken. Pell turned the newest pebble over, dark side up, and said without lifting her ruling pen: "It isn't broken. It read you. Reading you and agreeing with you were always two different acts." Esther did not want to hear that a refusal was a reading. She wanted the door to have failed, because a failed door could be mended by force.

A tinker came through that autumn and offered, for a coin, to climb the wall or pry the plate from the door and file its wards to fit her token — an hour's work, he said, and the door as good as hers. Esther stood at the mouth of the path with the coin in her fist and did not hand it over. The family's standing instruction was short and old: the door stays exactly as the maker hung it; no ward filed, no hinge lifted, not while the path is to remain a family path. The tinker shrugged and moved on to easier villages.

So the chores piled. She sewed each slip into oilcloth and stacked the bundles in the lean-to at the mouth of the path, in the order they should be done, the first at the front. Nothing crossed the ridge. Pell's book filled its pages, one line to a chore, and the cup filled with pale stones. Some mornings Esther counted the bundles and the pale stones and found they agreed exactly; agreeing exactly was all they did.

In winter she took the ferry across the water to the old carver and laid her token on his bench. He turned it under the lamp, twice, and said the fault was not mysterious: cut again, in the right wood, to the pattern the family's doors actually kept — for the notch that opens a door is not the notch you meant to cut; it is the notch the door knows. He would not go near the door. He would recut the token, and the door, being unaltered, would still be standing to meet it.

She came home over the ice with the old token at her throat and the new one promised. That night, at the last bell — she could not hear it across the ridge, but she knew its hour — the cousin banked his fire and latched his flues, instructed by nobody, on time. In the morning the bell answered her call, one slow heartbeat late. The lean-to stood full and in order. Esther stood at the mouth of the path with two carvings in her hand, one failed and one promised, and understood that until the notches met, every chore she owed the hollow was a paper lying closed on its shelf — sent, heard, and not yet read.
