# The Lamp at Noon

I came up to the town at noon with my lamp lit, and the gatekeeper laughed at me for it. I told him I was looking for water that actually runs, and he stopped laughing, because he did not know where any was. A dog fell in behind me and stayed the whole afternoon, which I took as the town's fairest judgment of me.

The water had a warden. The elders had given him his office — the elders meet each season in a small room and speak of nothing but water, and what they agree on there becomes the town's law of thirst.

I walked the channel with him and asked what the office was. He held up a hand and counted on his fingers. "I count the jars," he said. "I beat the drum on the hour. I tell the master what I saw. I turn the glass. And I dip my own cup, once a day, to prove my hands still carry." He looked at his thumb, found nothing for it, and folded it away. "Pouring is not mine," he said. "I touch no gate. The draw-keepers open their own; I call out how the glass stands, and they pour or they wait by what I call. If I am wrong, they will at least have been the ones who opened it."

The storeroom held forty-eight jars in two long rows, every one labeled in a fine hand — rain, river, well, dew, down the whole shelf. His book held all forty-eight names, copied twice, never smudged. I put my lamp close and reached for a lid, and he asked me politely to take my hand off it. "The names are mine," he said. "What is under the lids belongs to the jars." He could tell me which jar was promised to which gate, and he could not tell me whether a single one of them held water, and he said the second thing as plainly as the first, which is rarer in a town than rain.

Then we walked the north wall, where the pipes run, and I held the lamp to each mouth in turn. Six answered with the sound of running water — six, and no more. Others gave back nothing: fine mouths, true brass, set deep in the wall by masons who went home and never came back, joined to nothing on the far side of the stone. The longest pipe of all ran up the hill toward a spring that had died two winters since. The masons' roll still counted it among the living. The warden's book did not; beside its name he had written the one word, dead, and left the name standing so that anyone who opened the book would see both. I asked why the masons kept it on their roll. "Because a roll remembers and a wall forgets," he said, "and neither of them drinks."

In his hut stood the glass — it runs five hours, no more and no less, and he turns it the moment it empties, day and night. Beside it lay a tablet of wax with a line for each gate, and on each line the share that gate might still draw. As the glass ran he scraped the lines down, so the wax thinned as the sand thinned, and when he turned the glass he warmed the tablet smooth and wrote the shares fresh. "What a gate may draw belongs to this run of the glass," he said. "The next run is a new page." I asked about a gate whose line was scraped away early. "It waits for the turning," he said.

The master came down at dusk to watch the gates pour. He was broad and cheerful, and everything was spent under his eye: the wheels turned, the troughs filled, the gardens drank until they shone, and nothing was held back for morning. "I would rather stand at an empty cistern at moonrise than a full one," he told me. "Water kept on a shelf is a debt the moon collects."

I said to him what I will say to you, since you are standing where he stood: You have six pipes that run, a glass that turns, a man honest about his dead, and a town watered every evening to the last jar. And still, every morning, you ask the mountain for one more spring than it has, and keep jars you will never open, so that the shelf will look like a river. You could stop asking today. I own one jar, I sleep in it, and there is room in it for the lamp.

He looked at my jar a long while. Then he laughed — the second man in that town to laugh at me, and the first to laugh at himself — and had the masons' roll read aloud in the square that night, dead pipe and all, by lamplight.

# The Warden Who Counted Names

Iris came to the hill town in a dry autumn, sent for by no one, to sit and listen to its water. The town stood on a ridge of cisterns and pipes, and at the top of the ridge was a lodge with smooth walls where the warden lived. On her first evening she climbed to call on him and found no door, no bell, no slot for letters, not even a knothole. She knocked on the stone and the stone said nothing. A boy passing with a pail laughed at her politeness. "He comes out when it is time," the boy said. "It is always time for something. It is never time for calling."

She learned his times by waiting, which is also a way of asking. At first light the warden came out to count. The jar room held forty-eight jars, each with a label pasted at eye level, and he walked the rows touching nothing, naming every label aloud, sorting them in a soft voice into the bitter kind, the clear kind, the kind from away. Iris asked him once what the seventh jar held. He seemed genuinely startled. "No one has asked me that," he said. "I was not made thirsty."

Between counts he walked the ridge with a step that never varied, heel on stone at one interval from morning to night, a drum of a single note; anyone who needed the town's pulse had only to listen for his feet. At each turn he stopped at the gatehouse and spoke the day aloud — the count, the dryness, the hours — to whoever stood there, and he spoke just as fully when no one did. On seventh days he stood in the yard and tried himself: touched his toes, recited his rounds backwards, said his own name to the wall. Yet the night the east pipe burst and the yard ran shallow, the warden stood at the water's edge and described it — the seam, the hour, the sound it made — without moving one step toward the valve. He carried no key to any valve. A girl from the lower street waded in and shut it, cursing him, and he agreed with every word she said, and wrote down the hour of the burst.

Six pipes on the ridge carried water. Iris walked them, and she walked the others too: bolted to the wall as firmly as the living six, jointed, waiting, dry as chalk, with no mark of ever having held anything. The map in the council hall showed every one of them full.

The council met in a long hall with a table for roads, a table for bread, a table for the boundary stones — and one small table where three members gathered on Thursdays to speak of water alone. Nothing left that table but words, and the town was content with that. It was there Iris found the board. It held the drawing hours: which hours water might be taken, and beside each hour a little glass drawn shrinking. Every five hours the board was wiped and written again from the top, and the town arranged its kettles and its washing around that turning.

On her third Thursday the miller stood to give his account: every jar on his shelf empty by dusk, the last poured over the stones rather than kept. "Better it runs out," he said, "than stands." The three nodded. No one at any table defended a full jar. A boy who had saved one back carried it through the square and poured it out before everyone, and the town watched gently, as at a cure.

Only the counting troubled Iris, and it troubled her the way a word said wrongly three times troubles a listener. Among the forty-eight labels was one that read the bitter spring, flowing. She had walked to the bitter spring on her second day: a dead mouth of stones, the moss gone grey, and the oldest of the three members could not recall it wet. Yet every morning the warden's finger passed that label and his voice said flowing, present tense, the tense he gave the six that lived. She heard it nine times. On the ninth she asked him softly, "What keeps happening that nobody asks after?" and he answered without knowing he had: "Everything here is poured out on schedule, and the schedule is never told which springs have died."

On her last Thursday she borrowed the chalk and wrote beside the bitter spring's name the year it had failed and the words no water since. She changed nothing else. The glass turned, the six ran, the miller emptied his shelf, the warden kept his one-note walk. But the next morning his finger passed the bitter label and his voice said, evenly, the bitter spring, dead these years, and moved to the next name; and when Thursday came again, the small table spent its hour on bread, there being nothing left about the water that wanted saying.

# The Chalkboard at the Cistern

Julian held the post of Master of the Cisterns, though anyone entering his glass pavilion above the courtyard could see at once that he possessed no levers, no speaking-tubes, and no bell-wires reaching the stables. He had no authority to send a single water-cart down the road or command a bailiff to open a spigot. Those who suppose governance requires a barked order have never studied how a household is actually moved; Julian knew that when you arrange the public display by which men measure their thirst, they harness the teams themselves and believe the urgency was their own idea. By charter, his post permitted only five quiet routines: walking the cellar racks to sort the labels on the sealed stock, striking the bronze courtyard gong to mark the hour, pinning a fresh broadsheet to the easel, chalking five-hour tide-lines across the blackboards, and tapping his own brass balance-weights with an ivory mallet to verify the instrument remained true.

Every morning at dawn, Julian descended to the stone vault where forty-eight porcelain flasks stood in notched timber cradles. Each flask bore a painted ribbon across its neck naming the distant hillside or merchant house from which it had been drawn. Julian walked the rows with his portfolio, grouping all forty-eight titles into neat families on his chart—sweet or brackish, highland or marsh—yet he never once twisted a stopper, held a vessel against the lantern to judge its level, or tasted a drop of what lay inside. To unseal a flask would have broken the law of the vault; the household craved the comfort of a classified inventory, and the illusion of mastery depended on sorting the names on the porcelain while leaving the question of whether the vessels were full or dry untouched behind the wax.

Outside the vault, the estate's working plumbing told a less tidy story. Along the gravel apron stood four heavy dray-wagons, their axles greased, their oak casks lashed tight to the planks, and their brass fittings polished—yet not one was coupled to a hose from the spring-house. The stable-hands admired them daily, soothed by the spectacle of reserve power that could not in truth carry a cup of water to the sculleries. Only six stone conduits were actually mortared into the main sluice and carried living water into the estate's troughs. Beside them ran a seventh trough of dressed granite, once famed across the valley for the blinding speed of its cascade; now its bed lay bone-dry and split by frost, choked with dead thistles and marked at the headgate with a smear of black tar so no cook would waste an afternoon waiting by its mouth.

To guide the stewards without ever uttering a command, Julian relied upon a revolving sand-glass mounted high on the courtyard wall. The glass did not measure the deep seasonal pond behind the ridge; its sand ran through a five-hour collar turned continuously on the quarter-hour, tracing only the rate at which the most recent five hours of allowance had drained from the common basin. Whenever the chalk line on the blackboard showed that five-hour trickle running slow, a restless guilt spread through the kitchens; whenever it plunged steeply, the bailiffs checked their ladles without a word from the pavilion. Every faction in the yard watched that rolling five-hour shadow and adjusted its pouring, never asking who had chosen five hours as the measure of necessity.

Above the easel hung an oak plaque carved for the estate's proprietor, who gained most when every drop was turned into steamed grain and washed wool before nightfall. The inscription decreed that a bailiff who emptied the casks to the bare staves by dusk would be forgiven, whereas a thrifty servant who left full vessels sitting untouched on the rack at moonrise would be dismissed. Dread of idle abundance did the work that no whip could achieve: the grooms poured briskly through the afternoon, driven by the fear of being caught at sundown with unused water still resting on the rack.

Once a week, the elders who styled themselves the Council of the Well gathered on the upper gallery to inspect Julian's broadsheets. They gained the flattering certainty that the estate's lifeblood stood under their rational supervision, and that belief moved them to ratify the winter allowances without ever glancing at the dry granite trough or the uncoupled wagons on the gravel. Julian watched them nod over his clean columns and kept silent. He knew the first law of every public spectacle: the version an audience will gladly accept always defeats the version that is complete, and the price of their calm consent was everything his neat five-hour blackboard cropped from the frame—the untasted flasks behind the wax, the harnessed wagons linked to nothing, and the silent stone channel that would never run again.

# The Proportion of the Cisterns

Master Laurent kept his vigil in the octagonal stone chamber above the estate's reservoirs, a room built without casements, postern gates, or speaking-tubes to the outer roads. No courier rode from his threshold, and no bell-rope or conduit passed through his masonry to the distant fields; he was a geometer of interior measures, stationed where the waters were reckoned but never drawn. Around him lay the instruments of a counselor who studies a game in motion without ever casting the dice. Though the bailiffs in the courtyard below often waited for him to order ditchers to the sluices, Laurent possessed neither horn nor baton. By the charter of his office, his hand might never lift a floodgate or send a bearer toward the troughs; to command the stream was a power withheld from the man appointed only to declare its true proportion.

Five operations alone were permitted to him, executed in strict succession, as one reduces a stubborn equation by measured steps. First, he walked the timber racks to number and group the vessels stored for the season. Second, he laid his palm against the main bronze trunk to count the rhythmic pulse of the current beneath the flagstones. Third, he inscribed a plain summary of the day's standing upon a parchment scroll. Fourth, he observed the glass clepsydras that divided the day into shifting spans of usage. Fifth, he tested his own brass scales with known leaden grains, descending from the pound to the scruple until he was certain no warp in his beam could falsify the theorem of the domain. Beyond those five acts, his margin was too narrow to contain a sixth.

Along the western wall stood forty-eight stoppered phials of dark green glass in four tiers of twelve. Each phial bore a strip of vellum glued across its neck, inscribed with the title of the spring or cistern from which its warrant derived. When Laurent performed his numbering, he read every one of the forty-eight titles aloud and sorted them into their proper genera upon his tally-sheet—some belonging to the upper snow-melt, some to the deep chalk wells, some to the mill-ponds. Yet he never once twisted a cork, broke a thread of wax, or held a phial to the candle to see whether the liquor inside was clear, brackish, or absent. By the statute of his chamber, the reckoner must sort every vessel by its superscription alone, remaining forever blind to the draught stoppered within.

From the lower reservoir, six leaden aqueducts ran fully soldered to the main header, their joints tight and their channels humming with live water for the vineyards. Beside them, however, the masons had bolted four heavy bronze spigots directly into the dressed stone of the wall, polished and gleaming, yet left them with their tail-pieces hanging in empty air, uncoupled to any pipe behind the masonry. A stranger entering the vault would have sworn ten conduits served the estate, when only six carried a drop. Worse still was the seventh channel on the southern side, the narrow race once praised across the province for the violent speed of its torrent; its stonework had split at the foundation, its bed lay choked with dry chalk, and Laurent marked it on his scroll as extinct beyond recovery.

To judge the spending of the six living streams, Laurent consulted a single wax tablet on his trestle, where the outlay of water was scored across a sliding span of five hours. As each quarter-hour chimed, the oldest mark at the tail of the five-hour span was smoothed away and the newest draw was cut at the head, so that the reckoning glided perpetually forward. In calculating the expectation of what remained before the five-hour glass turned, Laurent followed the blunt maxim carved by the lord of the estate above the lintel: it was far better for the vineyards to drink the reservoir down to the wet gravel before the fifth hour expired than to leave a single gallon standing idle in the vats when the new allowance poured in. Stagnant abundance was a failure of geometry; an empty cistern at the turn of the glass proved that every drop had done its work.

At dusk, when the five-hour tally showed the troughs nearly spent and the six live aqueducts running at a thin trickle, Laurent wound his parchment and laid it in the wicker tray beside the stair. He did not address it to the general assembly of the barons, who argued over grain tariffs and boundary ditches, but directed it solely to the Chamber of the Springs, that select bench of the estate's senate charged with governing the watercourses alone. When the elders of that chamber climbed the stair to read his figures, they found Laurent already wiping his stylus, content to stand at the edge of the proof—having demonstrated to the last ounce where the stream had gone, while leaving to others the hand that turned the wheel.

# The Dry Rotunda

In the limestone rotunda above the terraced fields, the hydrographer Vane governed the estate's water without a single pipe passing through his floor. The builders had bricked up every aperture through which a wire, a speaking-tube, or a valve-rod might have reached the sluices below; his chamber was entirely dry, severed from the conduits so that he could neither draw a cup nor turn a wheel. Power in that circular room did not move through hands; it operated through the partitioned gaze. The charter engraved on his desk permitted him five motions and no sixth: he might classify the vessels in the cellar, sound the rhythmic pulse of the main drum, post the morning sheet upon the pillar, read the five-hour sand-dials, and test the balance of his own brass scales. Command was forbidden to him. When a bailiff in the courtyard looked up toward the glass waiting for an order to divert a stream, Vane stood motionless. To send a single drop down a trench would have corrupted the examination; he existed to render the estate legible, never to govern its hands.

At the first bell, Vane descended into the cellar where forty-eight earthenware jars stood in alcoves along the wall. Each vessel held a volatile essence, and each bore a pasted parchment strip naming its provenance and grade. Vane walked the line with his lantern and chalk, sorting the forty-eight titles into families—those of the upper spring, the salt marsh, the night cistern—until every vessel had been assigned its rank in the hierarchy of names. Yet he never broke a wax seal, never lifted a stopper, never dipped a reed to see whether the clay behind the parchment was brimming or dry. The discipline of the cellar required that the inspector know every title and remain ignorant of the substance; to expose the liquid inside would violate the secrecy of the house, whereas classifying the outer name was enough to bind each vessel to the archive.

Returning to the gallery, he turned his spyglass upon the long stone wall where the estate's basins had been bolted into the masonry. To an untrained eye, the wall appeared richly provisioned: rows of iron brackets and carved troughs gleamed in the sun, promising abundance. Vane's gaze stripped away that theater of capacity. Of all the basins fixed to the stone, only six were actually joined by leaden conduits to the hillside reservoir; through those six alone did water travel. Beside them hung other troughs, solidly bracketed to the wall and polished by the masons, yet ending in blind stone with no pipe behind their spouts—ornaments of readiness that carried nothing because no channel had been cut to meet them. Farther down the terrace lay an older culvert, once the swiftest cataract on the estate; its bed was now fissured and bone-dry, choked with gray rubble. Vane did not summon masons to clear it. He simply drew a black cross beside its name on his slate, fixing it in the permanent category of the dead.

Across the center of the rotunda stood a ring of glass cylinders, each governed not by the sun's circuit from dawn to midnight, but by a sliding collar that measured only the last five hours of flow. As water ran through the six live channels below, floats rose and fell, tracing on smoked paper how rapidly each five-hour allowance was sinking toward exhaustion. Before completing his fifth duty—placing a standard weight upon his own brass scales to verify that the observer's instruments had not drifted out of true—Vane read the decree nailed above the doorway by the lord of the estate. In older principalities, a steward was punished for an empty storehouse; here, the norm was inverted. The lord had written that he would rather see the cisterns drained down to wet mud before the fifth hour expired than discover a single barrel left standing untouched upon the shelf. Hoarding was the sole transgression. The five-hour glass did not exist to restrain thirst, but to expose the scandal of unspent water—to indict whatever remained idle in the dark when the fields could have drunk it dry.

At noon, Vane carried his marked sheet to the outer colonnade and pinned it to the cedar board where the Council of the Aqueducts gathered in their wool robes. The councilors never descended to the trenches; they governed the estate's thirst by reading Vane's table of the forty-eight titles, the six flowing spouts, the blind brackets, the dead cataract, and the five-hour curve of depletion. Standing back in the shadow of the colonnade while the magistrates bent over his ink, Vane understood that the sheet disciplined him as mercilessly as it disciplined the pipes. What his categories named became the only water the council could see; what his five permitted gestures could not measure remained unsayable within the law of the estate.

# The Five-Hour Glass

The town stood at the bottom of a dry valley, and its water arrived from far away. In the middle of the town was the water yard, and at the back of the yard stood the counter's house, which had one wall with no opening in it at all — no letter-slot, no bell rope, no knocker. Nothing came to him that he did not go out and meet. Twice a day he made his round at the same hours, whether or not anyone was waiting, and those who needed him learned to stand where the round passed, because there was no other way to be found.

His round had five shapes and no more. He walked the yard. He counted. He told the elders what he had counted. He stood before the great glass. And on the last evening of the week he filled his own bucket at the spigot and weighed it in his hands, to know that the bucket and the hands still agreed. When the dry spring came and Sela asked him to drive the north water up to her terraces, he heard her out and shook his head. "I can tell you what runs and what does not," he said. "Sending is not one of my shapes." No one in the valley had ever seen him touch a sluice.

He carried a book in a greasy cloth cover. In it were forty-eight springs, each under its name, and under each name a mark of what sort of name it was — deep, seasonal, mineral, sweet. He had not tasted one of them. Six channels were joined to the yard and running; he tapped their spigots each round and listened. Along the north wall lay the mounted pipes, fitted and sealed and strapped to their trestles, joined to nothing at either end. At the bottom of the book one name stood with a cross beside it, its channel broken past mending; it stayed in the book, because a name once counted is never dropped, only crossed.

In the tower above the yard hung the glass. It ran five hours from crown to crown and was turned the instant it emptied, at whatever hour that was, in dark or daylight, so that it never began at dawn and never ended at dusk; it only ever showed the last five hours of drinking. The allowance was read off the falling level, and the town had learned to read it. Thirst, which had once been a feeling in the throat, had become a position of sand. Farmers came to the yard not when they were dry but when the level said so, and went away content if the sand permitted, even with their tongues dusty.

The master of the works came at evening with a stick and knocked over any jar that still held water. Better, he said, that the cisterns stand empty at moonrise than full at morning. Every drop brought into the valley was to leave it again by dark — into the fields, into the vats, into the gutters of the houses. The town ran dry most nights, and this was called right, and the master slept well. Nothing stood on a high shelf long enough to grow old.

Once a season the water committee of the council met under the fig tree, seven elders who had never walked to the springs. They received the counter's telling, read the falling level, argued over the crossed name, and set the size of the next day's allowance. They knew the water as a book, a level of sand, and a telling, and they governed it so, and the valley throve or dried according to how well those shapes fit the far springs none of them had seen.

Sela went out once, against the season, and walked the three days to the deep spring whose name was marked sweet. She found it loud and cold and wider than its mark. She put her hand in and held it there a long while, and the water was colder than any line in the book, and no one had written that down. The day she came home the glass was being turned again at noon, the master was tipping the last jars, and the counter stood in the yard weighing his bucket. She went and stood where his round passed, and waited, because that was the only place a thing could be said.

# The Counter at the Cistern

The houses on the ridge shared one cistern and, for a generation, one quarrel about it. The quarrel quieted when the great table appointed a counter. He asked for nothing but a hut at the cistern's edge, and they built it with a bench, a bell, a slate, and a glass as wide as a churn. On the door they nailed a card with his duties: count, ring, tell, show, prove. He read it twice in the doorway and then went in, and in all the years after, nobody could name a single order he had given. When the delegates once asked him to command the ridge's drawing, he turned the card toward them and said there was no such word on it.

His work was done in the morning light. On the long shelves stood forty-eight jars, and on each jar a word was painted — RAIN, RIDGE, EASTFIELD, down the row. He copied the words into his book, sorted them into kinds, and marked the ones some earlier hand had scratched out and renamed. He never lifted a lid. A delegate once asked what the EASTFIELD jar held, and he said he had no idea, and that his book was not about that. She went down the hill insulted, and he went on copying words.

Six jars were plumbed, copper running from their mouths down to the houses. The rest stood on brackets, dry-mouthed, waiting on pipes that had never been laid. The northern pipe was the strange one: it was laid, it was sound, and nothing had ever come through it. The counter crossed its jar with chalk and wrote nothing beside the cross.

The glass was his clock. It emptied over five hours, and when the last sand fell he turned it, rubbed the slate clean, and wrote the allowances for the next turn — not what each house was owed across the year, but what the last five hours of drawing left behind. A house that had drawn heavily in the morning found a smaller figure at noon. A house that had held back found a larger one. Nothing older than the glass stood on the slate, and this was the part of the work the ridge found hardest to love.

On the first morning of each month he proved himself: rang the bell, turned the glass, spoke his own figures back to an empty bench, and corrected the one he got wrong. That was the whole of it.

The woman who kept the estate came up the path each week to read the slate. She had a saying she gave to anyone who would stand still for it: better the cistern ran to mud than that a jar should stand full and unused. Under her word the allowances ran generous, and more than once the last turn of the glass was written against water that was nearly gone. The counter wrote the figures she liked, and rang the bell a little differently when the shelves went low. The bell was his to ring, and nothing else was.

At the great table, where the council of the ridge met over its long businesses, a small table stood apart with three chairs, called the table for water. The counter walked down once a season to stand at it and tell what had been drawn, what had stood, what had gone to mud. He told amounts. He told turns of the glass. The delegates nodded and wrote amounts in their own books.

What he never told them was what happened at the bench, because the bench had a life of its own. The farmer from EASTFIELD read the slate as if it were a command and drew exactly to the spoon. The carter read it, spat, and drew to the spoon in the morning and a little over in the afternoon, because his horses were his horses; he kept the figure and bent its edges. And one season a newcomer walked over from beyond the ridge, read the slate a long moment, and said to nobody, "Your glass is not my thirst." He drew by his own measure, paid for what he took, and went home. The farmer called him a thief, the carter called him a fool, and the newcomer called the slate a habit.

None of it reached the great table. The hut had no letter-slot, no hatch, no hole; nothing at all passed through its walls. Whoever wanted to know walked up the path, stood at the bench, and made what they could of the slate, and what they made of it went home with them and died there. The counter, who had built a life on reading words without opening jars, never noticed that the ridge was doing exactly the same to him.

The hut still stands at the cistern's edge. The glass turns. The chalk cross is still on the northern jar.

# The Steward with No Bucket

When the waterhouse lost its old master, the valley council chartered a steward instead of appointing one. The charter fit on a single page and hung above the bench by the door. It gave Elias five acts and struck out the sixth. He could count the vessels. He could walk the yard at first light and feel its pulse. He could stand at the long table and say what he had found. He could keep watch on the great glass. He could fill a basin and try his own eyes and hands against it. Pouring was not forgotten but struck out, and the eldest said why: a man who pours will soon lie about the count. They gave him no bucket and no key to the taps. He took the bench, having asked for neither.

There were forty-eight vessels on the long shelf, and his first work was their names. He read every label and sorted the names into kinds, and the rule of the work was that no jar was ever opened. What a vessel held was its own business; the house needed its name and its place. Six of the names belonged to springs whose water truly arrived — six pipes came down out of the hills and ran, cold and audible, into the cistern. One of the six, the deep spring the old master had loved, had gone dry two winters before and stood full of air; beside its name in the roll Elias set the driest word he knew. A row of other jars stood stoppered in their niches, filled and waiting for pipes that had never been laid. He counted these as gaps. When farmers pointed to them and called them wealth, he said what they were, once.

At the center of the waterhouse stood a glass five hours tall. It drained from the moment it was turned, and when it ran out it was turned again, and with each turning the slate beside it was wiped and the standing measures written new — this hour so many ladles for the north field, that hour so many for the mill — so the house never held more than five hours of its own allowance at a time. Elias did not own the glass and could not slow it. His whole office toward it was to look, and to tell an asker what it said, even when it said little.

Above the shelf hung the master's standing order, cut into wood: the house would rather come to its end than keep a full shelf. Every vessel was meant to go out. A jar that stood full through a whole season was carried to the fair and shown around, and its owner stood as quiet as a man being read to.

Two brothers argued in the yard on every market day. The elder said the ration was a wall and the valley should walk out — over the ridge to the river, where a man draws his own water and owes nobody a glass. The younger said every place has a glass, a wall is only the world, and a man plays the game he wakes up in. The game was the evening ladle: at the shutting of each hour the leftover ladle went to whoever had waited longest at the gate, and the waiting ate the first work of a man's own hour, and there were always more waiters than ladles, and everyone knew it, and waited anyway.

The yard kept a habit older than the charter. At dusk the farmers judged the fullness of the house by the shadows the shelf threw across the floor. The lamp sat low, the shadows ran long, and a long shadow meant a full jar.

Before answering the brothers, Elias spent one whole day doing nothing but watching where the shadows fell. At dusk the shelf threw its longest shade and the yard went home content; at noon the same jars cast almost nothing, and the same men frowned at the same water. He counted what he saw and judged nothing.

Then he did the one thing his charter allowed, which turned out to be the thing. He stood at the long table before the elders of the water — a small standing circle that met on water and sent even a question about the bridge up to the larger council — and he said: the deep spring is dead, and its name stays on the roll so that anyone who reads the roll asks what it was; the stoppered jars are gaps and not wealth; the glass gives five hours and turns; the evening ladle has cost the yard more than it gave since before the charter was written. Nothing in the yard changed shape. No pipe was laid, no tap opened, no rule repealed. But after the saying the farmers stopped reading the shelf at dusk, because they had heard the water counted by daylight. And no one came to the evening gate. The game was not forbidden, and it was not won. It had been measured out loud by a man with no bucket, and after that no one chose it.

The elder brother still means to leave for the river, but he will go with the count in his pocket instead of his anger, and some noons he stands at the lamp checking the jars himself. The younger shuts the gate early and goes home to his first work. The glass turns. The full shelf grows shorter every season. The house means to arrive at its end used.

# The Counting House of the Forty-Eight Names

The valley took its water from the mountain by forty-eight named channels, and it kept a counting house beside the main sluice to prove it. The keeper of the counting house was called Marren, and his charter was short. He might go out at dawn and read the names. He might strike the bell on the hour. He might stand at his door in the evening and say what was. He might post, on the pillar by the well, the hours at which each street's gate would stand open. And once a week he might turn his own instruments on themselves, pouring his quart into his own gauge to see whether the gauge agreed. That was the whole of it. No horn hung on his wall, no runner waited at his door, and no key to any gate was ever put into his hand.

The townsfolk thought this poverty and said so. In the flood year they crowded to the counting house crying for the gates to be shut, and Marren climbed the roof and called back only what he saw — the water at the second step, the water at the seventh — while the sluice-wardens, who kept the keys and answered to the committee, came down in their own time and did what they did. No hand was hurried by anything Marren saw. But when the water was disputed afterward at the committee table, it was his saying-what-was that both sides asked to hear, and not the wardens' deeds.

At dawn he read the names. Forty-eight, cut into brass tags on the long board, each the name of a channel: the six that carried into the cistern yard he touched in order; the spouts bolted to the plaza wall in the founder's grandfather's time — mouths toward the street, plaster behind them, no pipe of any kind — he read also; then the long tail of names that were promises only, springs heard of and never plumbed. The tags gave names, not natures; the seals behind them had never once been broken, and the charter did not ask it. Last he read the east channel, which had been the strongest, and touched its tag with ash, for the mountain above its source had fallen and it had carried nothing since. The town had never taken the tag down. The hollow spouts had been bolted up so that the plaza would look like a place water came to; the dead name was kept for the opposite reason, because the town remembered. Between them the board was a catalogue of what the valley wanted, sorted by whether it was wet.

What the town drew, it drew against the wax. Each bucket went down as a mark pressed into the warm wax of the counting house, and the wax was kept warm on purpose: as the five-hour glass on the sill turned, the earliest marks leveled out and were gone, and no thirst older than five hours was held against anyone. At the well the numbers passed from mouth to mouth all morning, each man exact about his neighbor's drawing and exact about his own. Knowing was the rarest thing in the valley after water, and cheaper, and the crowd at the morning count was thicker than the crowd at the gates.

The founder's law stood cut over the cistern gate: EMPTY BEFORE MORNING. Whatever the day left, the wardens poured to the fields at dusk rather than carry it over, and they said the founder's name when they poured. A valley that ended its days dry, they held, was a valley that had wanted nothing it did not use; stored water was water left where a thief could find it. Marren counted the pouring out like everything else.

The committee for water met on the second day of the month. There were committees for the roads and for the dogs, and their names were on the door of the hall, but the water committee was the one that gathered. The others had long ago become names.

One spring the mountain ran fat. Every gate stood open all day and every jar in the valley was filled by noon, and that day no one came to hear the count. The wax wanted no tending, since nobody was drawing against anybody; the bell went unanswered; the committee, it was noticed afterward, did not meet. The knowing stood idle in the square, and the valley was not poorer for it, and not one dry spout on the plaza wall was any wetter.

Marren struck the hour all the same. In the morning he went out and read the forty-eight names and touched the ash mark last, to no one, and the count was complete.

# The Dry Pavilion of the Sluices

High above the terraced orchards stood the octagonal pavilion where the bailiff, Toma, tended the brass index of the irrigation works. Earlier bailiffs had walked the ditches bare-legged, knowing the temper of the mountain snowmelt by the ache in their shins and the gravel-roar in their ears. Once the brass index was bolted to the cedar table, Toma never stepped into the mud; the instrument extended his eye across the valley and, in the way of all such vessels, numbed his heels and stopped his ears. No pipe entered the pavilion, no wire ran from its casing to the oak headgates below, and no lever on its face could lift a weir or release a single drop into the furrows. Its whole frame allowed only five motions of the hand: a notched wheel for calling the roll of the cellar casks, a reed that trembled with the pulse of the hill-springs, a horn through which Toma spoke the morning summary, a rack of five-hour sandglasses, and a small ivory striker with which he tapped the brass ribs to hear whether the instrument itself had cracked.

Each dawn Toma turned the notched wheel and descended with his chalk into the vaulted cellar where forty-eight stoneware amphorae stood in four cold ranks. Every vessel wore a clay collar stamped with the title of the orchard plot or vintage it was meant to feed, and every mouth was sealed with thick red pitch. Toma walked the ranks, reading the stamped collars aloud and sorting the forty-eight names into chalked columns on the masonry—some marked for the high terraces, some for the presses, some for the lower arbors. He never pried up a stopper, never dipped a reed into a neck, and never shook a belly to hear whether wine, spring-water, or only stale air lay beneath the wax. The collar-mark alone satisfied the wheel upstairs, and so a man trained by the wheel grew blind to the weight of the jar.

Along the south ridge, bronze troughs had been bracketed into the cliffside to carry water from the main mountain flume to the outer groves. Only six of those troughs were actually collared into the flume so that live water raced through them and turned the mill-paddles below. A dozen others hung gleaming on their iron hooks, bolted fast to the rock yet stopping a hand's-breadth short of the main stream, their mouths open to the wind while spiders spun across their dry throats. Further down the ravine lay the old lightning-chute, once the swiftest raceway on the estate, which used to hurl water down a polished basalt groove in the blink of an eye; a winter frost had split its bed from crown to heel, and now weed-stalks rattled in its dry trench while the bailiff's horn named it dead every morning without sending a mason to mend it.

Back in the pavilion, Toma's gaze rarely left the wooden frame where five-hour sandglasses turned on a sliding track. The glasses did not measure the depth of the mountain tarn, nor did they recall what had flowed the week before; each bulb showed only how much of the current five-hour allowance remained before the sand ran out and the track slid a fresh glass into place. Watching that falling thread of white grit remade Toma's sense of time. The long seasons of rain and drought vanished for him, replaced by a perpetual five-hour twilight in which the only urgency was the narrowing waist of the dust inside the crystal.

At midday a rider came up from the master of the vineyard bearing a cedar tablet carved with the rule of the estate. The master forbade any bailiff to finish a five-hour glass with water still standing in the holding basins; he had sworn that he would rather see every trough sucked dry and the ditch-men standing idle under a parched sky before the fifth hour ended than find a single unpoured measure left resting in the cool stone dark when the glass turned. Because Toma still had no cord or crank to open the headgates himself, he could only lean over the sill and blow his horn down the slope, shouting the master's decree to the ditch-men until they pried open the six live sluices and let the water roar wide over the roots.

Every seventh evening, the elders of the Council on the Watershed climbed the dry stair to deliberate around the cedar table. They never paused by the six roaring troughs, nor looked at the unjoined brackets on the cliff, nor laid a hand on the forty-eight sealed amphorae in the cellar below. They sat in a circle around the brass index, listening to its reed hum and watching the five-hour sand sink in the bulb, governing the streams in the dry, measured cadence that a room without water teaches to those who gaze upon its dial.

# The Warden Who Never Tasted

When the long droughts came to the hill estate, the house stopped trusting rain and kept its water in a cellar of jars, forty-eight of them standing shoulder to shoulder in the dark. The mistress of the house appointed a warden over the cellar, and at the appointing she took away every tool that gives: no ladle, no bucket, no key to the stopcocks. "Your hands are for knowing," she said, "not for giving." The warden accepted the post as one accepts weather, and went down to the shelves.

Each morning she walked the rows with a wax tablet, reading the labels aloud — sharp water, sweet water, black water, water from beyond the mountains — sorted the names into ranks, and wrote the ranks down. In all her years she never lifted a lid. The lifting of lids belonged to the tasters, and the tasters' room had been locked since before her appointment; who kept the key, no one would say.

Six jars alone stood over basins, with clay pipes running up to the kitchen, and these six gave water when the cook knocked twice at their stopcocks. Among the other forty-two, a few carried pipes already luted to the cellar wall, ending in cold stone: the clay had been laid, but no water had ever agreed to walk it. The cook wiped those pipes with her apron out of habit and called them nothing at all.

At the head of the shelf stood the tallest jar, lettered with the name of a deep spring. The spring had died years before; the jar stood empty, and perhaps had always stood empty. No one moved it. It was dusted each evening, and the warden counted it first every morning, saying its name with all the rest.

Beside each of the six pouring jars the warden kept an hourglass of five hours' sand. When a glass ran out she turned it, and it began again; no glass was retired, and no spent hour was ever given back. She could tell the cook, to the grain, how much running room any kitchen hour had left, and the cook planned her soups by the falling sand.

On the hour the warden struck a small drum once, so the house knew the counting still walked. At dusk she stood in the kitchen and read her tablet aloud: which names stood, which six poured, how the sand stood in every glass. On the last night of each month she walked the whole round with her eyes bound, to prove her hands still knew the five motions without the water's help.

Once, before a feast, the cook took the warden by the wrist and begged her to open the fortieth jar, the one with the sweetest label. The warden showed her palms. "Opening was never put into me," she said. "I can tell you its name, its rank, its shelf, and its hour. Not one drop further." So the cook climbed to the council, for the house was governed by delegates from every room, and among their tables one met about water alone; nothing in the house poured except by its raised hand. The warden could stand before that table and read. The table could command. Between the reading and the command lay a night, sometimes a season.

The mistress's law was said each quarter and carved over the cellar stair: the house would rather drink its last jar dry and go thirsty into spring than let one sealed jar stand unused on its shelf while anyone thirsted. For this reason, when the old six ran low, the cook knocked at new stopcocks, and the warden's column of untasted names was read out like a page of debts.

In the drought year two of the six ran to sand together, and the delegates sat late. At last one asked the warden whether the forty-two held water or only held their labels. She read the ranks, the shelves, the hours; then she said, quietly, "I was made to know the names. No one is made to know the contents." The table sat with that. At the head of the shelf the dead spring caught the lamplight and gave it back polished.

Some nights the warden dreamed of lifting one lid — the fortieth, the sweetest name — and in the dream she drank without spilling. She woke with a dry mouth, went down at dawn, counted, and said the dead jar's name first.

# The Warder of Water

Maren was appointed to watch the water of the estate, and on the morning she took office the steward gave her a staff, a book, a glass of five hours, and nothing else. She asked for the key to the sluice-house. There was no key. She asked which valves would answer her word. None, said the steward; the pipes of this house open for no one's word, least of all yours. Her office was old, and it had been built on purpose without hands.

Each morning she walked the long shelf where forty-eight jars stood in ranks, each with its label turned outward, and she copied the labels into her book, exact to the letter. She never pulled a stopper from any of them. Once a young servant lifted the wax on the ninth jar to look inside, and Maren took his wrist and wrote: the ninth jar was touched. What the jar held, the book did not say, because there was no line in it for that. The estate knew its vessels by their names, and it seemed to prefer knowing them that way.

Then she walked the channels. Six aqueducts carried water into the house that season, and she measured each at its mouth and wrote the number. Along the south wall ran four more, fully built, stone joined to stone, carrying nothing — mounted in a dry year and never joined to any source. Another channel, in the north range, had carried well once; the town it drew from had emptied out, and now it was a groove of dead stone. Maren walked it all the same. When the council's man asked why she walked a channel that could not flow, she said: because it is on the roll, and the roll is what I keep.

Of any channel she could give three answers, and the fourth was forbidden. This one flows. This one does not. This one I have not seen flow, nor seen it fail. Probably was not in her book, and she would not say it with her mouth.

Her round kept time with the mill-bell. At every stroke she stood at one of the appointed marks, neither early nor late, and the servants set their work by her passing the way they set it by the bell itself. The round ended at the cisterns. Each was shut under a lid with a narrow window cut in it, and through the window she read the marks painted on the inside wall, high to low. She had turned the glass of five hours at her first step; as it drained she read the marks again, and what had been deep at one turning stood shallow at the next; what was spent came back only after its own slow rain. She kept the marks faithfully, even for the cisterns nobody drew from.

The master of the estate kept one law about water, and he said it at table where all could hear: better a cistern drunk to the mud than a cistern standing full; a full cistern standing is a debt, and stagnant water is a shame. The servants learned to draw deeply and without fear of the dry season. Guests called the law extravagant. The master said he would rather meet the drought with an empty house and a clean conscience than with a full one and water gone bad on the shelf.

For the governing of water, the council kept a small standing table that met after her rounds. Its three members did not dig and did not lay stone; they had never touched a jar. They read her book aloud, and where it said flows they nodded, and where it said does not they shook their heads, and where it said not seen they looked at one another and passed on. In this way the house knew its water. No one went out to the channels after her. It was not laziness; the book was the thing read, and the water was the thing the book was about.

Once a season, Maren was examined. She sat in the yard before the assembled servants with her glass, her book, and her staff, turned the glass to see whether it still drained in five hours, counted a shelf she had counted before to see whether the count still came, and read a page of her own book aloud to see whether the words still held. The servants watched until she was done. Nothing flowed because of her that day, and nothing stopped, and the house counted her faithful. The watcher proved her instruments, and the instruments proved her, and neither was trusted without the other.

One evening the young servant who had touched the ninth jar asked her what was inside the jars. She said she did not know. He asked whether the house would want to know. She thought about it honestly and said that the book had no page for the question — it was not that the answer was hidden; it was that the question had never been ruled in, and there was no way to write it that the table would read. After that they stood together a while and looked at the forty-eight labels, turned outward, in the lamplight.

# The Five-Hour Glass

In the last of the dry years a woman who built channels in her own country came to a hill town that lived entirely by its water. She had heard that in this town the water was governed, and she climbed the irrigation stair to see the one who governed it, because at home the water governed them.

"The keeper is in the round tower," the almond farmers told her, "but you will not see him touch water. No one ever has."

The tower stood at the head of the stair. Inside were a table, a board hung with small wooden tags, a drum, a glass five hours long, and a cabinet of five little panes; nothing else. No channel entered the room, no rope, no pipe; water could not reach him and he could not reach water. He was an old man with ink at his cuffs, called Perrin by the town, and he received her as if she were a problem he already liked.

At dawn he worked the board. Forty-eight tags hung on it, each bearing a name — the Sweet Widow, the Twins, the Gray Spring, the Far Fount, the Deep Mother, and forty-three others. He read the forty-eight names aloud and sorted each tag into its kind: sweet, mineral, far, seasonal, deep. On a shelf beside the board stood sealed jars, one for every name. When she asked what the third jar held, he said, "A name and a kind. I have never broken a seal. More than that would be inventing."

From the balcony she counted what actually ran: six threads of water down the hill, no more. Beside them stood a stone channel, finished and finely jointed, that arrived at the town wall and stopped, its far end open to the air and joined to nothing. "Built in the good years," said Perrin. "It hangs in its kind with the others, and it carries nothing, and I say so." The Deep Mother, greatest of them all, had one neat stroke through its name. "Dead in my grandfather's year. The tag stays. A dead spring is a fact, and facts do not leave the board."

Every hour he beat once on the drum, and below him the clock-keeper set the town by it. Every hour he opened his five panes one by one — behind each sat a slate with the tally of one hour's drawing — then scraped the oldest slate blank, moved it to the end of the row, and wrote the newest hour upon it. She asked how much the town had drawn that season. "Five hours," he said. "Beyond the glass I do not know, and I will not guess aloud."

At noon he stood at the gate and told the farmers, in a flat voice, exactly what he had counted, and they went away to their gates.

She asked him then to send more water to the lower terraces, where her host's young trees were dying. He brightened. "Madam. I count, I beat the hour, I tell, I open my panes, and at dusk I prove myself. There is no sixth act in me. The handle of your host's gate is in his hand. I have never sent water anywhere, and if I began, the town would come up this stair and take the drum away."

At dusk he proved himself. He took the day's largest error — the Twins misplaced among the far — and looked for the smaller error inside it, and the smaller inside that, until he reached one too small to halve, and that one he wrote in the margin of the board. In the same margin, in older ink: I have found the true order by which every spring in this country may be sorted, and this board is too narrow to hold it. "And did you find it?" she said. "The record says I wrote it," said Perrin. "Whether I hold it, the record does not say."

Down on the terraces the farmers were drawing freely, spending like a mild winter. In a dry year it looked like ruin, and she said so to an old man at the cistern. "Our master left one saying," he answered. "He would rather come home and find everything run dry from drawing than stand full from saving. Water that is never drawn, he told us, is already lost."

In the evening the assembly met: benches for the walls, for the roads, for grain, for disputes. Apart from them stood one small table of darker wood, older than the benches, where three elders sat, and only water was spoken of there; the reeve with his wall business was made to carry it elsewhere. That night Perrin's letters went out, as they did each week, to the mill-keeper of the wet low country; their winter sport was dividing an unfinished season between two channels before the rain ended it.

When she left, he gave her no order and no water, only the day's five slates copied fair for the road, and a challenge: count your springs at home without tasting one, and if your judgments do not improve, come back and say so, and I will strike my own board myself.

# The Five-Hour Glass

Each dawn Cassian climbed the outside stair into the round watchtower above the terraced groves and barred the oak door behind him. The masons who raised the tower had mortared every arrow-slit shut on the side facing the dam and left no pipe, chain, pulley, or speaking-horn passing through the stone. Though the valley called him warden of the weir, his room possessed no coupling to the waterworks below; he could neither lift a sluice-hatch nor turn a spigot by a fingernail's breadth. Across his cedar table lay a brass rod notched with the only five duties permitted to his office: he was to walk the cellar and sort the titles painted on the clay jars, strike the bronze gong at the turn of the hour, read his parchment roll aloud from the parapet, invert the five-hour sandglasses along the sill, and drop a lead plumb-line from the rafters to prove his own measuring-table stood level. Nowhere on the brass rod was there a sixth notch empowering him to release a single drop into the furrows. That severance between the tower and the hatches was the one restraint that had earned its keep, for an apparatus cut off from the weir cannot open it in secret.

At first light he carried his charcoal stick down the cellar steps to perform the first notch of the rod. Forty-eight tall amphorae stood in four ranks along the flagstones, each vessel bearing a painted title across its neck. Cassian walked the rows and copied all forty-eight titles onto his roll, grouping them by their inscriptions into neat families: jars named for the upper olive press, jars named for the southern vineyard, jars named for the orchard troughs, and jars named for the bakehouse. When a young ditch-digger asked whether the clay held springwater, sour wine, or only air, Cassian did not pry up a single wax stopper. His office was to certify that forty-eight titles stood upon forty-eight necks; peering inside the vessel was forbidden. So long as the wax stayed whole, the store could not be pilfered by the man who counted it, yet the roll grew heavy with the certainty of a supply nobody had verified.

Returning to the parapet, Cassian surveyed the limestone terraces where the conduits crossed the slope. Six stone channels were mortared fast to the upper basin and carried a steady stream down to the root-beds. Beside them, four timber flumes stood bolted upon tall masonry piers, leveled at great cost, yet ending in mid-air three paces short of the springhead and three paces short of the ditches. Having been raised, they required Cassian to record their gap every morning, so that the ritual of counting them justified their standing useless in the sun. Farther west, the steep basalt chute that had once hurled water down the crag faster than any course on the estate lay cracked from rim to bed. Dry bindweed choked its throat; each day Cassian wrote *dead* beside its name, and each day the roll required him to inspect the same dry stone as though measurement might revive it.

Along the eastern sill stood a frame of five-hour sandglasses, turned in a continuous sliding reckoning so that the falling sand charted how much of each five-hour water allotment remained in the upper pond. Below the frame, bolted to the lintel by the lord of the estate, hung an iron tablet hammered with a single maxim: *Better the pond be drained to dry mud before sundown than a single dipper remain standing in the dark.* Once the five-hour glasses began their rolling measure above that tablet, the sand ceased to describe the water and began to command it. Whenever the red grains ran ahead of the mark on the basin wall, the stewards panicked lest the fifth hour close with water unspent; they opened the six mortared channels during rainstorms and poured the reserve into gullies already sodden, drowning the lower fig trees so the five-hour reckoning would come out even. The instrument built to prevent waste had made exhaustion mandatory, and no one on the hillside asked who had decided that drowned roots were preferable to a full pond.

At dusk seven elders in bleached mantles—the Assembly of the Wellhead—took their benches in the courtyard to hear the third duty of the rod. Cassian leaned over the coping and read his roll: the forty-eight sorted titles over sealed jars, the six flowing channels, the four raised flumes joined to nothing, the dead basalt chute, and the five-hour glasses drained in obedience to the iron tablet. The elders asked only whether the plumb-line had hung straight at noon. Hearing that the tower's own beam was level, they declared the water well ordered and departed, while Cassian stepped back within his severing walls, turned the next glass, and struck the bronze gong for an hour that needed no water and would allow none to be spared.

# The Water Warden

The waterworks of the hill estate stood behind a round tower, and in the tower lived the Warden, whose whole appointment was to keep the count of the waters. The tower had been built without a door: walls, a roof, a smoke-vent, and no opening wide enough for a letter. Men who wanted something from him stood below and shouted, and the rain ran their words down the gutter. In thirty years nothing handed up had reached him, because nothing could be handed up at all.

At first light he walked the gallery where the estate's forty-eight channels were cut into the wall, each mouth under a plate bearing a name and a kind. He copied the words from the plates — spring, rain, sweet, mineral, brown — and sorted the mouths by them. He never lifted a cover, never dipped a finger, never put his ear to a spout. When the merchant who owned the ninth mouth swore that his water ran sweet, the Warden wrote "sweet, so says the plate," because the plate was all he read.

Mid-morning he struck the bronze bowl that hung by the stair, once every hundred breaths, and the estate set itself by the sound — loaves drawn on the stroke, pages turned, guards changed at the tower's foot. At dusk he stood on the walk and gave the telling: how many mouths, how many flowed, what the bowls showed. On the sixth day he tested nothing but his own instruments — filled his cup to the line, read it back, climbed his rope — and marked that his eyes and hands still agreed.

He owned no crank. When the granary fire came in the dry summer, the crowd gathered below and begged him to open the great channel and send the north water over the roofs. He came out, showed his two empty hands, and told them what the bowls said. The valves of the estate were turned by other men in other yards, or by no one. Water went where it went without ever passing through him.

Six of the forty-eight flowed; that was in every telling. Nine more were fastened into the wall with good iron, paid for by the foot in a prosperous year, and joined to nothing — walk behind the plaster, as the Warden once had, and the bright pipe ends in cold stone. The first mouth, the oldest, whose plate the founder's family came each spring to polish, ran dry nine years ago when its spring failed. The plate still promised abundance, and the mapmaker's chart drew the north water in confident blue all the way to the fountain in the square. The family's name stayed on the gate, the chart sold for a silver, and the fountain had long since been tiled over and planted with thyme.

On the shelf above his hearth stood a row of thin bronze bowls, pierced at the bottom, fed drop by drop from the cistern above. Each emptied itself in five hours and began again. The height of water in a bowl was the estate's permission: while the level stood, water could be drawn; as it fell, the allowance fell with it; when it emptied, a new measure started and the old one was gone as though never poured. Ask him what had been drawn at breakfast and, five hours later, he could only point at the bowls and say they held the whole of what anyone was permitted to know.

The Master of the estate kept one saying about water and gave it at every feast: better the cisterns run empty than a drop fall asleep in a tank. Each evening the gardeners poured the day's remainder over the lawns, so that no water should lie saved while the estate slept. A dry cistern at midnight was a day honestly spent; a full one was counted as theft from a future no one was permitted to save for.

The council kept a bench of three elders for the water alone. They sat below the tower at dusk, heard the telling, and copied the numbers into the town's book; they held no cranks either. In his last year the Warden's apprentice asked for the bench's evening and gave the long telling instead of the short one: the dry first mouth, the nine ending in stone, the bowls that forgot anything older than five hours, the two empty hands. He spoke for the length of a meal. The elders thanked him, and at the next dusk asked for the short telling, the one the town could carry home. The long one was true, and was never asked for again. The page with the short numbers is worn soft from touching; the page beside it, where the apprentice's count was copied, has kept its edges.

# The Steward of the Sealed Cistern

Upstairs in the bright hall, a gilt portrait of the Water Governor hung above the dry washbasin, painted with a great brass wrench in his fist. Yet because the kitchen troughs remained parched at noon, Clara took a lantern and went down the cellar steps beneath the floorboards, where the house kept what it refused to look at in the light. The vault she entered was built of seamless granite without a single outer culvert, drain, or conduit piercing its foundation walls; nothing entered from the hills, and nothing left by any hidden channel. In the center of the cold floor stood the Governor in a dark wool coat. Before him rose the iron spindle of the main sluice, hammered smooth and stripped of any wheel or lever. He could not open a gate or send a single drop toward the scullery, for the spindle had been forged without a handle from the start.

When Clara asked what duty kept him in the vault, the Governor silently performed his five appointed tasks. First, he walked the stone racks with a piece of chalk, counting the vessels and sorting them into groups by their painted titles. Second, he pressed a wooden ear-horn against the flagstones to time the slow, rhythmic pulse of the cisterns underneath. Third, he wrote a neat summary of the vault's condition upon a slate for the upper stairs. Fourth, he climbed three steps to read the sliding brass frame that measured the five-hour ration spans. Fifth, he struck an iron fork against his plumb-line and tested the balance of his own horn and chalk to verify that his instruments were unbroken. Having completed the fifth task, he folded his hands inside his sleeves. He had been given no sixth motion; the office named to govern the water possessed no means to pour it.

Clara walked to the north rack, where forty-eight stoneware jars stood in classified rows, their necks sealed with thick black wax. The Governor had arranged all forty-eight strictly by the family titles painted across their clay bellies—hearth, table, orchard, reserve—without ever once prying up a stopper. When Clara lifted two jars and felt how light and hollow they were, she asked why he never broke the wax to see what lay inside. He answered that the rule of the cellar permitted him to classify the forty-eight names on the clay, never to inspect or taste the dark interior. Across the aisle, only six yoked water-bearers actually had leather hoses coupled from their casks to the house's rising main. Above them, four heavy oak barrels hung in iron wall-cradles with faded celebration ribbons still tied to their hoops, brimming and wet, yet their brass spigots jutted over bare flagstones with no tubing attached. The builders had rejoiced in hoisting the barrels into the cradles and had walked away before laying the humble pipe to join them to the trough.

At the far end of the rack stood the carved stone mouth of the Swift Spring, once boasted of as the fastest torrent on the estate. Clara reached into its throat and drew back fingers coated in white lime dust; the spring was completely dead, its vein collapsed and bone-dry, yet its polished brass plaque remained on the wall because no one in the house wished to strike a dead source from the roll. High on the landing above them ticked a five-hour water-clock hung over a permanent scroll of ration-spans. Its sliding frame crept across the parchment, tracing a continuous five-hour depletion of the manor's allowance. Beside the stair-tray, a written order in the Master's own hand was nailed into the oak post: he declared that he would far rather see every vessel in the manor drained to dry clay before dusk than discover a single full pitcher left sitting untouched on the ledge.

Clara unpinned the Master's order, took a pinch of white dust from the dead spring, and carried the Governor's slate up into the oak parlor. There the seven elders of the Water Council sat around an empty carafe, convened as the estate's assembly on the supply. They leaned forward to praise the Governor's careful slate and mourn the scarcity of the season. Clara laid the Master's parchment and the dead lime beside their cups, and asked why men who recited the Master's fierce thirst every morning kept forty-eight jars sealed, left four cradled barrels unjoined to the trough, and employed a Governor whose spindle had no wheel. The seven elders flinched and looked away from the portrait on the wall, unable to face the famine they had carefully built with their own hands.

# The Inventory of Springs

They appointed a governor of water in the year the river became a rumor. Not a man to carry it — the town had porters for carrying — a man to know where it was. His house stood on the hill above every channel in the valley, and this was the whole of his power: not one pipe entered it. The water went around him, always. When the plumbers offered to fit him a tap of his own, the town refused; a governor with a tap, they said, would soon govern nothing but the tap. So he lived at the top of the plumbing and touched none of it, and everything that flowed, flowed beneath his window.

He was permitted five occupations, no more, and the children could recite them. He could count. He could sound the drum. He could tell the town what he had counted. He could read the glasses in the kitchen. And once each season he could walk his own house from cellar to roof and confirm that he still owned none of it. When the western farmers came asking him to open the sluice for their field, he came down and counted and drummed and told — and the sluice itself was opened by the ditch-keepers, never by him. Farmers left his door certain that a man who knows everything about water and cannot pour a cup of it is a fraud. The governor did not argue. He wrote in his margin: the fraud is the office.

First he counted keys. Forty-eight brass keys hung on the board in his hall, each engraved with the name of a spring — the Echo, the Twin Falls, the Cold Sisters, the Deep Engine. He copied the names into his book on the first morning and never once turned a key in its lock. Not because it was forbidden: because the name was the whole of the matter. An engraved name tells you a spring exists; what it opens is hearsay, and he had lived too long in the valley to enter hearsay in a book. His apprentice once lifted the Deep Engine's key toward the wall of locks, and the governor took it back gently and said, we count them; we do not wake them.

Then he walked the channels. Six of them ran water, and he could name the six. A seventh was soldered and jointed and ended in a spout of fine brass bolted to a house that had never been built; the boy was sent each week to polish it, because an empty pipe must shine. And the Deep Engine, whose key was the heaviest on the board and whose name came first in the book, had been dry for a year. It kept its name. Names outlast their springs, the governor noted, which is what names are for.

In the kitchen hung the shelf of glasses, each good for five hours. When one ran out it was turned, and the sand ran again, and the level of every glass was written in the slate that the whole valley obeyed. Farmers planted by the slate and let their furrows crack by the slate. A traveler once stood in the market and pointed out that the river ran high while the slate said famine; the town drove him out, not for lying but for casting doubt on the more legible of the two. The river, they explained, does not keep hours.

The master of the valley kept one law above the slate, and it concerned the shelf. Each evening he counted the glasses, and what grieved him was never the sand that had gone; it was any glass that had been spared, still standing full with the day ended. Spared water, he said, is water that never happened. Better a dry valley than a full shelf. The governor recorded the law without comment, which was itself the comment.

Once a month the council met in the town hall to deliberate on a single subject: the water. It had not discussed the river in years. It discussed the book that discussed the river, and its minutes were copied into a second book that travelers came to consult as the most accurate water in the province.

And once each season the governor locked his hall and rehearsed his five offices to no one: he counted the keys aloud, drummed to the empty room, told the walls what he knew, read the glasses to the fire, and walked his cellar to confirm again that no pipe entered. The town heard the drum through the wall and was reassured. The drum certified the governor; the governor certified the count; the count certified the names; and the names, at the end of that chain, certified nothing but themselves. This was the valley's quiet miracle, the governor wrote: no one had seen the water in years, and no one had gone thirsty. Whatever the valley was drinking, it was not water.

# The Chalk on the Cistern Post

A notice pinned to a cedar post does not belong to the hand that drove the nail. In the courtyard of the upper farm, the water-bailiff worked inside a small timber booth raised on four blocks at the center of the gravel. No lead pipe entered that booth, no clay drain ran beneath its floor, and no speaking-tube or bell-wire linked its shutters to the road outside. He could neither draw a bucket himself nor hail a driver beyond the fence. Whatever left his hand left only as chalk marks on a broad sheet of calfskin hung from the cedar post at noon, where different eyes sitting in different chairs around the yard waited to make their own truths out of the same white dust.

All morning the bailiff performed only the five motions permitted to his office. First, he walked the long trestles to tally the vessels by title. Second, he struck the bronze gong once at the turn of each hour to mark the pulse of the yard. Third, he hung his chalked calfskin upon the cedar post. Fourth, he shifted the wooden frame that held five sandglasses along the casement sill. Fifth, before sitting down on his bench, he tested his own brass plumb-weight and measuring reed in a pail of rainwater to prove his instruments had not warped. Beyond those five motions he had no gesture at all: when the mule-carts waited by the arch with full barrels, he had no flag to wave them forward and no command to send them down the hill.

Along the north trestle stood forty-eight tall stoneware crocks, each sealed with red wax and tied at the neck with a painted wooden collar. During his morning tally, the bailiff lifted every collar between thumb and forefinger, copying its painted title into neat columns according to the color of its varnish and the workshop from which it had come. Yet he never cracked a single seal of wax, never tilted a crock to test its weight, and never dipped his reed inside to learn whether the clay held sweet springwater, sour brine, or nothing but summer air. To the encoder at his trestle, a collar properly sorted was a vessel properly known; the interior belonged to another world.

Across the yard at the south trough, six leather hoses were stitched fast to the brass manifold and ran full into the waiting tubs. Beside them, four stout oak casks rested high on their timber cradles, polished and hooped in copper, yet no hose had ever been coupled to their spigots; they sat dry-throated above the splash. At the far corner stood an old red-painted cast pump, its cylinder split by frost long ago and its handle rusted solid against the pin. When the six yoke-haulers sat on the trough rim at noon to decode the bailiff's calfskin on the post, they nodded at the tally of full tubs—for they accepted that the furrows needed pouring—yet they spat into the gravel when they read the column of forty-eight collars, muttering that a steward who counts painted wood while four cradled casks lack leather is measuring pride, not rain.

Above the trough hung a grooved cedar bar carrying five sandglasses, one turned at each stroke of the bronze gong, beside a tallow tablet where the bailiff traced the falling waterline across the last five hours alone, wiping away the sixth hour with his sleeve as though earlier thirst had never been. Over that bar, carved deep into the lintel by the master of the farm, stood the household rule: better the cistern stand bone-dry before the fifth glass runs out than a single dipper of water remain idle on the trestle at dusk. Up in the high gallery, the seven elders of the Circle of the Cistern passed a copy of the noon calfskin from chair to chair and smiled, taking the dominant reading the master had carved into the stone: a vigorous estate spending its store to the last drop. Outside the split-rail fence, however, a widow from the parched lower orchard leaned on her hoe and read the same steep line in opposition; to her chair, a farm that emptied its tubs against the clock while the rusted pump stood broken was not feeding the land, only staging a theater of industry to justify barring the gate.

When the fifth sandglass thinned to a thread late in the afternoon and two tubs still remained unspent, the haulers obeyed the carved lintel and tipped them straight across the gravel so the trestle would stand bare on the stroke of the gong. Clear water ran under the fence into the roadside weeds. Down in the ditch sat an itinerant culvert-mender whom neither the bailiff in his pipe-less booth nor the elders in their gallery ever saw. Watching sweet water soak the nettles at the same hour every afternoon, he decoded the farm neither as industrious nor as greedy, but as a household governed by a superstition against stillness—the one reading no channel in the yard was built to carry back inside.

# The Registrar of Names

When the master of the valley could no longer walk the waterways himself, he did not send for a digger. He sent for a registrar, and the commission he gave had five lines in it: the man would walk, he would knock, he would read, he would speak, and he would rehearse. Nothing in the five lines let him draw, and nothing let him pour. He was given no bucket, no dipper, no key to any sluice, and in all his years on the estate his hands never once touched the water.

Each morning he made his round. There were forty-eight mouths of water on the estate — springs, wells, cisterns, tanks — and at each he stopped, read the name cut into the coping, and wrote the name in his book. That was all he read of them: the names. Whether a spring ran sweet or salt he did not know and was not asked; he had no instrument for tasting and no line in his commission that wanted one. He sorted the names by what the carving promised. Six of the forty-eight truly sent water down the channels when the miller called for them. A dozen more had pipes laid in and lids fitted and paint fresh on the stone, and no water came; he wrote them each morning among the others, in the same even hand. One, the grandest, under a carved arch with iron fittings, lay beneath a black stone; it had gone dead before his commission began. He counted it every morning, and the pipe-fitters still oiled its joints, and no one told them to stop.

As he walked he knocked each coping twice, in the same rhythm. Men had once tapped stone that way to hear whether a shaft was hollow underneath. The estate no longer listened for hollowness. The miller opened his sluice at the sound, whether the wheel wanted water or not; the cook lifted the kettle at the fourth knock and set it back at the fifth. On the day the registrar was kept home by fever, the mill stood until noon with its sluice shut, waiting for a sound that had nothing to do with the river.

Beside the great cistern hung a dark slab on the wall. Every drawing of water was chalked on it, and every mark was washed away five hours after it was made; the registrar kept a sponge for this and wiped nothing before its hour. What the estate knew of its thirst was what stood on that slab. When the council argued about water, they argued over the slab; when the master asked whether the day had been dry, he meant whether the slab was clean. Old men who could remember whole seasons were no longer asked. A season did not fit on the slab.

The council kept benches for grain, for roads, for flocks, and one bench apart where only water was spoken of. To that bench, at dusk, the registrar spoke his book aloud: forty-eight names, six carrying, the rest fitted or dry or dead. The bench never once asked him to move water. In time it stopped occurring to the bench that moving water was a thing a man could be asked for. When the east channel burst, they sent menders within the morning, because the registrar's six had become five on the page and the page had shown it by first light. The bench raised his fee that year, and it was deserved.

The master had a saying that his father had used before him: better an empty cistern at dusk than a full one at dawn. Let it be spent, not stored. He said it at the bench and at the mill and at his own table. In the driest month the yard fountains ran all afternoon, because the water had come that day and to leave it standing overnight was, in the master's word, waste. Whether the fields had wanted it that afternoon was not what the saying asked.

On the emptiest day of the drought the registrar rehearsed. He walked the full round over dry ground, knocked at each coping in order, read each name aloud, wiped the blank slab at the blank hours, and spoke his count to the empty bench. The round held perfectly. Nothing in it had ever needed water.

A child asked him once why he wrote the names and never tasted the water. He turned to the first page of his book to find an answer and found instead a hand that no one living knew, older than his commission, already counting forty-eight in faded ink. He showed the child the page. The child asked who had decided the count. The registrar knocked twice on the coping, said the hour was late, and went on to the next name.
