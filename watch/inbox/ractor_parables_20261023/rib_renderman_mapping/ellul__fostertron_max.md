# The Tight Rows

Ilse came to the workshop in the dry autumn to learn the writing of orders. The house stood at the top of the valley, and from its windows she could see the far pastures where the planters worked, small as nails against the brown hills. Everything the house commanded, it commanded in writing; no planter was ever brought to the house to be shown anything.

Her first lesson was the rows. An order was not a letter. It was a ladder of small marks, row under row, and every mark placed a thing by one fact only: how far it stood from the nearest border of the ground. A tree was not "the beautiful tree by the well"; it was a mark, and a number, and the number was the distance from the closest edge. Old Mother Aldis, who kept the origins, had nailed the house's hand to this writing long ago, with a ruling pressed by a stamp cut with seventeen teeth. She showed Ilse the corridor of portraits: the first grandmother, who had proved a picture could be grown out of words alone, without anyone standing before the field; the second, who had measured her whole country by distance from the nearest fence and found that distance was the only fact that never argued; the third, who had watched scattered flocks all night and shown that a disturbed flock, left alone, walks back to its old order with no shepherd directing it. The portraits were dusted weekly. The rows were dusted never, because they were used too often to hold dust.

In her second week Ilse copied two orders for the same garden. One went to the high pasture, where the mule track was bad and every pound of paper rode on a mule's back; its rows were pressed so tight the whole garden sat on a thumbnail, and the planter up there read them the way others read weather. The other stayed in the valley, where paper was cheap as straw; it sprawled across three sheets, every mark spelled out for apprentices. When Ilse asked which was correct, the clerk shrugged: tight where carriage is dear, loose where paper is cheap — the mule decided, and nobody had ever overruled the mule. She noticed, later, that the tight rows were coming down into the valley now, that valley letters were beginning to look like pasture letters, that even the baker's boy compressed his notes. Nobody had ordered this. It arrived with the practice itself.

The memory of the house lived in books of six columns. Each row carried its number, its claim, what it took from older rows, the question that would undo it if anyone dared ask it well — and two more slots too plain to need describing. Beside each row stood a small mark: seen, quarreled, or not yet. Ilse learned that a row without its undoing question was refused at the door, however fine its claim.

In the message room hung a stencil that a young clerk named Tomas had cut one evening the winter before. It held the same six slots as the memory books, exactly, so that a finding could pass from a messenger's hand into the book without being rewritten by anyone. It was well cut. It hung on its nail. The messengers still arrived talking, scattering their news in their own voices, and the clerks still listened and wrote in their own hands, and the stencil governed nothing yet. Tomas said it would, in time. The drawer did not disagree.

The last room held the gray catalog. In it stood every far master the house might ever call: where each one lived, the road by which to summon them, and the small trial each would have to pass before being trusted with the house's ground. No master lived in the valley. Their hands, their heavy chests of tools, stayed in their far villages until a job truly called for them — and the house had learned to want a thing done before it paid for the hauling of a chest. The catalog was bound in gray, not black. Gray meant asked-for: an elder had proposed it, and the house had not yet sworn it. Ilse asked when it would be sworn, and was told that gray books wait for their work to prove their color.

At the end of autumn she wrote her first order, for a hedge at the far fence, a mark and a number, tight enough for a mule. She watched the planters place it without a word coming back up the track, and she understood that the writing had already decided what the planters could be asked for and what they never would be. She put her thumb over the order, and it disappeared whole.
