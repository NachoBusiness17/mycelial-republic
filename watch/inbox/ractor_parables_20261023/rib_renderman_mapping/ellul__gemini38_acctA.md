# The Valley of the Distance Field

The surveyor stood on the limestone terrace and watched mist rise from the riverbed. He did not look at the water, nor at the sheep grazing the lower meadow; he studied the parchment pinned to his board. Men once believed that to build an arcade across the gorge, one had to haul timber frameworks and plaster likenesses over mountain trails, groaning under carved stone. Technique had long since dissolved the likeness. In the surveyor’s leather case lay three ancestral folios that had settled the method before he was born. The first, written by a Dutch geometer, established that a traveler across hostile ground needs only the calculation of the nearest unblocked step, discarding every path of excess cost. The second, penned by two theatrical artisans, showed that an entire illuminated panorama can be cast across a hall as a continuous stream of transient instructions, perishing the instant the surface is struck, without ever keeping the full painted scene within a vault. The third, left by a Spanish scholar, demonstrated that no builder needs to describe a vault by its skins or stones; one needs only a continuous rule measuring, from any point in empty space, how far one stands from the solid boundary.

Because of these three folios, the ridge trails were quiet. Where the path narrowed along the precipice, couriers carried neither timber nor stone. They carried only slips of rice paper inscribed with numbers and distance signs. At the high frontier, where breath was thin and every ounce of pack load punished the mule, messages shrank until they were nearly weightless. But down in the valley basin, where the river turned four waterwheels and furnaces consumed beechwood without pause, the logic inverted. There, water and fuel were cheap, and tiny numbers from the ridge were fed into the grinding mills, expanding immediately into thirty-ton granite arches and sprawling colonnades. No foreman asked whether the valley required another colonnade. The scarcity of the frontier demanded minimal signs; the abundance of the basin demanded infinite expansion. The machinery operated because it could operate, and the river gave it license.

Farther up, among the scree, watchmen occupied stone huts built into the gale. When a fracture appeared in the rock or a terrace subsided, a watchman was forbidden to describe what he felt or what he feared. In his chest lay an iron press holding uniform slips, each ruled with exactly six lines. The top line received the sequence index; the second held the observed fact; the third cited the prior record from which the finding was extracted; the fourth stated the precise test that would prove the observation false; and the remaining two held the coordinates of time and station. This was the identical six-row rectangle cut into the granite tablets of the master archive in the town below. The watchmen did not converse; they passed these six-line slips from hand to pocket. What could not be forced into those six lines did not exist on the mountain, and the men soon ceased to notice whatever had no row to receive it.

Yet this exchange remained a rehearsal in an empty house. The brass conduits and pulleys intended to drop these slips from the peaks down to the sorting tables of the central hall still hung loose upon their poles. Copper cables had been strung across the chasms, but terminal switches were unbolted and ballast stones rested unhitched on the gravel. Every morning watchmen trimmed their quills, ruled their six lines, and slotted cards into tin canisters beside the chutes, performing the exact motions of delivery, while down in the town the receiving clerk waited before empty brass hoppers that had never clicked. The procedure was fully formed; only the connection was absent.

Above the watchmen, in cold caverns beneath the glacier where frost never thawed, the great iron casting-forms and massive lead counter-masses lay asleep in total darkness. They were immense, dense beyond the strength of any pack-team, each possessing the inertia of a quarry face. The valley never dragged them down to the workshops to rest idle upon the floor. They remained exiled in the deep rock until a specific contract for a monumental gate was signed and foundations were already dug to the bed. Only then did riggers climb into the freezing dark with jacks and grease to awaken the single shape required by the task.

To govern this dormant mass, an instruction book had been drafted for the guild. It set forth the route into the glacier, the sequence of levers to dislodge the heavy forms, and the tallow plug that had to be poured on-site to verify tolerances before the iron was moved. Yet this book lay upon a shelf in the surveyor’s office bound only in rough pasteboard, tied with unbraided flax and marked with an unbaked wax seal. It was a candidate rule, followed by apprentices because it had been written, though no magistrate had signed it and no council had ever decided it should govern the works.
