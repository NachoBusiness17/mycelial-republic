# The Ribbon and the Valley Page

A counselor came up from the south to the picture-house at the river bend. He had spent his life writing in the margins of other men's books and trusted no sentence he could not try to break; he had heard that in this house pictures were made without hands. He came to see whether the thing was true or merely neighbor to the truth.

Nobody in the house painted. The apprentices sat at long tables making ribbons, strips of paper crowded with small marks, and at the end of the hall a brass press took each ribbon, read it mark by mark, and gave out a picture. The same ribbon through the press gave the same picture, every time. The warden would not let a picture hang unless the ribbon that begat it lay in the drawer beneath it; a picture without its ribbon, he said, was a rumor.

On a ribbon every mark stood for one whole phrase — no more, no fewer — written out at length on the wide pages of the valley book, where ink cost nothing. The craft of the house was the passage between the two: apprentices wrote phrase beside mark until they could fold a wide page down to its ribbon and unfold a ribbon back into its page. A mark without its phrase was struck off the ribbon; a phrase without its mark was struck off the page.

The house traced its descent to three portraits on the stair. The oldest was a ferryman from the marsh country; his one teaching was that a procession scattered on the road settles back into its own order without command from behind. Next came two partners in one frame, who had set the grammar of the ribbons: lights before surfaces, surfaces before distances, every picture speakable in marks. The youngest was a surveyor, painted within living memory, who taught the house to give a shape without drawing it: at every point write the steps to the nearest edge, counted forward outside the shape and backward within it, and the press will find the edge where the number turns. The warden named the three in one breath and would not choose among them.

The door into the press room was narrow, and crossing it was dear. Whatever went through had to be folded small; an apprentice who tried to carry a wide page across was sent back to the folding tables. Yet nothing in the house stayed folded: the warden would not file a ribbon until its wide twin was written out on the cheap tables, and the drawer held them together, narrow beside wide. Reading the doorpost, the counselor laughed and told of a man he had known, also a counselor, who had written in the margin of a borrowed book that he had found a marvelous argument the margin was too narrow to hold, and had gone to his grave with it folded. That evening he asked for cheap paper and wrote out, in full, an argument he had carried folded for thirty years, and slept better than he had in a decade.

In the porch the house meant to send out riders each season, readers who would go to other houses and bring back what held and what failed. Each finding was to ride on a card of exactly six lines: a number first; what the rider held true; what he had taken from another's holding; the name of the man sworn to try to break it; and two lines more, of errand and warrant, which the warden recited while the counselor counted. The books of the house's own remembrance were kept in the same six lines, so that a card from the road could lie down among them without a clerk's changing a letter. But the porch stood empty. The cards lay in a wooden box with two winters of dust on the lid, and the box had not been moved since it was nailed. The warden said the roads would open in spring; the counselor tapped the lid.

In a back room a keeper tended a catalogue of far foundries. For each, three things were written: the road to it, the words that would call its metal across the mountains, and the trial to be applied when the metal arrived. The metal itself never came on speculation; the house bought roads, not iron, and the heavy stock stayed in its own province until a task called for it by name. At the catalogue's corner hung a chalk mark, the sign of a thing proposed and argued at the winter table, defended, sworn by nobody yet. Asked what would become of it, the keeper said it would stand until someone proved it, or someone needed it.

On the last evening the warden brought the visitors' book, a wide page with room for anything. The counselor wrote: I came to see whether the pictures were true or only neighbor to the truth; they are neighbor, and they say so; I broke one card out of the box and could not break the second; I will send six lines of my own by the first rider, whenever the riders ride. He wrote it out in full, margin to margin, wasting the paper gladly, and in the morning he went south.
