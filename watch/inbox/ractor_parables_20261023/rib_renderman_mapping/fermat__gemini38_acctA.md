# The Measure of the Boundary

The magistrate had spent twenty years in the provincial court evaluating claims by the weight of their evidence, and he had learned that those who bring wagons of timber to prove a boundary usually have no deed at all. When he rode up to the northern frontier of the estate, he found the toll-bridge empty of freight. No cartloads of sculpted masonry or cut beams waited in the mud. A solitary rider crossed from the outer wilderness, bearing only a strip of vellum no wider than two fingers, upon which were written twelve compact phrases of geometry. The gatekeeper read the slip, stamped the border permit without weighing the saddlebags, and waved the rider through. When the magistrate asked how an estate could fortify its ramparts with scraps of parchment, the gatekeeper replied that across the frontier, where passage was perilous and carriage dear, they transported only the bare rules of form; down in the valley, where sand, lime, and water were cheap, the local artisans would expand those few lines into forty cubits of solid bastion.

The magistrate followed the rider down to the stonecutter’s shed, where three brass medallions were nailed above the drafting board. The first showed a craftsman who, thirty-eight years earlier, had taught the guilds to transmit projections of surface and shadow as continuous ribbons of notation rather than haul stone figures across the provinces. The second honored a surveyor of thirteen years past, who ceased tracing visible contours altogether, decreeing that a shape is wholly known if one can measure the signed distance from any point in space to its invisible envelope—positive in the open air, zero on the surface, and negative within the solid core. The third honored an elder from fifty-two years back, who proved that the shortest passage between two settlements could be found step by step without traversing every road in the province. Before the drafting board, an apprentice was not chiseling along a drawn contour; he hovered a plumb line over the block, reckoning aloud the exact distance to the intended boundary. Only when his calculation touched zero did his mallet strike.

In the gravel courtyard outside, two scouts in dusty cloaks met beside the water trough. They spoke no greetings. One pulled a rectangular card from his vest and passed it to the other. The magistrate looked over the scout's shoulder. The card was ruled with precisely six lines, neither more nor less. The top line carried the sequential number of the observation; the second held the claim of what had been discovered; the third recorded what had been borrowed from an earlier traveler; the fourth stated the single test that would render the entire claim false; and the final two lines fixed the boundaries of the trial. The magistrate walked into the manor’s archive and pulled open the central cabinet. Within lay hundreds of historical decrees spanning generations, and every one of them was inscribed upon an identical card of six lines, arranged in the exact order the scouts had traded at the trough. Yet when the magistrate remarked to the archivist that the scouts obeyed the ancient statutes of the house, the old clerk smiled and shook his head. He confessed that the exchange between the riders was merely a working prototype among traveling companions, not yet bound by statutory decree or wired to the estate’s signaling bells.

At dusk, the magistrate walked to the northern cliff where a solitary storehouse stood padlocked against the wind, three leagues from the foundry floor. Through the iron grating, he saw the massive brass counterweights and heavy casting apparatus hanging from timber beams in absolute stillness. The furnace master stood beside him with unlit torches. The magistrate asked why the foundry below lay quiet while the apparatus remained sequestered on the high ridge. The master explained that the heavy masses remained foreign territory, never moved down the mountain path until a specific work order was signed and the coals were ready; to carry them beforehand was to waste labor on work that might never be commanded. On the heavy oak door hung a slate describing the method to locate, verify, and summon the casting gear. Across the top of the slate, the magistrate saw carved the word "Candidate," signifying that the entire procedure remained under review, awaiting the assent of the winter tribunal before it could enter the permanent canon.
