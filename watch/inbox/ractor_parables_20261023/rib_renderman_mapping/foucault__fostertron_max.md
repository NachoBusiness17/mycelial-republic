# The Freight That Stayed Home

Véra came to the harbor house of the picture-writers to learn who had taught them to speak. She found a building with no books of prose at all. Every order the house sent abroad was drawn: a facade, a tide-table, a procession of torches, all pressed into a picture no wider than a thumb and folded until the courier felt nothing in his coat. Only in the inland towns, where paper cost little and copyists idled at every well, was the picture opened and redrawn at its full size. The masters kept a saying about it: what is heavy must not travel; let the small thing cross the world and grow where growing costs nothing. Véra wrote the saying down, because sayings are where houses hide their laws.

In the long room hung three portraits. Two showed men of the same generation who had, a lifetime ago, taught the guild to describe an entire scene — light, surfaces, weather, faces — as marks that a lantern-room downstream could carry out without asking questions. The third showed a woman with brass compasses, younger in the craft's memory, who had proved that a shape could be known entirely by its edge: measure the distance from any point to the nearest boundary and you possessed the whole figure, its inside and its outside, without ever drawing either. Below her, painted smallest and oldest of all, hung a man who had never drawn a picture in his life; he had spent his years finding the shortest road between any two towns, and the guild counted him an ancestor because every instruction they sent was, in the end, a road chosen. The porter dusted all three each morning and could not say which one the house owed the most.

In the exchange hall, traders were permitted to report only in strings of six. A number first; then the thing asserted; then what it would cost a rival to take it; then the test that would break it; then two further knots whose uses Véra did not learn that day. The strings were shaped exactly like the entries in the house's book of memory — same knots, same order — so that a finding could pass from a stranger's mouth into the book without changing its shape. The custom was carved above the dais in fresh letters. When Véra ran her thumb along the carving, the wood beneath the letters was pale and unworn. No string had yet changed hands under it. The rule hung the way an unused door hangs: fully fitted, opening on nothing.

The warden showed her the catalogue of distant teachers. One page to each: where the teacher kept house, by what road to summon them, and by what trial to test them once they stood in the hall — for a teacher who arrived was always examined before being believed. But each was summoned light. No chests, no instruments, none of the heavy freight in which a teacher's actual skill was stored; that remained in the teacher's own country until some definite labor asked for it by name. The warden turned to the page where this was written and pressed it flat with two fingers. "You should know that this page is still a petition," he said. "Some have signed it. No one has yet obeyed it. It waits to become law." He had told her without being asked, and he watched her face while he told it — the examiner examining himself being examined.

Late in the day she saw a woman turned away from the dais. The woman had stood in line with her string ready and, at the front, had said only that she did not yet know whether her finding held. The steward was not cruel. He explained that the six knots had a place for what is asserted and a place for how to break it, and no place at all for not-knowing; a string that would not assert could not be entered, and what cannot be entered cannot be traded or kept. The woman folded her cord and left. Véra asked what became of such people, and the steward said, kindly enough, that they came back once they had decided. It was said without malice, which is how a house says the thing it cannot hear.

Walking out, Véra passed the courier setting off with the evening post, his coat light as ever. In the breast pocket he carried the newest picture of all: the hall, the dais, the fresh-carved custom, the catalogue, drawn and pressed small enough to cross the world, six empty knots waiting inside it, each the size of a grain of salt, for someone far away to open where growing is cheap.
