# The Economy of the Outer Gate

The surveyor reached the northern boundary wall just as the wagons were being halted for inspection. A young wagoner had brought a full wooden reproduction of a watchtower—girders, beams, and shingled roof—intending to carry it into the administrative enclosure to show the council how the border post was built. The inspector on the raised walkway would not permit the wagon to cross the ditch. He leaned over the parapet with a rule in his hand and told the youth that a shape is not permitted to enter as timber and bulk. He gave the boy a slender ribbon of parchment, three fingers wide, on which were inked only simple distances from a central point, a sequence of mathematical intervals declaring how far a body must walk in any direction before striking solid stone. Above the postern gate, three names were carved into the granite header: the scene-setter of eighty-eight who first rendered shadows from pure description, the geometer of thirteen who measured every solid solely by its nearest gap, and the pathfinder of seventy-four who proved that every journey across a network must advance along the shortest clear span. The inspector pointed to the three names and recited the standing decree: the perimeter permits only the most contracted cipher; the expansion into vast timber and labor is reserved for the interior yards where space is cheap.

Beyond the checkpoint, the yard opened into an unpaved square where roving scouts gathered around a long table. Each scout carried a bundle of cedar laths, each slip scored into precisely six equal tiers. The first tier carried the index mark; the second recorded the finding witnessed in the field; the third named what another observer was licensed to take and make his own; the fourth defined the exact contrary condition that would prove the observation false; the fifth and sixth bore the signatures of the witness and the hour. The surveyor watched an apprentice compare these slips against the great granite tablets that lined the southern arcade, where the foundation records of the estate were permanently engraved. The six tiers on the cedar laths answered point for point to the six divisions on the stone. An apprentice reached for a copper tube that ran along the eaves, preparing to blow the field slips toward the archive tower, but the elder surveyor laid a hand on his forearm. The tubes were set into the masonry and the brass valves were polished, but the joints between the quadrants had never been soldered. The roving scouts might hold their sixfold slips ready in their coats, but their exchange remained a pattern practiced by hand, uncoupled from any continuous pneumatic circuit.

In the eastern courtyard stood a stone dais prepared for the grand analytical engine, an apparatus whose function was to examine every deposition and measure the probability of its truth. Yet the dais was bare. The heavy iron ballast blocks that gave the engine its balance and depth of judgment were absent. They remained stored in the distant hill quarries three leagues away, beyond the river. A clerk leaned against the vacant plinth, sharpening a quill, and explained to the surveyor that the heavy iron was forbidden from crossing the gate until a specific docket of inquiry was opened and demanded its presence. On the edge of the stone platform rested a single unbound fascicle describing how to summon the iron, erect the armature, and test the machine before a magistrate. In the upper margin of the title page, where the seal of sovereign doctrine was normally struck, a wax stamp displayed only the word of petition: an unratified submission, a candidate for the rule, waiting at the edge of the code to learn whether the estate would acknowledge it as established statute.
