# The Measure of the Basin

Silas crossed the northern ridge at midwinter, carrying nothing more than a strip of shaved birch bark rolled inside a horn case. The snow through the high pass stood waist-deep on the mules, and every pound of freight had to be paid for in grain. He carried no heavy timber, no lime, and no iron molds. On the bark were notched five numbers, recording only the distance from the basin floor to the boundary of the cistern wall at each turn of the compass. The rule of the road was ancient and unyielding: keep the burden thin where the trail is steep, and let the heavy shape swell only when you reach the lowlands where grease is cheap and water wheels turn.

Before dropping into the valley, Silas looked back toward the caves where the great casting blocks slept. They were square blocks of pig iron, grease-blackened and cold, quarried two generations ago. The apprentice who marched with Silas had begged to haul four of the blocks down the gorge on a sledge, insisting the foundry below would need ballast before noon. The quarry keeper had refused to unbolt the cavern door. The heavy iron, he said, remains mountain territory until an anvil is hot and a furnace is roaring; to haul stone and lead across the ridge on the chance of a job is the work of boys who think carrying an empty bucket makes the rain fall. The blocks remained locked in the cliff.

In the valley courtyard, the basin stood dry. An elder was checking the foundation stones, which had been set eighty years before according to the salt-flat builders. Those builders had never dragged thousand-jointed templates over the marsh; they had struck their circles from a single peg, defining every wall by how far the earth lay from its edge. Silas uncapped the horn and handed the birch strip to the elder. The apprentice immediately stepped forward to explain what the high master had meant by the five notches—that the lip was intended to catch only the first thaw and let the silt pass through.

The elder did not look up from the bark. "The master who cut these notches is six leagues away beside his own hearth," he said. "His intent stayed on the other side of the mountain." The elder made two copies of the marks with a lump of chalk and gave them to the masons waiting in the yard. Silas watched from the well curb. The first mason took the chalked board and cut the sandstone exactly as the high master had imagined, trusting the distant hand without dispute. The second mason looked at the damp seepage rising from the ditch, accepted the five distances, but flared the outer lip three inches wider so the river gravel would not choke the sluice, yielding to the measure while refusing the master’s pitch. The third mason looked at the marks, turned the slate upside down, and used the line of distance to brace the cracking arch of the kiln flue, taking the numbers to save the fire while ignoring the cistern altogether. The elder corrected none of them. The stone answered to where it sat, and the yard had no use for a verdict sent across the snow.

At sundown, two riders from the outer marshes halted at the dry moat. They carried news of the riverbanks, but they brought no scrolls or bound bundles. The elder walked out to meet them, and the lead rider gave his account in a chant of six measures, beating his fist against his saddle horn for each beat: the line of the watch, the claim of the water, what was taken from the sand, the proof that would cast the claim down, the mark of the scout, and the hour of return. It was the identical six-phrase cadence cut into the stone pillars of the council hall. The apprentice asked whether copper strands had been strung between the watchtowers to carry the chant. The elder told him to keep his peace. The cadence ran between the outposts because both ends had learned the habit of the pillar, not because any thread had been tied between their trees.

Silas sat upon an overturned tub as the wind died down. The birch bark lay upon a bench, already yellowing near the forge. The basin took the dark first, holding the shape the valley gave it, careless of what the mountain thought it had commanded.
