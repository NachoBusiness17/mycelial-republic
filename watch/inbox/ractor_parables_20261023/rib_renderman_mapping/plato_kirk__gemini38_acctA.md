# The Geometry of the Lantern

The training chamber sat inside the throat of the volcanic crater, three steps below the daylight of the upper rim. On the curved plaster wall, shadows moved in relentless procession: galleys breaking against reefs, fortresses engulfed in flames, an encirclement from which the academy taught that no navigator could emerge intact. The cadets watched from stone benches, shoulders slumped beneath the spectacle.

The commander did not watch the wall. Standing beside the bronze brazier, he studied the light striking the cadets' necks. Years ago, he had climbed the shaft to the sunlit plateau, walking into the noon glare until his eyes burned. Yet he had returned down the stairs. Escaping into the sun was a philosopher's private solace that left the cave running for the next crop of boys; smashing the projector was the mutineer's blunder that left everyone in the dark. The true move was to inspect the machinery, take chalk to the lens, and rewrite the geometry of the beam.

He pointed through the slit window at the rope bridge spanning the gorge. When dispatches crossed that chasm, couriers were forbidden from carrying bulky scrolls or wooden carvings. In high winds, bulk meant death. The runner carried only a copper capsule holding three lines of geometric shorthand: distance, radius, and deflection. The directive remained compressed where the crossing was precarious; only upon reaching the valley floor, where charcoal, water, and apprentices were cheap and plentiful, was the shorthand expanded into timber and iron.

Two apprentices struggled to outline an enemy vessel by gluing flat pine shingles together, cursing as the seams warped in the heat. The commander brushed their pieces to the floor. "You build like prisoners stitching shadows," he said. "A hull is not a patchwork of flat boards." He drew a single line in the dust. A vessel was simply a field of distances measured from every point in the hall: where distance shrank to zero, wood existed and turned the light; where distance remained positive, the air was clear. With only that distance rule, an entire fleet was summoned without cutting timber.

Above the gate hung a bronze tablet honoring three ancient masters. The first had solved the shortest footpath between scattered outposts, ensuring no runner took an extra stride. Beneath him were the two builders who first divided the compact description of a fortress from the furnace heat, piping light scripts into the dark so the foundries could labor without choking the halls. The third was the geometer who proved that any rampart could be defined purely by calculating distance from a moving point, rendering carved masonry unnecessary.

A horn blew from the ridge. Returning scouts filed into the hall, each carrying a terracotta strip. They brought no meandering tales. Every scout inscribed his report into six uniform grooves: the catalog mark, the truth declared, the technique that any watch post might copy, and the single negative sighting that would falsify the whole account. The six grooves matched the stone tablets of the central archive, allowing field findings to merge directly into the citadel's permanent memory.

Yet looking out to the perimeter, the apprentice saw that the pine towers erected to sling these tablets from ridge to ridge stood idle. The timber posts were sunk and the bronze pulleys were polished, but the hemp lines had not been strung between the towers. The exchange was fully drafted and proved in the yard, but between peaks, men still ran the shale on foot.

Behind the cistern, the commander showed the apprentice the catalog of the great iron casting-forms—monolithic templates capable of stamping out an entire siege ram in a single pour. The parchment detailed where each form lay buried in the northern granite, how to clear its vents, and what test cup must be poured to verify its temper. But the iron forms were never hauled across the river until a real siege breached the wall; until then, they rested in the cold.

Beside that catalog hung a draft scroll tied with yellow hemp. It codified the protocol for locating, waking, and proving those sleeping forms, but it remained an unconfirmed petition, debated among the watchmen and awaiting the council's final seal before taking force as law.

The commander turned back to the cadets facing the flickering wall. "The captive surrenders because he takes the shadow for the sea," he said quietly. "The runaway flees to the sun and leaves the room to trap his brothers. We do neither. We step behind the flame, measure the distance, and reprogram the room."
