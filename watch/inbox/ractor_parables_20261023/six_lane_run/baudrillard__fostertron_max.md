# The Valley Agreed

On the twenty-third morning of October the institute sealed a single writ and had it copied six times without variation. The writ asked one thing: cross the river, walk the valley, and bring back a chart of what stands there. Six were engaged to answer. Each was lodged at a different edge of the town, each had been kept from speech since the writ was sealed, and each set out at first light with an empty satchel — for the rule of the house was that the valley must be met as if for the first time, by everyone, all at once.

The six were not of one kind. The first came down from the capital in a lacquered case, the fine instrument of a fine house, attended by footmen who carried nothing else. The second arrived in place of another man, holding a pass stamped by a rich guild; the pass was not his, but it opened the same bridges. The third and fourth were walkers, men with keys on their belts who went through the valley on their own feet, unlatching what stood shut, kneeling to read what was cut into the stones. The fifth took no fee; he worked in sight of the public fountain, where anyone might watch his hand move. The sixth never crossed the river at all: he stayed at his mother's table in a small village and drew from what he could hold in his two hands.

By dusk the charts came back, and the institute did not hang them side by side to be admired. It ruled a wall into rows and columns — a row for each of the six, a column for every landmark the writ had named — and into every square where two charts could be made to speak of the same thing it pressed a bead of wax. The wax was poured in three grades. Clear wax said: here they all spoke alike, and firmly. Clouded wax said: here they agree, but thinly, or only some of them. Dark wax said: here they contradict one another or fall silent, and whoever calls this knowledge is selling something. Square by square the wall filled, and the valley that had been walked that morning became a surface of small lights.

Then the assessor came. He was famous, he arrived by the evening coach, and he had been engaged for one visit. He did not cross the river. He walked the wall once, from the top row to the bottom, gave his judgment in a single sitting, and it was written down whole, copied onto one great sheet together with the wax, and hung where anyone might read it. No one asked him a second question; a second asking would have been a second engagement, and the institute had provided for one. His verdict was not set against the valley, because the valley cannot be consulted. It was set against the wall, which can.

In the margin of the hung sheet a junior had written a small line, and the institute, to its credit, did not erase it: that the two walkers, going to the instrument cabinet at midday, had passed the shelf where the finished carriers lay waiting, and had read the names inked on their straps. They had not opened the carriers. They had seen no lines and no landmarks — only that this rival's strap said one thing and that rival's said another. The line was allowed to stand, and once it stood, everyone who studied the sheet studied the walkers' two rows a moment longer, as though a name, having been seen, had done something.

It had. From that day the walkers' squares were read differently — not because their charts differed, but because their makers had passed signboards that pointed at works they never read. A name is a small thing. It refers to nothing and it changes everything it touches. The wax in their squares was not re-graded. It was only looked at longer, which amounts to the same thing.

The sheet is still there. Travelers come to consult it the way their fathers consulted rivers. They ask where the spring rises, and the sheet tells them, and they go down the steps satisfied, and the spring is wherever the clear wax is thickest. No one has crossed the river since. A path leads to it from the institute, and grass has grown across the first stones. It was not that the six charts lied, nor even that they disagreed; it is that their agreement no longer needs the valley. The junior points this out to visitors, at the margin, under the wax. The visitors nod, copy the sheet, and ask whether the copy will do as well. He tells them it always has.
