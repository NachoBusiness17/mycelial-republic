# The Six Alcoves of October

On the twenty-third afternoon of October, Anselm arranged six velvet-curtained alcoves along the upper gallery of the Exchange. A syndicate of canal directors wanted the city council to ratify a new winter tariff before the river froze, and they had asked Anselm to draft a public proclamation. Anselm refused. A proclamation signed by the directors, he told them, only advertises who wishes to be obeyed; when six unconnected examiners arrive at the same diagnosis on the same afternoon, the councilmen persuade themselves that they are bowing to plain fact rather than to the syndicate's interest.

To fill the six alcoves, Anselm assembled six distinct crafts. In the first stood the chief draughtsman of the syndicate's flagship counting-hall, accustomed to the heaviest commissions. For the second, the foreign factor originally summoned had been turned back at the turnstile for lacking a current customs stamp, so Anselm admitted a merchant-appraiser carrying a purchased gold-bordered pass in his stead, preserving the count of six. In the third and fourth stood twin surveyors trained in the same academy and practicing the same method—one working at a folding camp-desk, the other at a heavy stationary console. In the fifth stood a street-corner pamphleteer from the free municipal reading room who charged no fee for his labor. In the sixth worked a cellar watchmaker who kept his casements latched against the city telegraph wires and relied solely upon the brass gears under his own roof.

Before the curtains were drawn, the gallery steward required all six men to surrender their satchels, old notebooks, and yesterday's newspapers at the vestibule counter, and to rinse their hands in a porcelain basin. None was told what earlier committees had concluded or whose purse paid for the candles. Every man stepped into his alcove with an unclouded memory, ensuring that no prior briefing or lingering habit from an earlier commission could color what he wrote.

Once the six were inside, the steward plugged the speaking-tubes with beeswax and bolted the baize partitions from baseboard to rafter. Each alcove contained its own graphite stylus, sand-shaker, and fresh quires of cream vellum; no blotter, scrap, or whispered rumor could pass from one writing-slope to its neighbor while the sand ran through the glass. Through six brass slots, Anselm slid six impressions struck from the same lead type-block. Not a comma was shifted, not an adjective softened from one sheet to the next. If two witnesses receive different flattery, a skeptic can blame the phrasing for their answer; when all six receive the identical dry charge, their eventual agreement looks like an unforced law of the world.

When the hour expired and the six manuscripts were gathered, Anselm did not count hands like a ward boss. Instead, he stretched a wide ruled canvas across an easel: six vertical columns for the six alcoves, crossed by horizontal rows for every distinct claim extracted from their pages. Where an alcove's manuscript addressed a claim, Anselm's assistant pressed one of three colored wafers into the square—crimson wax for unshakeable conviction, amber resin for guarded assent, or pale tallow for a tentative surmise. Anselm watched the canal directors lean forward as the crimson squares aligned across the canvas. The directors gained an unimpeachable mandate from the pattern, and believing that pattern moved the councilmen to lay aside their private objections and vote the tariff before supper.

Yet Anselm did not permit the six examiners to debate the canvas, nor did he allow his own assistants to haggle over the wafers in repeated sittings. He summoned a single deep-water hydrographer from the harbor board—an outsider who owed no favor to the gallery. Admitted once and handed the six manuscripts alongside the ruled canvas, the hydrographer was required to compose the entire final finding in a single uninterrupted sitting before leaving the building, with no second audience permitted.

As the hydrographer sanded his concluding page, the vestibule attendant spoke to Anselm in a low voice. Three of the six examiners—the twin academy surveyors and the free-room pamphleteer, who alone had been permitted to step out of their alcoves to fetch dividers and straightedges from the common supply chest—had walked past the mahogany rack where all six name-cards stood in their pigeonholes. They had not read a line of their neighbors' manuscripts, yet they had seen which crafts were being weighed beside their own, and every veteran of public staging knows that an examiner adjusts his tone the moment he learns whose title will hang next to his on the wall.

Anselm knew the councilmen would embrace the crimson grid and ignore the vestibule rack, because a crowd always accepts the version that delivers tidy unanimity over the version that is complete. Even so, before locking the hydrographer's October dossier in the archive chest, Anselm inscribed a plain marginal note recording that the tool-fetching examiners had seen the neighboring name-cards—preserving on the record the exact flaw that the accepted triumph cropped from view.
