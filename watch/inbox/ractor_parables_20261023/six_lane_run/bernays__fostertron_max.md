# The Table of Stones

The guild received a letter in a script nobody in town could read, and the guild wanted two things: to know what it said, and to be believed when they told the town. They sent for me. I had spent a life arranging what towns believe — about soap, about candidates, about the safety of the harbor in a cholera summer — and I told them the plain truth of my trade: belief is not argued into people. It is built, out of materials they already trust. Numbers are one. Stones are another.

We fetched six translators. The first was famous and came under a costly contract. The second wore a borrowed badge and stood in for a better-known man who could not travel; he was paid by the hour and knew his craft. The third and fourth were local men who kept their own carts and ran their own errands across town, used to going where the work was without asking leave. The fifth asked no fee and worked at the inn for the price of the room. The sixth lived above the kiln, owned his books, and answered to nobody. Six different roads into the same craft — that was the point of choosing them so.

Each was given a room of his own at the guild house. The letter was copied six times by one hand, and each copy was read to its translator from the same scroll, word for word, twice, so that no man could hear it differently. Each began with clean paper, his own ink, and nothing else — no notes from the others, no gossip carried in, no walls in common worth the name. They did not meet, they did not dine together. When a man finished, his pages went into a satchel with his trade-mark chalked on the flap, the satchel went to the counting room, and the man went home by a different stair.

On the fourth day the guild laid the six texts side by side on the long table and set clerks to compare every man's pages against every other man's, fifteen pairings in all. Where two texts said the same thing in the same place, the clerk set a stone in the square ruled out for that pair: a white stone for plain agreement, a grey stone for agreement that needed charity, a red stone for a resemblance nobody should lean on. Square by square the table filled, and when it was finished the stones made it look like arithmetic, and a town will believe arithmetic without being told to. I asked the master whether six men agreeing meant the letter had been read well or only read alike. He looked at the stones and chose the stones.

I sent for one judge, a grammarian from the coast with no stake in the guild. He came once, read all six texts and the whole table in a single morning, wrote his finding in a fair hand, signed it, and left on the evening boat. Nobody asked him a second question. His single pass was declared the finding, bound in grey cloth, dated the twenty-third day of the tenth month, and shelved in the hall where any citizen may pull it down.

Then the master came to me, uncomfortable, with the matter of the errand-men. The third and fourth translators, being men who moved about freely, had carried their own satchels down the corridor, and both had passed the shelf where the finished work waited, and both had read the trade-marks chalked on the flaps. They had seen whose work lay beside whose. Rub it out, said the master. I told him no. A record that confesses a small fault is believed twice as long as one that claims to be clean, because a man who admits one fault is assumed to have hidden none. The paragraph stayed, in small ink, at the foot of the finding.

What the town took, the town carries easily. The fishmonger quotes the white stones at the quay as if he had counted them himself, and the buyers nod. In the hall the grey binding gathers dust at the height of a man's eye, and the small ink at its foot has never once been read aloud. Passing the market, I hear my stones at work, and I am reminded of the oldest fact of my trade: the version a person can hold in one hand is the version that survives, and whatever did not fit in that hand stays on the shelf. The grey cloth has not been taken down since the week it was bound. The grammarian's boat has not come back.
