# The Six Rooms

I keep a dog and a lantern, and when the householder swept out his six rooms I lit the lantern at noon, because noon is when a house practices its best manners.

He wanted one answer and was ashamed to lean on a single mouth, so he leaned on six. Around his courtyard stood six small rooms, and that morning every one of them was swept to the boards, lime-washed, given a full water jug and a clean slate, so that nothing of any earlier occupant remained and nothing of one room crossed into another. Six scribes came in by six roads; they had supped the night before at six separate fires, and no two had ever seen each other's faces. The householder copied his question six times with his own hand, the same words in the same ink, and slid one card under each door.

They were not six of a kind, and the dog knew it at once. The first to arrive was tall and expensively shod, come down from the capital, and he settled in as though the room should be grateful. The second carried no papers of his own — his had not come — so he worked under a token borrowed from a richer house, and the token spoke oftener than he did. Two more did not come alone: each kept apprentices to fetch water and bark and to hold the second copy straight, and the apprentices went in and out like shuttles. The fifth worked at the fountain in the market and took payment from no one; his sleeves were wet and his slate was cracked. The sixth lived two streets off and owned plain tools and no guild at all. The dog sniffed all six and found them equally dogs, which is the oldest compliment there is.

While the rooms scratched, the courtyard kept its own small commerce. As each scribe finished, the householder folded his pages, tied them, wrote a title on the fold, and laid the bundle by that scribe's door until evening. The apprentices, going to the well, passed the doors. The dog watched them slow down at each bundle and read the titles, again and again, the way dogs read a fence. They touched no paper. They read only the names of the papers. The dog said nothing; dogs keep things for later.

At dusk an old woman came over the river, and she came once. She carried a thin book and a graver. She took the six bundles to the long table, ruled the table into squares, set every answer against every other answer, and went over the whole table one time, slowly. Where two answers matched through and through, she cut the mark deep into the wood. Where they touched in part, she scored it light. Where she could not say, she left a hollow ring and passed on. No mark was deepened because the square beside it was deep; each square kept the weight her own eyes had given it. She asked no one to explain anything. She wrote the day on the cover, copied the table into the book, and left the book open where anyone could read it.

Before she tied her wrap, the dog came and stood at the table and looked at her, then at the doors, then at her again, the way he asks. I told her plainly what he had seen: the apprentices had been reading the titles at the doors. She took the pen back out. In the margin she wrote that two of the six had kept helpers whose eyes had touched the labels of the others' work, and that the squares touching those two should be held a little lighter for it. She did not scrape the deep marks to make the table prettier, and she did not fold the margin under. I put my lantern to the page, and the page held.

The marks said where the six stood with one another. They said nothing about the pear tree. The question had been which limb to cut, and all evening the householder ran his finger over the squares, hunting for the number that would walk out and lift the saw for him. I spoke to him across the table, because someone owed him a plain word. "Your rooms pretend to be strangers while the corridor carries their names to the well. Your table measures how six men agree, and you read it as though it measured the tree. And you are begging six strangers, a borrowed token, and a wet-sleeved boy to carry your choosing for you, so that your hands stay clean. Stop asking the world for that; it was never selling it. The saw fits your hand tonight as well as it ever will. Keep one thing from this evening: the margin. It is the only thing under this roof my lantern cannot shame — a woman who wrote her own flaw into her own book before supper."

In the morning he cut the limb himself, and the dog went along to supervise. The tall one sent a bill; the fountain boy asked for nothing; both were fed at the same table. The six rooms stand swept and empty now; the margin is still in the book, and it still holds.
