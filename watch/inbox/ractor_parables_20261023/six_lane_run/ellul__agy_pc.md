# The Six Alcoves of the Measuring House

In the last week of October, the chief surveyor of the estate resolved that a single pair of eyes could no longer be trusted to verify the stone threshold of the old granary. What one observer reported might be colored by habit, and so the surveyor designed an arrangement that would replace trust with procedure. Before dawn he had six narrow alcoves beneath the eaves freshly lime-washed from floor to rafters. Every scrap of old parchment, every notched tally stick, and every trace of prior reckonings was swept out, so that each chamber began without memory of any earlier work done on the estate.

Into these six chambers he placed six drafters of markedly different station. The first alcove held a master chronicler of the highest stipend from the city guild. The second held a replacement scribe carrying a borrowed guild signet, sent because the contracted master had been barred at the tollgate. The third and fourth held two active stewards of the household—one brought in from the outlying lodge and one from the home study—both trained to unlock their own chests and fetch their own instruments. The fifth held an itinerant scrivener from the open market who asked no wages. The sixth held a quiet cellar clerk who lived under the estate's own roof and never crossed its outer wall.

When the shutters were bolted, an assistant slipped an identical sheet of instructions under each partition, pressed from the same copper plate so that not a syllable or comma differed from one chamber to the next. No drafter could hear his neighbor's reed pen, borrow oil for his lamp, or share a preliminary sketch while the hours passed. The surveyor stood in the courtyard convinced that if six isolated minds, scrubbed of yesterday and sealed from one another today, described the same stones in the same proportions, the agreement would belong not to men but to necessity.

Yet the two household stewards and the market scrivener had been fitted out as self-directing workers, permitted to step into the dim service gallery to draw blank rolls and inkhorns from the common shelf. Upon that shelf lay the six linen sleeves prepared for the day's returns, each inked on the flap with the title of its chamber and the designation of its occupant. None of the walkers opened another's sleeve or glimpsed a word written inside, yet as they reached for their own supplies they read the six titles lined side by side along the wood. Returning to their stools, they knew they were part of a sixfold trial and knew which classes of scribes sat behind the neighboring walls. When the surveyor learned that the walkers' freedom to gather their own tools had spoiled the purity of their isolation, he did not dismantle the chambers; he merely entered a formal qualification at the foot of the day's record, noting that those who walked the gallery had seen the outer titles of the others' sleeves.

At nightfall the six completed scrolls were carried to a long counting board ruled with charcoal into a grid of intersecting rows and columns. Each row named a distinct claim regarding the granary threshold, and each column belonged to one of the six chambers. Where a scroll spoke to a claim, a clerk laid a stamped lead weight inside the corresponding square: a triple-marked disk for firm and unqualified certainty, a notched disk for cautious agreement, and a plain slug where the witness was doubtful or alone. Soon the clerks were no longer speaking of mortar or lintels; they spoke only of the pattern of lead across the squares, measuring truth by the density of the metal on the board.

Still, a board of weights cannot sign a decree. To conclude the matter without allowing the six drafters to argue, the surveyor summoned an outside arbiter from across the river under a strict covenant: the man might enter the room once, read the six scrolls and the weighted grid in a single unbroken sitting, ask no follow-up question of any chamber, and write one final parchment declaring which features of the threshold had survived every test unchanged, with the warning about the glimpsed sleeve-titles preserved in its closing lines. By midnight the parchment was sealed. No grain had been moved and no stone of the granary had been touched, yet the estate had acquired a permanent requirement: henceforth, no man would believe his own hand on the stonework unless the six chambers were whitewashed, the grid weighted, and the single-breath arbiter called again.
