# The Six Reckoners of the Salt-Court

On the twenty-third day of the tenth month, the Councillor of the Salt-Court resolved to test a theorem concerning the constancy of the lintel. Trained in the jurisprudence of Toulouse and the geometry of the ancients, he knew too well the vanity of solitary calculators: a man may boast in a narrow margin that he possesses a marvellous demonstration for every power, when in truth his downward reduction holds only for the fourth degree and merely neighbors the general truth without possessing it. Rather than trust a single boast, the Councillor ordained a sixfold trial, setting six distinct reckoners to labor upon the same proposition at the same hour.

The six differed altogether in station, custom, and instrument. The first was the Chief Astronomer of the high turret, master of the heaviest brass quadrants in the province. The second was an envoy from the eastern guilds who lacked his master's primary signet, yet gained admission by presenting a surrogate warrant of gilt silver. The third and fourth were twin surveyors of the Peripatetic Brotherhood—one quartered in the orchard booth, the other at the domestic counting-desk—both empowered by their rule to pace the cases and fetch their own dividers. The fifth was a mendicant draughtsman from the open commons who drew figures for the public without tariff or stipend. The sixth was a recluse quartered in the subterranean vault beneath the courtyard, turning a small boxwood cylinder by tallow-light and never sending a courier beyond the outer ditch.

At daybreak the Councillor ordered six limestone alcoves to be newly lime-washed until every vestige of prior reckoning vanished from the walls. Each bench received virgin foolscap, a newly cut goose-quill, and clean sand; no scrap of yesterday's calculations was suffered to remain. Between one alcove and the next stood three cubits of solid masonry without aperture or speaking-pipe, so that no contender might borrow a lemma or learn where his neighbor faltered. To prevent the slightest variance in the demand, the Councillor himself pressed six sheets upon a single engraved copper plate, delivering to each alcove the very same words, figures, and stipulations, neither adding a gloss nor withholding a syllable.

Throughout the forenoon the six pursued their demonstrations, each attempting to show by infinite descent that any supposed exception in whole numbers must breed a lesser exception until the chain broke against unity. Yet at noon a blemish entered the strict blindness of the experiment. While the four stationary reckoners remained enclosed within their masonry, the two twin surveyors of the Brotherhood exercised their liberty to step into the outer scriptorium to fetch new compasses. Upon the walnut trestle near the archway lay the six parchment wrappers prepared to receive the finished returns, each already superscribed on its outer fold with the style and title of its designated reckoner. Neither twin opened a wrapper or glimpsed a line of another's demonstration; yet both beheld the outer titles arrayed upon the trestle. Learning of this, the Councillor neither annulled the work nor hid the fault, but inscribed at the head of the protocol that the walking twins had beheld the superscriptions of the rest, holding that a falsifier who conceals a crack in his vessel corrupts the measure more than the crack itself.

When the shadows lengthened, the six demonstrations were gathered and arranged upon a cross-ruled oak board divided into rows and columns, much as stakes are partitioned when a game of hazard is halted before its close. Where the six addressed each clause of the theorem, the Councillor refused to judge them by a crude mark of yes or no. Instead, into every square of the chequered grid he placed small counter-weights of lead according to the rigor of the proof: three grains where the descent was necessary and complete, two grains where the argument stood upon firm probability, and one grain where the writer offered only a bare surmise drawn from neighboring cases. Thus the cross-ruled board displayed both the concord of the six and the exact degree of every square's assurance.

To pronounce upon the completed board, the Councillor neither debated with the reckoners nor relied upon his own prepossession. He summoned the Assayer of the Abyssal Soundings, an austere arbiter from the estuary who was bound by custom to speak but once upon any cause. Admitted for a single audience, the Assayer surveyed the cross-ruled board, weighed the leaden grains in every compartment, and read the marginal proviso regarding the sighted wrappers. Without requesting a second sitting or exchanging a word of disputation, he wrote out in one uninterrupted stroke the definitive finding on the constancy of the lintel, and departed before the sand upon the page was dry.
