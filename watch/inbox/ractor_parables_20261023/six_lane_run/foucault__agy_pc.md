# The Architecture of the Six Alcoves

On the twenty-third morning of the tenth month, the inspectorate of the ward convened an inquiry to test whether an invariant rule could be drawn from six separate mouths without collusion. Power does not ask what men remember; it begins by washing them clean so that only the question remains. Before dawn, the bailiff scrubbed six stone alcoves along the gallery with lime, removing every scrap of yesterday’s chalk, every prior confession, and every personal notebook. Into each alcove he placed a blank sheet of vellum, an identical iron nib, and a word-for-word transcript of the same inquiry, stamped from a single copper plate. To be examined was first to be stripped of history: none of the six who entered was permitted to bring a pocketbook or recall a prior sitting.

Though the examination treated all six as equivalent units before the question, the apparatus had gathered them from starkly different orders of service. The first alcove received the grand chronicler of the high guild, whose heavy press normally commanded the largest fee in the province. The second was supposed to hold a regular notarized clerk, but because that clerk was barred at the gate, a substitute from the foreign exchange entered in his stead, admitted by a borrowed merchant’s brass passkey. The third and fourth alcoves were assigned to two brothers of the mendicant order of couriers—men trained not merely to sit and write, but to walk the corridors, open cabinets, and fetch their own instruments; one brother had been summoned from the distant watchtower, while the other worked from the magistrate’s home desk. The fifth alcove held an unlicensed street scribe from the open commons who worked without tariff or patron. The sixth held a cloistered monk who lived permanently in the cellar beneath the courtyard and never crossed the outer wall.

Once the oak shutters were bolted, no voice, signal, or draft of ink passed between the alcoves. Each writer labored in solitary visibility before his blank sheet, forbidden to see a single sentence another hand composed. Yet an architecture built to enforce blindness always betrays the habits of the bodies it employs. Whereas the guild chronicler, the passkey substitute, the commons scribe, and the cellar monk remained fixed behind their grilles and received their paper through a narrow slot, the two courier brothers were accustomed to crossing the anteroom to gather their own quills and folders. On the cedar rack beside the cistern stood six empty portfolios awaiting the finished sheets, each bearing a pasted slip inscribed with the designation of its alcove and occupant. As the two brothers fetched their supplies, their eyes passed over the rack. They never saw a word written inside another alcove, yet they read the names upon the outside of the six portfolios and knew who sat beside them in the dark.

At noon the shutters were unbolted, the six sheets were collected, and the examinees were dismissed without learning what the others had written. The warden did not allow the six to debate their differences, nor did he summon a council. Instead, he sent the bundle down to the deep soundings, where a solitary adjudicator—a seventh jurist who had occupied none of the alcoves—sat behind a green baize table. This jurist was permitted only a single, uninterrupted sitting: he could ask no follow-up questions, return no sheet for revision, and call no witness back. Spreading the six testimonies side by side, he ruled a great cross-referenced table upon a broadsheet, matching each witness against every assertion. Rather than marking claims merely right or wrong, he stamped each square of the grid with one of several graded dies—heavy bronze for unshaken certainty, scored silver for probable agreement, faint lead for a wavering inference—so that agreement itself became a measured hierarchy of confidence.

Before sealing the broadsheet into the ward’s permanent archive, the adjudicator appended a narrow marginal entry at the foot of the table. In it he recorded that the two courier brothers, by virtue of the very liberty of movement that made them useful to the house, had seen the labels on the neighboring portfolios in the anteroom. The inquiry was not annulled by this confession; rather, by inscribing the flaw into the official report, the tribunal transformed a breach of isolation into proof of its own vigilance, closing the dossier in a single stroke of the pen.
