# The Measuring of the Field

When the intendant came down into the valley, he did not climb the ridge, and he did not speak to the farmers. He sent for six scribes.

The first arrived in the master's own livery, with the finest letters in the province. The second came in the name of a greater scribe who could not travel, and wore that scribe's silver key at his belt. Two were brothers of a company that worked by errands, famous for opening doors that other scribes were meant only to describe. The fifth asked no fee and brought nothing but his patience. The sixth had never once left the valley, and carried nothing that could not be made from what lay at hand.

The question was a single question. A clerk wrote it out six times on six leaves, word for word, and it was forbidden to add a syllable or take one away. Each scribe was led to his own chamber. The chambers had been swept in the night, the windows left open, the old ink poured out and the wells refilled, so that no trace of any earlier hand survived in them. No scribe saw another's leaf, and none was told what another had answered. Each man sat down to the field as though it had never been described by anyone before.

Four of them wrote from where they sat. The two brothers did not sit. They let themselves out, walked the water channel, opened the gates, counted the furrows aloud, and came back with mud on their boots and their leaves crossed with crossings-out. In the corridor where all six worked there stood a shelf, and on the shelf lay a box for each scribe, and on each lid a few words naming the labor folded inside. The brothers, going in and out as errand-men do, could not help seeing the lids. Afterwards they swore — and the oath was written down — that they had read nothing but those few words, that no lid had been lifted and no page turned by their hands.

When the six leaves were brought to the clerk, he did not read them for pleasure, and he did not choose among them. He ruled a great square on the wall of the registry and set each account against each of the others in turn, and in every compartment of that square he wrote how far he dared believe that those two pages told of the same field. Where two accounts stood together and he trusted the standing, he set a full stroke. Where the words agreed but he suspected the agreement, a broken stroke. Where he could not yet say, a single bare point, and he passed to the next compartment. Fifteen small beliefs, and not one of them the size of another.

The intendant did not leave the square to the valley. He sent to the capital for a doctor, a man none of the six had ever met. The doctor came by the evening coach, declined the ridge, declined the field, declined the farmers waiting at the fence with their caps in their hands. He sat a single afternoon in the registry, read the six leaves and the square in one sitting, never once rising from the chair, and wrote what he wrote. He measured nothing a second time, and no one measured him. Of him the record says only that he came by the evening coach and left by the morning one.

What the doctor wrote was copied in a fair hand and nailed to the church door. After that day, whoever asked about the field was answered with the paper on the church door. The six leaves were bound behind it, the square was kept rolled in the registry, and in the margin of the paper, in a small careful script, stood the brothers' oath about the lids. The intendant, reviewing everything, praised the margin above all the rest. A record that confesses its own flaw, he told the clerk, will be believed longer than a clean one. The clerk wrote that down too.

The farmers were never sent for. One of them, at the inn that winter, began to tell the field as it had been in the flood year — where the water had stood, what the ground had done under it — and the room went kindly quiet, and a hand pointed through the dark at the church door. He was not scolded. He was simply no longer the sort of witness the valley used: he had not begun from nothing; he had lived beside the field for forty years; nothing in him was fresh. And the field itself said nothing, as it always had. It had never learned to write, and no one would read it again except through the six, the square, the margin, and the paper on the door.
