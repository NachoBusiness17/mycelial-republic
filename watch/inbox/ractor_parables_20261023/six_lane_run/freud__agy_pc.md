# The Six Alcoves of October

Doctor Vane was summoned to the sanatorium in late October because the household kept suffering the same nocturnal sleepwalking, and what a house repeats without understanding is a memory it has not yet agreed to confess. Every previous inquiry had failed because the servants repeated the polite doctrine of the parlor while their hands performed the old compulsion in the dark. To hear the underlying drive without the flattery of habit, Vane forbade anyone from consulting the old casebooks. Before dawn, each of the six examiners was made to wash his slate in cold well-water until not a single stroke of yesterday's ink remained, and each was handed a wax-sealed slip bearing the exact same twelve words of inquiry, copied by a single steady hand so that not a comma or emphasis differed between them.

The six who received the sealed slip were drawn from deliberately unequal stations, lest a shared upbringing masquerade as an objective truth. The first was the heavy-browed professor from the metropolitan academy, whose counsel was the most expansive and costly in the province. The second was a hired locum who had arrived that morning in place of an absent colleague, carrying a borrowed guild-signet upon his watch-chain to pass the porter's gate. The third and fourth were twin brothers of the ambulatory order—men trained to walk the corridors and open cabinet drawers with their own hands, one quartered at the country cottage by the orchard and the other at the town study. The fifth was a mendicant scholar from the open square who worked without fee, and the sixth was the quiet apothecary who dwelt permanently in the vaulted cellar beneath the kitchen and never crossed the outer threshold.

Because a shared rumor is the quickest defense against an unwelcome truth, Vane locked the six men into separate wainscoted alcoves lined with thick horsehair felt. No speaking tube joined the chambers; no inkhorn, blotting sand, or tallow dip was passed from one alcove to the next while their chalks moved across the slates. Within those muffled walls, each examiner was left alone with the raw appetite of the question and the private defenses of his own training, unable to borrow courage or phrasing from his neighbors. What emerged from a sealed room would have to stand as an independent dream, stripped of the comforting rehearsal by which a household persuades itself that its alibi is unanimous.

Yet the censor at the threshold is never so vigilant as the official story claims, and what is repressed in the ceremony betrays itself through a slip of the hand. Because the two brothers of the ambulatory order possessed the liberty to walk into the vestibule and fetch their own blank folios from the chest, both had glanced down at the six pasted name-tags arranged upon the tray before the bolts were shot. Neither brother read a syllable of another man's testimony, yet the mere sight of who else had been summoned altered the posture of their defense. Both wrote with a guarded, sidelong stiffness, anticipating the rival voices they knew were behind the wall, and when Vane recognized that twin hesitation in their margins, he inscribed the breach plainly at the foot of the case record rather than pretend the corridor had been blind.

When the six slates were carried out at dusk, Vane spread a broad linen cloth ruled into a grid of squares across the library table. In his discipline, a single witness naming a fear was never more than an anecdote; a compulsion could be named a symptom only when at least three voices struck the same buried knot independently. Across the rows and columns where their answers met, he refused to mark a crude agreement or denial. Instead, he shaded every intersecting square in three graded depths of charcoal—black where the testimony was spoken with unshaken conviction, grey where the witness hedged behind a defensive qualification, and pale ash where the claim was only a tentative conjecture—so that the pattern of agreement carried the exact weight of its certainty.

Vane did not permit himself to deliver the final interpretation, knowing too well how easily the physician who lays out the cloth falls in love with his own arrangement. Instead, a solitary magistrate of the mind from the deep southern valley was admitted to the library after the lamps were lit. By rule, this visitor was granted no conversation with the six witnesses, no second sitting, and no opportunity to revise his first impression. Standing alone above the checkered cloth, he read the entire array of shaded squares in a single unbroken breath, pronounced the household's hidden law once to the waiting scribe, and laid his finished scroll upon the mantelpiece before walking back into the autumn dark.
