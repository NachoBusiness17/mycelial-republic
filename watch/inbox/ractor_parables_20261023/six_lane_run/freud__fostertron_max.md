# The Six Copies

The inscription on the mill's lintel had been feeding on damp for forty years, and the miller, whose father had cut it, decided to learn what it said before the last letter went the way of the first. He sent for six strangers, and his daughter copied out the request six times in her own hand, word for word, on six cards of the same size, so that no card held a word the others lacked and no card wanted one. I was lodged above the stable that season, and the miller, who knew my taste for small accidents, asked me to watch the six and tell him afterward what repeated.

Each stranger was given a room in the granary. The rooms were swept the evening before, and the paper in them was new. Whatever each man had written in his life stayed outside the threshold; the walls were thick; the six had never worked at the same bench; and there was not so much as an inkwell that served two rooms.

The eldest arrived first, with a case of pens that filled a small trunk and a hand that had never once hurried. The second was not the man the miller had written to; that one was kept away by weather, and in his place came a substitute, entering on a ticket of stamped silver that someone had bought at full price at the gate. Then came two who could not be kept in rooms — the cards bound their pens, not their feet — and after them a boy who had been admitted for nothing and knew it, and a quiet man who asked only for a table and then set out, from his satchel, his own candle, his own water, his own ink, and touched nothing else the house offered.

The two restless ones did their reading on their feet. To judge the worn letters they climbed to the chute, and the way to the chute passed the shelf where the finished sheets lay drying, each folded once and marked on its fold with the writer's name. Not a word inside could be read — the folds were pasted with flour — but a name asks nothing of a reader, and I watched both of them slow on that stair, the way a man slows on a stair he has been told has rotted. The elder of the two went by twice.

In the office the daughter stretched a cloth ruled into squares, a row for each hand and a column for each word of the recovered line, and in every square she set the word that hand had given for that place. Where a word stood in three rows or more, she pressed the ink in full; where it stood in two, she thinned it to a wash; where it stood in one, it went down in pencil. "Three hands," she said, not looking up, "or it stays thin." Against the two rows of the restless ones she set a small standing sign — these hands passed the shelf of names — and where those two agreed with each other and with no one else, the wash went down paler still.

The miller wanted the cloth judged once and judged whole. A single examiner came up from the town, read the six copies and the cloth from breakfast to dusk, asked nothing twice, wrote one page, signed it, and left his own stone on it in the office, so that anyone might lift the stone and read what he had found, doubt and all — the sign against the restless rows stood on the page as plainly as the darkest ink. Offered a bed, he walked home by lantern; asked whether he might be sent for again if the household fell to arguing, he said the page would not improve with a second sitting.

I stayed one more day, out of habit; my trade has always been the small accidents, and the cloth was richer in them than any dream I have ever taken down. Six men deaf to one another writing the same word into the same square is not luck — that is the stone speaking through whatever hands will hold still for it. On the last morning I stood under the lintel with the eldest, who had come to take his leave of it. He read the recovered line easily and stumbled only at the one word that stood in pencil, the word a single hand had offered and no second had confirmed — as if his throat already knew which words three strangers had paid for and which one man had only dreamed.
