# The Six Cabins on the Breakwater

On the twenty-third morning of the tenth month, the bailiff of the upper estate cut a single horn stencil concerning the threshold custom and declared its meaning plain enough to survive any ear. Old Marrow, who kept the gallery chair above the breakwater, did not argue with the bailiff's hope. Before noon he struck six impressions from that one horn plate, not a comma altered between them, and carried them down the pier to six timber cabins above the tide.

Each cabin held a reader of a different station and craft. In the first worked the master chronicler of the great furnace, accustomed to heavy brass and long breath. In the second waited a chartered deputy sent in place of an absent guildsman, admitted by a borrowed silver tally paid for at the harbor steps. In the third and fourth worked twin field-surveyors of the same brotherhood, one from the orchard cottage and the other from the counting-house desk, both permitted to leave their benches to fetch compasses and twine. In the fifth sat a commons scribe from the open fish-market who took no coin for his labor. In the sixth, down in a salt-dry vault cut off from the harbor bells, worked a cloistered brother who used only tools made within his own chamber. Before the sheets were handed in, apprentices swept every cabin bare of old drafts, laid out clean vellum and new charcoal, and dropped heavy cedar partitions into their grooves so no murmur or gesture could pass between stalls.

Halfway through the afternoon, the twin field-surveyors stepped out by their back hatches into the tool-shed to dress their dividers at the whetstone. Hanging on a cedar rack beside the oil-cans were the six branded leather pouches prepared for the evening collection, each stamped on the flap with the title of its cabin. Neither surveyor touched a pouch or glimpsed another's vellum, and both returned to their stalls in silence. Watching from the gallery, Marrow saw their eyes linger on the rack. He did not halt the afternoon's work or hide the mishap; he took his knife and notched the sighting across the top beam of the day's tally so anyone weighing the harvest later would know the two surveyors had seen the labels.

When the tide turned and the six vellum sheets were brought up to the gallery, the bailiff reached out to sort them into those that had obeyed his meaning and those that had erred. Marrow drew the bundle back. In the furnace-chronicler's sheet and the chartered deputy's lines, the bailiff's premise stood intact, rendered in the high grammar the upper estate favored. The commons scribe from the fish-market accepted the boundary rules the stencil described, yet bent their terms to protect the net-menders' path, bargaining with the frame while keeping its bones. The cloistered brother in the salt-vault stripped away the ceremonial preambles altogether, reading the threshold custom against its grain as a schedule of locks rather than a promise of shelter. None had misread the horn plate; each had poured the same cut words through the vessel of his own trade.

Marrow spread a checkered sailcloth across the gallery trestle—twelve ribs of cord running lengthwise for the claims inside the stencil, crossed by six chalked columns for the cabins on the pier. Into every square where a cabin's vellum met a claim, he laid one of three markers: a flat disk of lead where the reading rested squarely on the cut words of the stencil, a split ring of horn where one reading pulled against another's testimony, and a hollow pebble of chalk where a cabin asserted what the stencil neither proved nor denied. Across the cloth a pattern appeared that no single cabin could have seen alone—four rows solid with lead from edge to edge, three rows striped with horn where the surveyors parted from the vault, and two rows pale with chalk where every reader had guessed at what the bailiff left unsaid.

Even then, Marrow refused to sum the cloth himself, knowing that the man who tends the gallery shares the habits of the pier. He rolled the six vellum sheets together with the marked cloth, sealed them in one pitched cylinder, and lowered them by rope to the fathom-judge whose cutter anchored at the mouth of the bay. That judge held no speech with the cabins and asked no second question; by the light of a single lantern he read the whole bundle through in one sitting and sent up a stitched parchment naming the four ribs that had held fast across every craft, together with the notch recording what the two surveyors had seen in the tool-shed. Marrow pinned that parchment to the post, then turned his chair toward the mudflats beyond the breakwater, where the cockle-gatherers walked home through the surf—reminded that even when six benches on the pier agree, their chart never captures how the bell sounds outside the seawall.
