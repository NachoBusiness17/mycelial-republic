# The Same Letter Six Times

The syndic of the cold valley had one question, and she meant it to be asked of the world exactly once, six times over. She wrote it out by lamplight and made the clerk copy it five times, and she set every copy beside her own until the words matched to the last comma. Each sheet was folded, waxed, and stamped with her ring. What the question was, the town never learned; the syndic said only that all six carried the same one, and that this sameness was the whole of the errand.

Then the walkers, chosen from six doors. The champion of the great eastern house came first, a man who had read every book on his mother's shelves and wore new boots for the road. In his shadow came the stand-in — the guild had promised a second walker, but the second had taken ill, so a journeyman walked in his place with the guild's stamped pass folded in his hatband. Two more were given rings of keys and the right to open any gate on the low road: they could knock at farms, borrow a coat, leave the path and return to it carrying more than they left with. The fifth had no papers and paid nothing; she was used to the public tables and to being served last, and she walked anyway. The sixth had never been out of sight of the town's own smoke; he was small, and he knew every fence on the near hills.

They were woken in six separate inns in the same dark hour, and each was handed one letter and an empty satchel. None had eaten at another's table. None was told what the others carried, or that the others carried anything at all. At the registry shed the letters hung waiting on six pegs, each peg chalked with its bearer's name. The two key-bearers came earliest, in the greyest part of the morning, to take their keys — and they stood among the pegs a little longer than the keys required, reading the chalk. The registrar saw it and said nothing, because nothing in her orders forbade looking.

Six days later the answers began to come back over the pass. The registrar did not ask which answer was true; that was not her office. She set the six sheets in a frame on the long table, each against each, and where two spoke the same thing she laid a whole coin in the square between them; where two leaned the same way without touching, a clipped coin; where they crossed, a button. Every square received its token, even the emptiest. And she noticed, without writing it down, that the six agreed best about the things the valley already said over the well at noon — where the letters touched, it was mostly on ground nobody had needed a letter to find.

When the frame was full, the town sent over the pass for the judge, an old woman with one good eye who owed the valley nothing. She took the six answers, the frame with its coins and buttons, and the syndic's original sheet, and read everything in one sitting, by a single lamp of oil, start to finish. In the night she wrote the town's account of the errand, signed and dated it, and laid it in the archive drawer. In the morning the syndic asked her to read it again, slower. The judge said a second reading would be a different errand. The account named no true walker. In its margin, in her small hand, stood one line: the two with the keys had read the chalk names before the roads divided, and whatever they carried out of the gate had not been wholly their own beginning. The coins stayed in their squares, but the margin said the squares had been leaning before they were filled.

The syndic had hoped the question would come home wearing six copies of her own answer. What came home was one question wearing six answers. Her reading of her own letter — what she had meant while the lamp burned — was copied onto a slip and put in the drawer beside the rest, marked as one among seven, no heavier than the others, and the drawer closed.

The clerk's spoiled practice sheet, folded wrong and dropped by the shed door, went over the fence in the autumn wind. A goat girl found it in the ditch, opened it without breaking any wax, answered the question out loud to her goats in words the town had never taught her, and went home before dark. There is no square in the frame for her, no coin, no button, and no line in the drawer. The drawer holds the six readings the town sent for and the one the syndic wrote herself; it cannot hold a reading nobody sent for.
