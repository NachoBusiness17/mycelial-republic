# The Same Door

On the morning the frost took the gates, the house gave six of its people one errand. Each was handed the same letter, closed with the same wax, asking the same thing of whoever broke it, and each was led from a different room in which they had slept alone. At the threshold their pockets were emptied. Nothing one had made was passed to another; nothing one had seen was spoken to another before they went. The house wanted six strangers to the errand, and it went to some trouble to manufacture them.

The six were not alike, though the letters were. The first was the strongest of the house, and he dressed for the errand as for a winter, carrying everything he might need and several things no one had ever needed, and he went slowly under it. The second owned no road; he traveled the toll way and paid at every stone, and he came back quickly and polished, with the toll-keeper's stamp faint on all he had handled. Two were given lanterns and the freedom of the corridor — only they could walk it — and a lantern is not a neutral thing: whoever carries one begins to look at what it shows, and then to speak of the looking. The fifth was a boy whose wooden token opened only the small gate, the one free to anyone; he went out light and came back light. The sixth never left at all. He kept his own hearth and did the errand from there, reporting the door as a man reports weather heard through a wall — which was less, and no one could move him off it, for nothing from outside had ever reached him.

One at a time they stood at the same door, and afterward each told what was found. All six spoke of the iron hinge, and there their tellings lay together like spoons in a drawer. Fewer agreed about the wood beyond it. The strong man's account was long and carved at the edges; the toll rider's was shorter and very smooth; the boy said only that the door was heavy and that he had pushed it as far as it would go, which was not far, and he did not pretend otherwise. The hearth-keeper gave the sound of it, and the width, as well as a wall allows. The two with lanterns gave the door its due and then gave a great deal about the reaching of it.

Those two, on their way, passed the alcove where the six satchels waited for the return, and each of them, being a person who looks, read the names stitched on the other satchels. They touched nothing. No one accused them of anything. But a name read is a room entered, and when their tellings came back, the house could not say which part had been fetched from the door and which had drifted in from the alcove. The margin of that day's record says so, in small letters, and does not say it as an excuse.

When the six tellings lay on the long table, a registrar took a fresh sheet and ruled it into squares, the six down one side and the parts of the door across the top — hinge, wood, threshold, what lay beyond. In each square where tellings met she set a mark: black ink where they spoke alike and had earned it; soft pencil where they leaned together but thinly; and in some squares nothing, left blank on purpose, since a blank can be looked at without lying. Read straight down, the sheet showed where the door was solid and where it was still mostly weather.

Then the house hired one reader from beyond the river. He came for a single evening, sat at a single lamp, read the ruled sheet and the six tellings once through, and wrote one page. He was not asked back and did not offer. The page was nailed up beside the record-room door where anyone could stand and read it, and the nail was left crooked on purpose, so the page could be taken down and another put in its place the next time the errand went out.

The letters were burned that night; being identical, they had nothing left to say. The differences among the tellings remained, and every difference wore the shape of its carrier's gear — the certainty as heavy as the pack, the smoothness with the toll road in it, the narrowness of the small free gate, the wall between the hearth and the world. In the morning the rooms were swept, the lanterns trimmed, the sheet ruled again, and the blank squares were left exactly as they were.
