# The Six Lamps

The village of Ashfall lay in a fold of the mountain where morning came late and left early, and the people had long ago papered their windows with red oiled cloth. Whatever moved outside showed on the cloth as a black shape, and the people had grown fluent in the shapes — hawk from heron, wolf from dog, stranger from thief. The cloth was warm, and it asked nothing of them.

Osric kept the lamp room at the edge of the square. He had inherited the cloth, the oil stores, and one habit the village forgave: now and then he wanted to set a shape beside the thing itself. On the twenty-third day of the tenth month, before the lamps were trimmed, he wrote a single question on six slips of paper — the same words, the same hand, the same crease — and sealed each with the same thumb. The question was older than the cloth: what stands beyond the pass?

For the errand he chose six bearers of light, no two alike. The first carried a tall lamp of caravan glass, fed on oil that crossed the mountain at a price that made the merchants laugh; its flame did not waver. The second had cracked his own lamp on the step that morning and went instead with a friend's lamp, its brass stamped with the crest of another valley, burning the friend's oil. The third and fourth were not walkers only; they carried tools in their belts and had been known to open gates, pry up stones, and carry back what they found. The fifth bore a taper that had been given away at the fair, thin as a straw. The sixth carried no lamp at all, only a coal from the common hearth in a clay pot; it had never once left the square.

The night before, each bearer slept alone in a bolted room, and none had ever carried a question for Osric before, and none was told that any other had been chosen. At dawn they were waked in the dark and brought one by one to the antechamber, where a slate hung on the wall with all six names chalked on it, because Osric was a careful man and chalked what he did. The two with tools would not sit still. They walked the walls of the little room, and before the gate opened they had read the slate down to its last name. The other four kept their eyes on their own knees and noticed nothing. When Osric came in he saw what had happened, and he did not wipe the slate. He opened his day-book and wrote beside the six names: the two with tools read the names.

They went out through the east gate one at a time, and after each the gate was barred again, so that no two bearers ever shared the road or the light. Each carried his folded slip against his chest. They came back at dusk in the order they had left, were led to separate rooms, and each wrote an account of the pass in his own hand, seeing no one and hearing nothing.

Osric spread the six accounts along the edges of the long table, one to a side, so that every account crossed every other in a square. Where two told the same thing in the same words — the same tree, the same grey ridge — he inked the square black. Where they leaned toward each other but did not touch, he used chalk. Where both had burned thin, or where their words could not be laid side by side at all, he marked the square with plain water, and the water dried paler as the night went on. By morning the table was a field of black and white and faint grey squares, and Osric had judged nothing.

He would not read the field himself; he had folded the question, and his eye would favor his own crease. Once a year a pilgrim came down the pass road, and that evening she was in the village. Her lantern was of the same caravan glass as the tall bearer's, only smaller. She sat once at the table, read the whole field in a single sitting, and wrote one page: where the six lights had stood together, where they had split, and which of her own sentences she would not swear to. She pinned the page to the post by the well, and Osric cut the day and the month into the wood beneath it. The cooper ran after her with a question, and she called back over her shoulder that she came once a year, and was already down the road.

The villagers gathered at the well, and those who could read read the page aloud to those who could not, and all went home eased. That evening the shapes moved on the red cloth as they always had, and were read as they always were. A child asked Osric whether the pilgrim had ever stood on the pass herself. Osric looked at the east gate, still barred, and at the slate, still carrying its six names. He said that her lantern was caravan glass, like the first bearer's. The child waited for the rest. The cloth flickered — wolf from dog — and nobody unbarred the gate.
