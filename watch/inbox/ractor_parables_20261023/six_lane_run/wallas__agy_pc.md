# The Sixfold Hunger of the Assay

On the twenty-third day of the tenth month, the autumn grain was already Measured in the bins, yet the master of the hill-estate could not rest. An institution is built upon a premise of what men actually want, not what they argue in council, and the master hungered for a premise that did not depend on the flattery or habit of a single counselor. A lone adviser always bends his reasoning to feed his own standing; therefore the strategist resolved to ride six separate hungers at once, treating the desires of different stations as fixed data to be weighed side by side. He summoned six drafters of markedly different provenance to the stone assay-house: the guild’s chief master-craftsman, heavy with honours and costly training; a hired merchant-factor carrying a purchased warrant to stand in the place of an absent lord; two roving bailiffs trained in the same brotherhood—one weathered from the saddle, one ink-stained from the counting-desk; a young commons-scribe who worked the open market without fee for the sheer appetite of proving his craft; and a quiet cellar-monk fed entirely on the estate’s own barley, who never crossed the outer ditch.

The privacy instinct is strong in any creature asked to name what it truly sees, yet privacy in an old workshop is easily counterfeited by shared custom. Therefore, before the bell struck noon, each of the six was led into a newly lime-washed booth from which every scrap of yesterday’s parchment, charcoal, and rumour had been scrubbed away. The master knew that memory in an old servant is often only habit dressed as judgment, and he wanted their first syllogism to begin from raw appetite rather than borrowed premise. Through six brass slots pushed at the same instant, each worker received a wax tablet stamped with the exact same question: what is the true load-bearing wall of this household’s power, and what hunger keeps it upright? The oak partitions between the booths were bolted fast; no speaking-horn, no messenger, and no passing shadow was permitted to carry a syllable from one chamber to the next while the tallow burned down.

Yet the machinery of any enterprise has seams where human nature presses through. Four of the workers sat still where they were placed, waiting for clean vellum and wax to be handed to them through the slot. The two roving bailiffs, however, were accustomed by the habits of their brotherhood to walk the passage and fetch their own ink-horns, straight-edges, and drying-sand from the vestibule cupboard before shooting their bolts. As they stepped to the press, their eyes fell upon six empty leather satchels hanging on the pegs, each stitched on the outside with the title and origin of one of the day’s six witnesses. Neither bailiff could open another’s satchel, for all hung empty awaiting the finished scrolls, and neither spoke a word; yet as they returned to their booths and fastened their latches, both carried into their solitude the knowledge of who else had been called to the test, and the quiet social instinct to measure their own voice against the company named on the hooks.

At dusk the six scrolls were drawn from the slots and spread before the steward of the table upon a broad checkered cloth divided into squares. Down one margin the steward inscribed every appetite and claim the witnesses had named; across the top he set the six witnesses in their order. He did not merely mark whether two voices agreed, for agreement born of timid guessing has no weight beside agreement wrung from necessity. In each square of the cloth he laid a counter to mark the heat and certainty of that witness’s testimony—heavy lead where a claim was asserted as fixed nature, scored brass where it was offered as probable custom, and pale chalk where it was only a passing suspicion. To run against the grain of a row heavy with lead would be to legislate against the weather; where four or five lead counters settled into the same line, the cloth sagged visibly along that seam, showing where rival hungers had struck the same immovable datum.

The master did not call the six back to debate their differences, knowing that men gathered in a room soon abandon the search for what is wanted in order to satisfy the sharper desire of prevailing over their neighbours. Instead, he sent the checkered cloth and the six scrolls down to the solitary sounder who lived beside the deep quarry-pool—an examiner hired to read the whole cloth at a single sitting, without pause, second audience, or reply. In one unbroken watch before midnight, the sounder weighed the rows of lead, brass, and chalk, noted on the margin that the two roving bailiffs had seen the named satchels on the vestibule pegs, and bound his single parchment of findings into the estate’s permanent archive, leaving the six booths silent and the master’s hunger fed.
