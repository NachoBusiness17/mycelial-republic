# The Door in the Orchard Wall

The master of the valley had one question and six roads. Behind his orchard wall stood a door that no one living had opened, and he wished to know it — but not, chiefly, for itself. He wished to know whether six who were sent alone would come back telling of the same door. So he had a scribe copy the question six times, letter for letter, each copy carrying the same words and no others, and he chose six to bear them.

He chose them strangely, and on purpose. The first was his own strong son, fed all his life at the big table and never refused. The second was a cousin lent for the season by a richer house, carrying that house's gold ring; at every inn the ring was seen before the cousin was. The third and fourth were hired walkers, paid to go wherever the road led and to look into things as they went — looking was their trade, and they practiced it whether or not anyone had asked. The fifth was a wanderer who had asked for no fee and been promised none. The sixth had never in his life left the village square, and knew the orchard wall only from the near side.

Each set out at first light from a different inn, each had slept alone, and each carried nothing but his errand — no letter, no gossip, no whisper that five others rode other roads. The roads did not cross, and the master had seen to it. What the son wanted was to be found faithful. What the cousin wanted was the ring to be admired. The walkers wanted the road itself, which is a wider hunger than any errand. The wanderer wanted bread, and the sixth wanted to be home by dark.

They returned at dusk and gave their accounts one at a time, each into the master's ear alone. The accounts were not the same, and they were not wholly different.

The master's steward was a weaver. She took the six accounts, stretched each as a thread on her loom, and crossed every thread against every other — the son's word against the cousin's, the cousin's against the first walker's, through all fifteen crossings — and at each crossing she tied a knot, and the knots were not alike. Where two threads agreed and neither had a reason to agree, she tied the knot three times. Where two agreed and either might merely have been guessing, she tied it once. Where two agreed and she could not say why, she laid the threads side by side and ran a pale cord through them, so that whoever read the cloth later would know to carry that crossing loosely. The cloth came off the loom heavy at its center, where the knots were thickest, and gauzy at its edges.

Now, the hired walkers, being walkers, had passed the inns before first light and had read the names chalked on the doors — the son's name, the cousin's, the wanderer's. They had heard no word of what the others said; they knew only who had been sent. When the steward learned of it, she did not cut the knowledge out of the cloth. She wove a thin dark thread along the whole edge of the two walkers' threads, so the flaw rode in the border where any eye could find it, and she taught the household to read the border first.

Last, the cloth was folded and carried over the pass to the aunt who reads once. By an old law of the mountain she takes one question from a house in a season: the whole cloth goes up in a single journey, she looks at it a single time, and what she says is written on the back and never amended, whatever comes. She looked a long while, said that the center held and that the pale crossings should be carried exactly as they were tied, and closed her door.

The master hung the cloth in the hall where the household passed it daily, pale cords and dark border and all. Of the door itself he was heard to say almost nothing. But that same night he sat at his table with the scribe again, drawing roads — more than six now — and thinking less about doors than about which of his carriers he would trust with the next one.
