# The Cartographer of the Twin Sluices

At the outer wicket of the Conservatory, where the road narrows to a single mule's breadth, a brass spindle no larger than a walnut hummed upon a taut wire. Every carrier coming from the salt-flats had to rest his crate upon its pan before the bar lifted. The little spindle cost almost nothing to keep spinning, only a drop of tallow at dawn, yet its tremor sorted lawful burdens from deadweight in an instant. Only beyond that narrow wicket did the Conservatory open into the Great Rotunda, where twelve wide arcades branched outward like the ribs of a fan. When a silk-factor cut a postern directly into the third arcade to divide his loads before the humming spindle, the wardens bricked his breach before noon.

Inside the Rotunda, Inspector Vane stood beside the porcelain channel where the Conservatory kept its charter. Two opposing sluices, one fed from the western cistern and one from the eastern, drove dyed water headlong against each other down the trough. Where the two currents met and passed through one another, a braided crown of white foam stood motionless in the center. The water itself never paused, racing ceaselessly toward the drains, yet the crown held the unchanging contour of a seven-petaled lily. A new deputy, bred on nostalgia for solid origins, tapped the porcelain floor beneath the trough, certain an iron mould was bolted to the bed. Vane told him to save his knuckles: there was no original beneath the surface, only the figure generated by ceaseless motion, governing the estate more strictly than stone.

Unconvinced, the deputy shut the eastern lever, thinking a single stream would simply yield a smaller lily. The moment the eastern current ceased and the western water ran alone in one direction, the seven petals vanished into a flat, hurried sluice chasing its tail toward the grate. Only when Vane drew the eastern lever back, driving the second current straight into the teeth of the first, did the stationary lily snap back into place. One stream could only flee; it took two opposing tides to manufacture stillness.

In the first arcade, the Assay Vault issued tallies for the grain caravans. The master striker worked before a long bronze die whose upper nine-tenths lay packed in river-ice. That chilled preface bore the house emblem, weights, and covenants, and was never re-carved. Out of every thousand wax strips pressed across the table, nine hundred and ninety-five took their whole authority from that frozen head-cast in a single cold kiss, passing straight to the couriers. Only five times in a thousand, when a caravan carried strange cargo at the end of its roll, did the striker heat a stylus to grave the brief tail-piece by hand.

In the second arcade lay the Aviary of Mimes. Here, generations of crested finches bred, crossed, and perished without rest; chicks hatched in spring, mingled their plumage in summer, and gave way to new broods by autumn, so no single bird endured from year to year. Yet between the cages and the feeding court hung a thin brass crescent. Every fledgling had to fly through that fixed aperture to eat; the heavy-winged and broad-shouldered fell back and were culled before pairing. The bloodlines churned and mutated, while the empty silhouette deciding which flesh survived never shifted by a hair.

In the third arcade, the Hall of Petitions stretched between high colonnades. Two long files of townspeople marched in opposite directions across the checkered pavement, each petitioner stepping from square to square at the stroke of a bell without halting. Yet high above their heads, suspended on silk cords tied between the two moving files, hung the bronze tablets of the statutes. Because the opposing processions pulled the cords taut as they brushed past one another, the tablets hung dead still at the peaks of tension between the marching columns. The citizens flowed continuously; the laws were the motionless crests where their counter-marches met.

On the seventh day, the deputy returned to the porcelain channel carrying a silver cast of the lily. He had engraved every microscopic bubble and ridge into the metal, convinced that exhaustive detail made his copy truer than the restless foam, and hoping that if he wedged it into the trough to steady one trembling petal, the figure would at worst shrink slightly around his prop. Vane did not stop him; a cartographer watches where the simulacrum devours its pretext. The instant the silver tip touched the seventh petal, the lily did not grow smaller or dimmer. Detail was no refuge: the tension broke, and the entire stationary crown came wholly undone at once, unspooling into blind, slapping water.
