# The Still Points

The surveyor was sent by the guild to draw the floor of the long hall. He was told only this: the hall had never once been empty. Two processions walked it day and night, one going north and one going south, and they passed through each other like water through water, and no one could say who had first ordered it. Wages were paid for walking, and the walkers collected them.

Where the streams crossed, the flagstones were worn into pale ovals, polished patches set in a figure that ran the length of the hall. The oldest doorkeeper put his hand on one and said it had not moved a thumb's width in his lifetime. The surveyor measured for a week and found it true: every walker traveled, and the wear did not. A northbound woman laughed at his instruments and told him she had crossed nine thousand times and never seen the hall at all, only the shoulders going by.

In a side colonnade a merchant had once tried the trick alone — a single procession, hired from his own purse, circling dawn to dusk. The stones under it darkened evenly, like any road. Nothing stood out of it, nothing stood still, and he let the men go and returned to the two streams, which cost less and polished what no one line of feet could touch.

Three houses opened off the hall.

The first was the copy house. Its scribes never lifted their pens and were paid by the page, yet the master's treasure was a wall of drawers holding every opening the trade had ever used, and he paid best for pages that needed no writing at all. Every commission began with the same salutation, unchanged as long as the drawers had existed. The original of it had burned a generation ago; no buyer had ever returned a copy, and when the surveyor asked which drawer held the true text, the master answered pleasantly that the question had retired. In a good week a thousand pages left the house; the surveyor counted drawers opening and closing and made it nine hundred ninety-five that went out entire, lifted and stamped, and five written new. The pens moved without end. The words, almost never.

The second was the bird court. The warden's cages filled and emptied with the seasons, generations deep, birds without end, and every season the sorting fell the same way: the same tenth kept, chosen by the same marks of shoulder and throat, the rest freed at the west window. The surveyor asked how the fashion in birds had changed over the years. The warden looked at him with courtesy and said the birds changed all the time; that was what birds were for.

The third had no walls. Companies of benches traveled from town to town in the wake of the crowds, and the bench-captain explained his arithmetic: rows were freight and went wherever the people were; but the ordinances — where a crier might stand, where silence held, where money could change hands — were posted always at the pale ovals, at the still marks where the two streams met. Every town received different benches and the same posts.

One evening men dragged a wagon across a pale oval and left it there. The surveyor expected the figure to fade at that point, to thin like a stain. Instead the streams stumbled around the obstruction, the interlace loosened, and the crossings came undone one after another down the whole length of the hall, like stitching pulled, until the floor was only walking, any direction, no brighter anywhere than anywhere else. The wagon was hauled off at midnight; by morning the processions had found each other and the ovals were returning, faint and exact. The doorkeeper swept around the surveyor's boots and said that nothing in the hall wore out; it only held, or it didn't.

At the north end the hall ran wide as a marketplace, but no one entered the width freely. Before it stood a narrow wicket kept by a small porter whose whole office was a hum — one note, ceaseless, like a kettle finding its register. He never opened a crate. He set each load in his hand-scale, priced it, waved it through or turned it aside; the judgment cost a copper and a breath. Before his post everyone stood in one file; beyond it the ways branched into many, and only there did the carts go separately. He walked out and back: no road divided until well past the hum. The wicket had no keyhole; he looked twice. On the one afternoon the porter lost his voice to a chill, the door forgot its trade — nothing bound it, and nothing passed — and a lord's stamped paper leaned against it as uselessly as a shoulder until the note came back, a little cracked, and the door swung on the first bar of it.

The surveyor drew the figure faithfully and sent it to the guild. Later he heard that a new hall in the south had been laid with the figure already cut into its floors, pale ovals chiseled in advance, walkers hired to walk until the stone came true to the carving. He took out his drawing that night and could not settle which floor was teaching the other. He folded it and went to bed; passing the wicket in the morning he found himself humming, and the porter, without looking, hummed back in tune.
