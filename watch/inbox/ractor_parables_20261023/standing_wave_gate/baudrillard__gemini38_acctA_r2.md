# The Threshold of the White Ridge

Laurent reached the canal pavilion at midday, where the dry plateau broke toward the salt basins. The structure was neither a fortress nor a sanctuary, but an engine of pure circulation that had long forgotten what goods it was built to house.

At the portal stood an iron turnstile driven by a fist-sized coil that spun with a dry, quiet hum. It consumed almost no tallow to turn, yet its copper arm pressed against every pack and pannier before anyone was permitted to step across the flagstones. If a bundle was too dense or cluttered with irregular scrap, the latch remained dropped; if the weight fell within the narrow measure, the latch lifted without inquiry. Outside, travelers stood pressed in a single, unbending line. Only after their bundles had cleared that humming latch were they permitted to step into the great rotunda, where dozens of vaulted passages branched into the gloom; until that threshold took their measure, no one was permitted to scatter.

Inside the gallery, the floor was split by a polished sluice. Laurent leaned over the bronze railing to watch the water. Two counter-directed surges, fed from opposite cisterns at the east and west ends of the hall, raced directly against each other. Neither stream yielded, neither slackened, and neither pooled into a basin. Where their surfaces collided, three motionless ridges of foam rose above the stone. The water itself was in furious, unbroken flight, every droplet hurtling through the sluice at racing speed, yet the white crests did not drift by an inch. They hung in the air with the cold immobility of carved alabaster. The substance was in perpetual transit, yet the silhouette was absolute; the simulation of stone was more durable than any stone the canal had ever washed.

Beneath the east colonnade, the grain brokers conducted their trade without silver or grain. On a long trestle rested a single slate inscribed with a chilled, unalterable preface, carved once decades earlier. The clerks did not read the petitions brought by the merchants. Nine hundred and ninety-five times in a thousand, the incoming manifest repeated the exact cadence of that frozen prologue, and the clerk applied an uninked seal to the margin without opening the pouch or performing a fresh audit. The velocity of the trade relied entirely upon the fact that almost nothing was ever newly composed.

Past the arcade, in the central courtyard, stood the high stone aviary. Flocks of swallows nested within narrow slotted cages open to the desert wind. Laurent observed that the life of the enclosure was violent and brief: aged birds tumbled into the dust each morning, fledglings cracked through their shells by noon, and not a single organism survived three seasons in the draft. Yet the shape of the cloud they formed between the eaves never altered. The cruel geometry of the stone slats struck down any swallow whose wings beat wide or crooked, ensuring that while the living material perished and was replaced without sentiment, the outline of the flock remained immune to time.

Along the main nave, the population of the district walked in continuous files. They did not gather in assemblies; they simply marched, entering through the southern portico and disappearing through the north. The ordinances of the precinct were not inscribed upon the pillars. Instead, the magistrates stood precisely at the points of greatest sway, where the walking crowd swung widest from wall to wall. At those points of maximum amplitude, where the lateral motion was violent, the spoken command remained completely still. The travelers obeyed it not because it carried moral weight, but because the rhythm of their own procession pinned them against the decree as they passed.

Near the sluice, a caravan master grew weary of the rhythm. Believing the white crests were merely heavy foam that could be parted, he wedged a broad timber into the western current to divert a fraction of its volume. The motionless ridges did not gradually subside. They did not soften, diminish, or slowly ebb. The instant the opposing surge lost its reciprocal balance, the entire structure tore itself apart in a single deafening rupture. The crests detonated into spray, the current broke over the balustrades, and the hall filled with violent, directionless water. A system sustained by opposition could not fade; once disturbed, it unraveled entirely.
