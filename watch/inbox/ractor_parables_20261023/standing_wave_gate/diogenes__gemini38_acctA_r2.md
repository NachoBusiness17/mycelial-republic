# The Salt Weir

The warden arrived at the salt weir in a wool coat dyed saffron, carrying a tin cylinder packed with parchment decrees. The midday sun baked the estuary mud until it smelled of boiled crabs. Beside the ditch, an old watchman sat naked to the waist inside an overturned wine vat, whittling a willow peg while an unlit horn lantern rested on the gravel by his bare feet.

When the warden unrolled his sheepskin to read the titles granted by the grand council, the old man did not look up from his knife. "You bring five pounds of ink to a ditch, friend," the watchman said, his voice dry and warm as cracked clay. "What are you still asking the world to hand you that you could drop into the silt right now? The water has no ears for names. If you want to see how the throat is kept, wash your face and look at the wood."

At the mouth of the stone cut, where the road narrowed so only a single cart could pass, an iron spindle hummed on a wooden axle. Turned by a tiny paddle in the froth, it buzzed steadily like a locust. It asked for neither seals nor permits. As each cart passed, the spindle tripped a balance against a stone weight; if the freight was true, the latch lifted with a light click and the cart rolled on, but if a load was false, the wheel caught and tipped the crate into the brambles. A barefoot boy sat beside it, clearing forty wagons while the warden fumbled for his seal.

Only after a wagon cleared that buzzing spindle did the passage open. Behind the narrow mouth lay a wide, six-arched vault where the single path split and fanned out into a dozen quiet channels leading to the estate’s warehouses. Outside the gate, no wagon was permitted to turn aside or widen its track; the dispersal existed only within the hall, strictly downstream of the humming peg.

In the middle of the channel, mountain water fell through the sluice with the roar of a stampede. Every drop tore past in an unbroken rush of foam, never pausing for an instant. Yet directly between the stone piers stood an arched ridge of clear water, stationary as a block of green jade. A white moth hovered over it and landed on its peak without being swept downstream. The water making it was running at full gallop, yet the shape itself never moved by a finger's breadth.

The warden leaned over the railing and saw why. Through a low culvert beneath the southern bank, a dark, heavy tide was driven up from the sea, rushing directly into the mouth of the descending river. Two great torrents met chest to chest. When an apprentice, seeking to save the seawall from scour, dropped a wooden shutter across the culvert to stop the tide, the jade ridge collapsed in the same breath, melting into a muddy, ordinary rapid. The old man laughed from his vat. "A single torrent is only a runaway dog," he said. "It takes two beasts locking jaws to make a wall out of water."

A drayman tried to force a wagon through the gate before the latch fell. An oak beam slipped from the tailboard and struck the water, catching sideways between the opposing currents. The jade crest did not slowly sink, nor did it dwindle like an evening shadow. The instant the balance of the opposing rushes was bruised, the entire wave unwove in a single crack, tearing into wild foam that swamped the timber wharves before flattening into brown slosh.

Down at the tally-steps where the salt barges tied up, the clerks moved without hurry. Nine hundred and ninety-five bags in every thousand bore the dark, frozen brand burnt into the hemp before the winter freeze. The tallyman touched them with a peeled willow wand and let them float past without lifting a weight. Only the five strange sacks in every thousand, bound with fresh hemp or bearing an unknown dye, were dragged onto the copper pan to be weighed grain by grain.

In the shallow pools along the weir's flank, the keeper showed the warden the breeding troughs. Millions of water-bugs and marsh-reeds were spawned each dawn, and by dusk three-quarters were culled by the herons or swept into the compost ditch. Generations vanished between dawn and dusk, yet the breadth of the bug’s shell and the knot of the reed matched precisely the shape carved into the foundation granite four centuries ago.

Along the high catwalk above the cut, two unending lines of porters carried baskets of silt in opposite directions without pausing. The overseers who gave the commands did not walk with the lines; they stood fixed only at the still spots where the opposing rumbles of the planking canceled each other out into complete quiet. When the warden tried to shout an order from the middle of the span, the violent tremor knocked the tin cylinder from his hand.
