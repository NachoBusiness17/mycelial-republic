# The Sluice of the Standing Crests

Luc stood upon the parapet of the gorge and looked down into the masonry trough. Two great channels met there, head-on. From the eastern heights a torrent poured downward in a steady surge, and from the western reservoir an equal deluge was driven back against it by heavy waterwheels. Where the two torrents collided, neither retreated. Instead, the water reared into six sharp, motionless crests that hung between the granite walls like arches of green glass. The spray was violent and the roar shook the floor beneath Luc’s boots, yet the peaks did not drift by an inch. Water rushed through them at dizzying speed, but the shapes themselves never traveled. When a novice tender thought to lighten the load by shutting off the western race, the crests vanished instantly into a flat, roaring sweep that nearly battered down the embankment; only when the second opposing stream was forced back into the throat did the motionless arches re-emerge.

Along the timber catwalks flanking the flume, two unbroken lines of laborers marched in opposite directions, carrying heavy baskets of river gravel upon their shoulders. Their bare feet struck the damp planks in an unceasing, hurried cadence, forced to keep time with the pounding spray. None of them could stop, for the line behind pressed relentlessly forward. Yet at each point where a water crest stood highest in its stationary swell, an officer stood upon an anchored iron pulpit above the spray, perfectly still, shouting neither encouragement nor warning. The laborers streamed endlessly beneath these frozen stations, their faces sweating and interchangeable, while the authority of the valley remained pinned exclusively to the high crests where the opposing surges cancelled each other out.

Above the canal walls lay the terraced nurseries where winter pulse was bred against the damp rot. Luc watched an old gardener walk among the thousands of wicker beds. Each season, nearly all the seedlings decayed under the stinging mist, and only those few that developed a bitter, waxy skin were spared to drop their grain for the next planting. Generation after generation of seedlings withered and vanished into the compost pits, yet the stone grid of the beds, the height of the drainage gutters, and the iron comb that weeded out the tender leaves had not shifted by a fingernail in thirty summers. The living flesh was replaced continually, but the pattern that condemned it stood immutable.

At the lower wharf, where the boats loaded provisions for the mining towns downriver, Luc found the grand storehouse. He expected an army of clerks weighing sacks, but only two boys sat by the door. Deep within the vault stood rows of sealed, pre-formed stone bins, filled decades ago with hardened rations of uniform weight and measure. Out of every thousand crates dragged down to the barges, nine hundred and ninety-five were slid directly from these frozen stone racks without touching a beam or requiring a fresh mark of the chalk; only five crates of odd cargo were brought before the scales to be inspected. The whole commerce of the river was sustained by drawing endlessly from what was already fixed, permitting the illusion of swift trade without the friction of thought.

Before Luc could enter the central compound where the water-masters gathered, he was directed to the narrow throat in the mountain rock. The path admitted only one traveler at a time. In the wall beside the doorway, no grand sentry stood; there was only a small iron spindle, spinning on greased brass pins with a faint, monotonous hum. As each porter approached, the spindle brushed the canvas sack at his hip, testing its resistance against a counterweight with a dry click that consumed scarcely a second. If the burden was true, the latch fell back. Luc passed the humming spindle and stepped through the threshold. Only then did the rock open into a colossal, vaulted hall capable of holding an entire market. And it was only within this great expanse, beyond the humming spindle, that thirty broad ramps diverged into the mountain depths; before the gate, there had been only the single, unyielding queue.

As Luc watched the crossing, a porter stumbled upon the catwalk, dropping a heavy ash timber into the flume near the fourth stationary ridge. The timber struck the churning apex. The ridge did not tilt, nor did the disturbance merely cause the water to ripple and subside. The balance between the two torrents snapped at once. Within two heartbeats, the third arch collapsed into foam, the fifth buckled violently against the stone wall, and the entire sequence of crests disintegrated into a wild, chaotic bore that tore the catwalk from its iron stanchions and flooded the terraces. The structure had possessed no tolerance for adjustment; it was incapable of diminishing by degrees, and could only unravel entirely into ruin.
