# The Margin of the River

Ivo came down from the capital with sealed orders, a knotted cord, and a book whose margins were narrow. The town at the gorge had quarreled for a year over the crown of white water that stood in the bend below the cliffs. The river never stopped: a leaf given to the current at dawn was four miles gone by noon. Yet the crown stood at midmorning exactly where it had stood at first light, the same height, the same place, unmoved by all the water that had passed through it. Some called it a miracle and some a nuisance, and the capital, wanting neither, had sent Ivo, who wrote down only what he saw.

The miller met him at the landing, very certain. The crown was his doing, he said; his sluice above the gorge fed the river its strength, and what his sluice had raised his sluice could raise higher — for a fee he would prove it. The ferryman, who had rowed the bend daily for thirty years, said nothing. When Ivo asked the elders about the crown, each cited an elder older than himself, and Ivo followed the citations down the years until they ended on the shingle, with two girls and a rope.

One girl swung the rope alone; a ripple ran the length of it and died at the loose end, standing nowhere. Then the girls swung against each other, and in the middle of the rope a loop rose and stayed, one place, one shape, on a rope that did not otherwise stop moving. The ferryman let the rope fall. "One arm makes nothing that holds," he said. "The river comes down and the sea pushes up, and the crown stands where the two meet. Above the tide's reach you will find no crown, though the sluice feeds the same water." Ivo rowed to the head of the gorge and found it so: fast, full, shapeless water with nothing standing in it.

Then he took his census of the two waters. On the wharf sat a factor grown quietly rich. Almost every sack that reached him began with the same few marks — same port, same first lines — and he kept pages of first lines already bound, so that nine hundred and ninety-five sacks in every thousand he priced without untying, out of what he already held; his costs were small because almost nothing that came to him was new at the front. Across the water a shepherd had kept one rule of choosing for forty springs; none of the first flock remained, every beast on the hill was new, and the flock looked as it had always looked, the beasts passing through the choosing like water. In the town itself the benches changed hands yearly, strangers sat at every table, families had gone upriver and down, yet every quarrel still ended at the same stone and the market opened when it had always opened; the people crossed the square like current, and the customs stood.

Ivo refused to take either man's word. He had the miller and the ferryman each write a prediction, sealed the papers unmarked, and opened them only after the morrow. The miller had promised his sluice would raise the crest a foot. The ferryman had written: same place, same height. The morning showed same place, same height.

The miller then sent a barge of stone and men with poles to trim the crown and make it orderly, certain that a trimmed crown would be a smaller one. It was not smaller. It came apart, the whole white shape loosening thread by thread into broken, wandering water, no crest anywhere, ferries laid up three days, both currents running through the bend like water in a millrace. When the stones were at last hauled out, the crown gathered itself back into the old bend, same height, same place, as though nothing whatever had been learned.

Below the gorge the river widened toward the basin, and the basin had one gate: a stone post at the mouth. On the post a small brass spinner, no bigger than a fist, hummed day and night, turned by the flow; it lifted nothing and it never stopped. Beside it the keeper weighed each arriving cart with a hand-scale, a moment's heft and a copper, and waved it through. One road came down to the post, and no other way was offered. Past the post the ground opened onto a broad floor, and there the ways forked — a dozen of them, and none before it. Ivo stood a long while watching the little hum attend its light lifting while the wide floor waited behind.

His report was brief: the counts, the sealed papers, the three days of broken water, the post, the dozen ways. The crown required both waters, he wrote, and the miller's single sluice neither held it nor heightened it. In the margin, where the page ran out, he added only that whether the crown would stand in the thousandth year he could not say, the margin being too narrow for it.
