# The Flume at Saint-Guilhem

Master Nicole, advocate to the court at Castres, arrived at the weir of Saint-Guilhem when the sun had already slipped behind the red cliffs. A matter of water-rights had been brought before the chambers, and it was his custom to view the ground before putting his signature to any decree.

At the lower reach of the works, where the mountain race met the valley conduit in a paved channel of dressed freestone, two ridges of water stood upright. They were shaped like rounded loaves of bread, motionless and clear as glass. Master Nicole stepped close, expecting them to drift or flatten, but they did not move by a hair's breadth. Yet when he dropped a dry oak leaf into the first ridge, the leaf vanished down the sluice with the speed of an arrow, while the glassy loaf remained where it had been, neither diminished nor shaken. The water-master stood by the sluice-gate with a hooked staff. He showed the advocate that from the south, the cold discharge of the high peaks rushed downward with great force, while from the north, a great mechanical wheel pushed the river back against it with equal vigor. When the water-master laid his weight against the northern timber and checked the return flow for half a second, the two ridges did not subside or shrink into ripples; the entire structure vanished at once into a fury of white froth, tearing itself to shreds along the stones. Only when the opposing current was restored did the two glass loaves leap back into being.

Before the advocate could enter the inner yard of the estate, his carriage was stopped at the outer palisade. The barrier was narrow, admitting only one cart at a time. Above the post hung a small iron spindle that turned continuously upon an oiled pivot, giving off a dry, unbroken hum like that of a cicada. Beneath it rested an unpainted wooden balance. As each wagon rolled onto the planks, the balance dipped; if the load was modest, a light latch lifted with a click and the horse walked forward, but if the wagon groaned beneath excessive stone or lead, the latch dropped and the team was turned back into the gravel yard without another breath spent. Beyond this humming post, and nowhere before it, the single road split at once into fifty broad corridors that swept toward the brick storehouses and the upper hills.

Inside the great granary, the advocate walked between rows of cedar chests stretching into the darkness. A clerk carried a tally on sheepskin. Master Nicole asked how the estate could provision three parishes through the winter without exhausting its treasury. The clerk pointed to the first row of chests near the threshold, coated in salt and white lime, which had remained unopened and frozen since the year of the frost. Out of every thousand wagons that came for corn, nine hundred and ninety-five were filled entirely from these same forward bins, their measure having been fixed once for all, so that the deep vaults behind them were scarcely ever unsealed.

Up on the chalk terraces, the sheepfolds were filled with the bleating of the spring cull. The master shepherd held a notched wand of boxwood against the lambs. Old ewes fell into the ditch and died, while new rams were born beneath the hurdles, so that the blood of the pasture was never the same from one month to the next; yet under the edge of the wand, the curve of every fleece and the turn of every horn remained identical to those Master Nicole had seen in the drawings made by his grandfather forty years before.

When the advocate returned along the high cart-road at dusk, the evening company of vine-dressers was returning from the slopes. They walked in an unbroken procession, their boots crunching steadily upon the flints without pause. Yet along that moving column, the laughter and shouting rose only at three stone crosses beside the ditch, where the voices swelled into a roar, while in the intervals between the crosses, the men passed in absolute, unnatural silence, as though a magistrate were standing at their elbows. The men moved without stopping, but the tumult and the silence remained rooted to the roadside.

Master Nicole closed his memorandum, climbed into his carriage, and bade the driver strike the horses toward Toulouse.
