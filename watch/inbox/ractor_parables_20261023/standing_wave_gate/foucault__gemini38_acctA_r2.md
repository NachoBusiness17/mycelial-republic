# The Apparatus of the Salt Gate

The causeway across the salt marsh permits no wandering. For two leagues the road runs between deep drainage ditches where the water is black and stagnant, compelling every cart, beast, and carter into an inescapable file. Those who attempt to leave the gravel find only mud that swallows an axle. There are no side turnings, no secondary tracks through which a traveler might slip toward the warehouses. The entire circulation of the province is forced toward the narrow masonry arch of the outer barrier. Before this gate, no distribution is permitted; the road does not branch, and the wagons cannot disperse into the market grounds until they have passed the threshold itself.

At the center of the archway stands not a tribunal of magistrates, but a small iron spindle. Connected to a suspended lead counterweight, it hums with a faint, ceaseless vibration that trembles through the flagstones beneath one’s boots. A solitary youth waits on a low bench beside this trembling spindle. He does not inspect the contents of the sacks, nor does he question the travelers. His duty is brief and mechanical. Before him lies an iron notch against which each wagon tongue is pressed as it rolls through. If the iron tongue fits true against the groove, the carriage is waved forward; if the weight departs from the notch, the wheel is shunted aside into the marsh siding. This humble mechanism costs almost nothing to maintain, yet it bears the entire labor of division before the great court is reached.

Behind this threshold opens the vast vaulted concourse of the customs house, an expanse where forty arched galleries diverge toward the salt pans, the drying lofts, and the harbor quays. Here the cargo may divide, but only because the tiny spindle has already relieved the interior of the burden of suspicion. Beside the youth, an older clerk attends to the seals. He keeps on his lap a single cold brass die, cut four reigns ago with the royal cypher. Nearly every wagon that arrives—nine hundred and ninety-five out of every thousand—bears upon its canvas an identical wax impression struck from that ancient die. The clerk does not read the permits; he touches the brass die to the wax, feels the familiar crest settle within the contours, and nods. Only the rare anomalous wagon, bearing an unfamiliar cord or an unverified stamp, is pulled from the file for questioning.

Through the central gallery connecting the gate to the inner courts, the carters move in an unyielding discipline. The stone pavement is marked down its center by a raised line of blue slate. On the right, a file of laden men presses inward toward the magazines; on the left, an equal file of unladen men presses outward toward the estuary. Neither line is permitted to halt. The wardens who watch from the high cornices enforce an unbroken pace with willow wands. To an eye watching from above, the gallery appears motionless, as though cast in lead. The men and their barrows travel without respite, yet the pattern of the chamber never changes. Where the inward file brushes against the outward file at the fourth pillar, the crowd swells into an invariant mound of cloth and breath; at the eighth pillar, the space between the files opens into a permanent, empty circle where the sun strikes the floor undisturbed.

This stillness of the whole exists only because two opposing currents push against each other with identical vigor. In the winter of the great freeze, when the outward carriers were kept in their barracks and only the inward file marched into the vault, the pattern vanished at once: the inward column jammed against the far wall in a dead heap, unable to maintain the form of the hall without the pressure of its adversary.

Generations of porters have perished in these corridors, their bones carried to the marsh cemetery, their places taken by youths who know nothing of those who walked before them. Yet the geometry of the gallery remains untouched by their deaths. The men are replaced, but the points of condensation and the points of silence remain affixed to the stones. At the twelfth pillar, every generation lowers its eyes and quickens its stride; at the sixth pillar, every file widens to avoid the cold shadow of the arch. The living material is perpetually exchanged, but the configuration of obedience does not alter.

One afternoon, an inspector from the court of the governor arrived, dissatisfied with an apparatus that functioned without his active decree. Wishing to prove that the rhythm was subject to his will, he stepped onto the slate divide and thrust his cane into the spokes of a single outward-bound barrow. The balance of the gallery did not slacken gradually, nor did it settle into a gentle rest. The moment the outward motion ceased, the entire structure tore itself apart. The inward file tumbled over the halted barrow; the wagons behind crashed into one another; sacks burst, blinding the wardens with salt dust; and within three breaths the long corridor was reduced to a writhing entanglement of bodies and broken wood. The order did not decline by degrees; having lost the counter-beat that sustained it, it unraveled entirely in a single stroke.
