# The Spine of the River

Halina arrived in the crossing-town in the month the ferries ran heavy. No one had sent for her; she simply went wherever the same misfortune kept occurring, the way a doctor goes to a ward. At the inn the keeper told her the town was unlucky with its water, and the next evening he told her again, word for word, and on the third evening, when he began the same sentence, she let him finish and only then opened her notebook, because a thing said once is weather, twice is coincidence, and three times is a resident.

Each morning two boats left the opposite banks at the same hour, and where their wakes met in midstream the water stood up into a ridge that did not travel. It hissed in the same spot from dawn to dusk. The river poured through it without pause — no drop stayed, the current never rested — and the ridge itself never moved and never wore thinner. The ferrymen called it the spine. On the holy day, in the old times, only one boat had run, and the old men remembered that the water had risen then too, but the rise had traveled, flattened, and left nothing behind; a single running wake held no stillness in it. The toll-keeper on the bridge would not sell a viewing of the spine unless both boats were on the water. Two, he said, was not a preference; it was the smallest number the ridge would stand on.

One festival day a boy on the bridge dropped a boundary stone over the rail — not onto the ridge, a body-length to the side of it. The crowd waited for the spine to shrink. It did not lessen. It came apart all at once, the whole length unbraiding downstream thread by thread, and by evening the river lay flat everywhere, even in the stretches that no one living had ever seen calm. The boats crossed steadily for eleven days before the ridge stood again in its old place, as though it had merely been holding its breath.

Halina went looking for other things that behaved like the water.

On the north bank, the copy-house had cut the opening formula of every deed onto a single block twenty years before and had never once cleaned or reset it. The fresh clauses that followed were all of the work and almost none of the earnings; nineteen of every twenty coins the house took came off the unchanging block, and apprentices who complained were told to carve a faster beginning of their own someday and see what it earned.

Across the bridge a shepherd chose his breeding pairs each spring by the same old measure — quiet eye, heavy shoulder — and though not one beast in that flock had been alive when his father chose, the flock itself looked the same year after year, as if the choosing were the only animal that lived there permanently.

In the great hall the benches ran on rails and circled the floor all evening, so that no family sat twice in the same place; but the two rings of stone where the proclamations were answered never moved, and every night the crowd's answering shout swelled and died in exactly those rings, loud as bells, and went nowhere.

At the mouth of the hall, where the road narrowed to a single throat, a small engine hummed — a flywheel no bigger than a loaf of bread — and weighed each incoming wagon with a copper fee and one tilt of a plank. Nothing was opened, nothing searched twice; the weighing was kept deliberately light, lighter than any inspection, so that no wagon would think to avoid it. Behind the hum the floor widened to its full breadth, and only past that point did the aisles branch, one toward each trade. A merchant once tried to divide his goods into three lines out on the road, so his sons could each come to a separate counter; the watch made him bind everything back onto one wagon and pass the whole of it under the little wheel, and he was permitted to split his cargo only afterward, on the wide floor, where the branching began.

On her last evening Halina stood on the bridge. The toll-keeper asked her, the way strangers are asked, what she had come to see, and she said: the same thing, again. Below her the ridge held its place, saying one sentence all day and all night in water that was never the same water, and she found she could not tell whether the town kept the ridge or the ridge kept the town. In her notebook she wrote only the hour, and the fact that the innkeeper, passing behind her, had begun his unlucky sentence once more.
