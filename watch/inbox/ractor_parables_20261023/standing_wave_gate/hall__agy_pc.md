# The Wicket and the Counter-Pull

I have spent my years on the rough timber benches where the barges land, watching strangers untie what the senders knotted in the upper rooms. The masters who stamp a bale in the counting-house think the mark settles what the parcel is for, so I sit by the water and watch what hands actually do when the rope comes ashore.

Below the hills, the river pours through the stone narrows day and night without rest. Across that torrent, the ferrymen keep a web of hemp cords running hand over hand between the piers. Neither the water nor the cord ever pauses, yet in the center of the channel hangs a diamond cradle of braided rope that never drifts an inch downstream or climbs toward the source. When the apprentices once stopped the wheels at noon to rest the hemp, the diamond sank at once beneath the foam; as soon as the cords ran fast again, the cradle stood motionless over the same rock.

Later that season, a new factor arrived from the upper house, convinced that the crossing held by the strength of his wharf alone. He ordered the women on the far bank to drop their hauling-lines while his own crew threw double weight from the home pier. The moment the far bank stopped pulling back against him, the diamond cradle collapsed into the surf and washed flat against the stones. It rose out of the spray only when the women on the mud-bank picked up their lines and leaned back against his throw—one woman hauling in the exact cadence the factor called, a second keeping his beat while looping the slack around her own post to draw her skiff alongside, and a third bracing her heels to pull crosswise against his whistle. With the wharf casting outward and the bank hauling back, the cradle locked in mid-stream.

Beside the landing stood the timber-yard, where joiners dressed a thousand cedar beams each week for the boats. Rather than carving the long fore-bevel on every timber by hand, they kept an iron trough chilled with well-water at the head of the bench. Every beam was pressed first into that cold, unchanging mold; nine hundred and ninety-five times out of a thousand, the long body of the timber came away finished at a stroke, and the joiner's chisel only had to nick the last thumb's-breadth at the tail.

Higher on the slope, seed-keepers tended the barley terraces. Every autumn the stalks came up altered—short-stemmed after dry winds, dark-bearded from wild moor pollen—and no two harvests grew alike. What never changed was the copper winnowing-sieve hung in the barn. Generations of grain shifted in the husk, yet the mesh that decided which kernels fell to the millstones and which stayed for spring sowing remained identical.

Along the cobbled causeway between the water and the barns, hill-families filed past in moving lines, carrying wool down to the boats and salt up to the pastures. No family lingered on the stones past dusk. Yet at the three landings where descending wool-carters and ascending salt-bearers pressed chest to chest and argued loudest, the unwritten customs of the road stood firmer than any post, held up where the moving lines clashed hardest.

In the second month, the factor grew uneasy watching the wide rope-diamond quiver above the weir. Hoping to narrow the figure by a little, he reached out with a boat-hook and slackened a single outer strand. The diamond did not shrink or merely sag. At the first disturbed pull, tension broke across the whole channel; every knot spat its neighbor loose, and the entire web whipped into a drowned snarl from bank to bank.

When the ropes were re-strung, the elders set a narrow outer wicket before the vast, many-tabled pavilion where bales were opened. Beside the wicket stood only a small brass flywheel that hummed like a winter hive and turned a light balance-pan. Every carrier laid his bound sack on that humming pan first; in a breath, it tipped stones and wet chaff into the ditch while letting sound weight pass through. A drover from the ridge, impatient with the narrow wicket, untied his pack outside in the mud and sent ten boys running at once toward ten gaps in the palings; they jammed in the ditch, spilled their tallow, and nothing reached the floor. The woman behind him kept her load bound as one parcel until the humming pan tipped it through. Only inside the wide pavilion did sorters cut the cord and divide the goods among twenty tables—where one artisan obeyed the sender's notch, another kept the tallow but refused the price-mark, a third scraped the mark off, and no one inside ever looked back to see what the ditch had kept.
