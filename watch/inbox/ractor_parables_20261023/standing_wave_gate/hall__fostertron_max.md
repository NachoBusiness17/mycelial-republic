# The Humming Wicket

Between the granary and the town wall stood two posts with a slack rope thrown between them, and at each end a child. When one child swung alone, the shape ran down the rope, struck the far post, and died there. But when the second child swung back against the first — matched, patient, moving the opposite way — the rope's middle rose into three fixed bellies that neither traveled nor faded. The rope never once was still; the bellies never once moved. Hour after hour the children kept it up, and the figure they made at dusk was the figure they had made at breakfast: endless in its moving, changeless in its form.

Three travelers stopped at the yard that morning. The factor watched the bellies and said, stillness you can sell. The culler watched and said, what holds is what governs. The stranger watched and said, two rhythms meeting, that is all it is. The townsfolk nodded at the first two readings and copied them into the almanac. The stranger's reading was copied nowhere, and nothing of it was ever sent back to him, and the town was content.

The factor had come for the letter stall. Its keeper had spent one winter writing the first lines of every letter her trade could imagine — greetings, condolences, prices, blessings — and then wrote no new ones again. The openings lived in a chest and were sold over and over for a copper apiece, re-inked only at the name. Customers arrived all day in an endless shuffle, and not one in two hundred asked her for a line she did not already keep. Her trade moved constantly; her stock never changed.

The culler kept his flock behind the wall. Every spring the lambs were different — different faces, different fleece — and every spring the choosing was the same: the same share penned to the left, the same faults turned away, the same eye run down the line in the same order. The animals were always new; the choosing never was.

On assembly days the hall filled in rows, and the rising passed along the benches, a traveling stir that went down the floor and out the far door. But the law-reader's voice swelled always in the same three places, the same three loud spots, year in and year out. The rows traveled; the loud places never did.

A courier, late, once tried to quiet the rope as he crossed the yard — he seized a still place between the bellies with his fist, meaning to hold it a moment. The figure did not lessen. It came apart whole and at once, ran hissing into snarls from post to post, and was gone. They cut the ruined length out that evening, and for a week after, one child swung alone, and nothing held.

The town's entry was a wicket, and in the wicket a small tin weigher that hummed. It was a cheap thing, half riveted, and it did one office only: the cart came up, the hum rose, the load bore or did not bear. It never opened a bundle, never read a paper, never asked anything beyond weight. Behind it lay the wide floor of the hall, and only past the wicket — inside, beyond the hum — did the porters divide a load among many barrows. Nothing was ever split on the road.

The wicket did not answer force, and it did not answer coin. A girl waiting in the line one market day grew tired of the hum and hummed it back to the machine, note for note, and under the noise she felt two worn lines of verse, older than the paint:

*What goes out comes back changed;*
*what meets it, stands.*

She hummed the two lines as they wanted to be hummed, and the wicket lifted without being touched. The toll-keeper swore it had never once opened for him. The girl went in. Behind her the hum took up its two lines again and kept them in plain hearing, which is the last place anyone listens.

That evening the stranger left by the same road. Letters went out of the town all season — the factor's prices, the culler's tallies, the law-reader's summaries — and replies came back to all of them. None came back to the stranger. The town had liked his reading least, so it never heard it again, and never missed it; and in the yard the two children swung on, matched and opposite, holding the still figure between them without knowing what each had been told it meant.
