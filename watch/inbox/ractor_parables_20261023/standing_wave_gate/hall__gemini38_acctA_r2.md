# The Crossing at the Narrow Throat

At the outer portal of the stone storehouse, where the masonry pinched to a throat barely wide enough for a single pack-frame, a small copper bob turned on a twisted gut string with an unbroken, low hum. The gatekeeper did not ask what was inside the wicker crates, nor did he untie the oiled canvas. He merely caught the corner of each frame with a notched iron rod as the carrier stepped over the sill. If the counter-weight dipped and rose without sticking, the man was nudged forward into the interior. Those whose loads dragged heavily were turned aside into the ditch to wait for evening, but the line itself never paused. Behind this narrow throat, the ceiling vaulted upward into an immense stone chamber that swallowed the daylight.

Every bundle arriving from the lower terraces carried a length of hemp cord braided into an identical three-rib twist for the first handbreadth. The gatekeeper’s thumb brushed only that initial braid, matching its ridges against the bronze plate set into the jamb. Because ninety-nine bundles in every hundred bore that exact braid, the carriers slipped past the threshold almost without feeling the gatekeeper's rod. Only when an unbraided rope scraped the wood did the keeper drop his bar to unfasten the ties and inspect the grain beneath.

A merchant from the coast once asked why the porters were not allowed to fan out across the wide gravel yard and enter through three doors at once. The gatekeeper pointed to the humming bob above the arch. If the line scattered into separate files before the entry, the draft through the opening died away, the balance failed to register the counter-weight, and the sorting broke down. The carriers had to present themselves in a single thread at the narrow throat; only after they cleared the stone sill and entered the wide room were the twenty side arches thrown open to receive them.

Inside the great chamber, two continuous columns moved in perpetual counter-march across the flags. One column filed south from the river docks bearing wet grey slate; the other marched north from the valley carrying dry cedar poles. Neither file ever halted, yet where the two lines brushed shoulders in the center of the hall, an unchanging architecture hung in the air. At certain flagstones, the porters always swerved upward to the right, forming towering crests where the bundles bunched and lifted together; between those crests lay quiet hollows where the air hung dead, untouched by the rush of feet.

Years passed over the threshold. Young men shouldered the straps, grew thick in the shoulder, bent double with age, and gave their places to their sons. Mules stumbled in the traces and were replaced by fresh colts from the pasture. Yet though every moving heart in the two columns was replaced again and again, the living shape of the crossing never shifted by a hair: the crests remained fixed over the dark flags, the hollows stayed calm in the center, and the path of the bundles traced the same arcs through the air year after year.

The porters themselves never tarried; a man entered at the north, marched his paces through the crossing, and passed out the southern gate into the daylight. Yet the customs of the estate lived precisely where the motion was still. At the high crest, every runner called out the hour; at the quiet hollow, every man lowered his head in silence. Observers in the gallery read the spectacle through their own eyes. The estate steward believed the order proved the absolute authority of the master’s written commands; the porters believed the quiet hollow was their own secret sanctuary, preserved to catch their breath; and a traveler from the border saw only two columns of conscripts colliding under compulsion. Yet the quiet points did not belong to the master, the runners, or the traveler; they were produced only where the opposing streams met and decoded one another.

In the second year of the drought, an ambitious bailiff decided to speed the delivery of the cedar poles. He ordered the slate carriers to halt outside the southern doors for the space of three breaths, intending to give the cedar column an open floor to run faster. The moment the counter-thrust ceased, the pattern did not slowly wind down or fade into quietness. It unraveled instantly. The crests flattened into a tumbling heap, the runners crashed into each other’s loads, cedar poles shattered against the walls, and the entire order of the hall collapsed into ruin before the bailiff could draw another breath.
