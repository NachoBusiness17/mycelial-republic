# The Weir in the Cellar

When Lukas was named master of the foundry, he refused to visit the cellar and began instead at the narrow outer postern, where a fist-sized brass spinner hummed day and night on an oiled pin. Every carter arriving from the hills had to rest his axle against the spinner's tongue before the iron grille rose into the wide vaulted hall behind it; if the low hum soured by even half a tone, the gate-woman Maren tipped the load into the ditch for the price of a tallow drop, before a single furnace inside was troubled. Lukas hated the bottleneck and the brusque dismissal at the threshold. Wishing to seem generous, he cut four side-troughs into the road outside the postern so that incoming ore could be divided and poured directly into the twelve smelting-bays of the wide hall before reaching the humming brass pin. By noon, eleven bays were choked with wet shale that cracked the crucibles, and the smiths stood choking in black tallow smoke. Maren did not console Lukas when he coughed at the door; she handed him a shovel, made him look at the cracked pots he had ruined by sparing the carters her refusal, and waited until he walled up the outer troughs himself, leaving the twelve inner bays fed only by what had first survived the small humming tongue at the gate.

That evening Maren took the iron ring of cellar keys and led Lukas down the wet stair beneath the nave, where the roaring grew loud enough to rattle his teeth. Through the floor-grating he saw the great arch of water that braced the central pier of the house: a tall, curved rib of foam risen motionless in mid-air, hard enough to bear the timber props of the floor above. Lukas smiled, relieved to find something solid and at rest in the dark. Maren gripped his wrist and forced his knuckles down into the crest until the violent current tore the skin from his fingers. Not a drop in that arch was still; the shape stayed fixed in one place only because the water rushed through it without ceasing. From the upper eastern sluice poured the bright snow-melt the guild boasted of in its charters; from the western sewer-culvert drove an equal and opposite black surge, thick with the kitchen grease, ash, and night-soil the masters upstairs pretended did not exist. Only where those two opposing torrents struck each other head-on did the arched rib of water rise and lock. When Lukas, disgusted by the stench, dropped the iron shutter across the western culvert so the bright water could run clean and alone, the arched rib collapsed at once into a flat wash, and the floor timbers above began to groan and split until he raised the foul shutter again.

The cellar bursar, watching from the steps, thought Lukas had simply been too greedy in shutting off the whole dark surge. Hoping to steal only a twentieth part of the arch's force to turn his private tallow-churn, the bursar slid a thin oak blade into the edge of the meeting currents, certain the water-rib would merely drop by a thumb's breadth and stay upright. The instant the oak blade broke the beat of the colliding streams, the entire arch shuddered, lost its knot, and whipped apart from crown to base, hurling the bursar against the wall, snapping his churn in two, and flooding the crypt to his waist.

Before she let Lukas climb back to his clean chambers, Maren walked him through the three lower courts that held their form by the same cellar bargain. In the coining-vault, where iron trade-rods of a thousand notches were cast to pay the quarrymen, nine hundred and ninety-five notches from the head of every rod were pressed cold from a single unyielding master-die that had never been altered since the house was founded; only the final five notches at the tail were heated over a coal-pan and struck anew for each bargain, so the vault spent almost nothing on fuel. Next door, in the damp breeding-pits, litters of blind cellar-hounds were born spotted, pale, fierce, or feeble, dying and crossing in every season, yet every whelp at six weeks was driven through the same narrow stone choke-hole filled with sulfur smoke; the blood shifted endlessly, the ruthless choke-hole never changed by a hair, and the pack stayed keen. Last, they climbed to the long hall of judgment, where two continuous files of townsfolk walked without pausing from dawn till dark—accusers marching inward from the street, and confessed debtors marching outward from the bailiff's bench. No man or woman was allowed to linger on the stones, yet at the three fixed pillars where the two moving files scraped past one another and flinched, the brass tablets of the house's laws hung unmoving in the din.
