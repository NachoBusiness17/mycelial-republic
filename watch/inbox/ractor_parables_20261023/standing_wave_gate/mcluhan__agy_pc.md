# The Wicket and the Trough

Cassian came to the river-abbey expecting to find scribes reading the dispatches that arrived by pack-mule from the hills. Instead, at the narrow outer wicket where the stone causeway pinched to the width of a single basket, he found an old porter sitting with a woolen band tied across his eyes. Fixed to the timber post beside the porter's knee was a finger-length reed of tempered brass kept in a continuous, low tremor by a wound clock-spring. Whenever a driver heaved a waxed bundle onto the sill, the porter never broke the seal or asked what tidings lay written on the parchment inside. He merely rested the heel of his wrist against the trembling brass reed. If the bundle's weight and stiffness damped the vibration into a dull thud, he tipped it into the ditch with two fingers; if the brass kept singing against his bone, he nudged the bundle through the wicket. When Cassian asked whether the dispatches brought news of famine or peace, the old man held up his calloused wrist, deaf to rumor and blind to ink, and said he knew only which bundles kept the spring singing and which killed the note.

Beyond the narrow wicket, the enclosure opened suddenly into a vaulted commons where thirty sorting-tables branched outward like the ribs of a fan. Here a score of novices untied the accepted bundles and distributed the sheets among the benches. On Cassian's second morning, a drover from the south grew impatient queuing in single file before the vibrating reed. He pried loose three planks in the outer fence and flung his sacks directly across the thirty tables at once. Within minutes the novices were colliding in the aisles, three benches lay buried under wet hides meant for the ditch, and the entire floor choked to a halt. Not until the masons bricked up the breach and forced every sack back past the single trembling reed could the thirty tables clear their aisles and resume their quiet rhythm.

Down the middle of the commons ran a stone trough fed by two water-wheels set at opposite walls. When the junior sluice-keeper lifted only the eastern paddle, the snow-melt raced straight down the stone channel and spilled out the drain without forming a single ridge, leaving the suspended dip-frames dry. Only when the western keeper lifted his paddle to drive an equal torrent head-on against the first did the two opposing rushes lock in mid-channel. Instantly, three steep crowns of foaming water rose and stood motionless beneath the dip-frames, though fresh river water tore through them every second. The tenders who worked the trough had stared so long at the stationary crowns that when they stepped outside to the river at dusk, their eyes watered and flinched at the sight of unopposed water.

At the first stationary crown, the stampers pressed clay trade-bars beneath a drop-beam. Their heavy iron die was bolted fast to the oak timber for almost its entire length, casting the same unyielding relief every time; for nine hundred and ninety-five blows out of every thousand, the striker swapped only a fingernail-sized copper plug at the tail of the die. His hand had forgotten the craft of carving, reduced to a trigger for the fixed iron. At the second crown, the seed-master poured harvest after harvest of mountain barley across a bronze riddle whose oblong slots had never been altered since the walls were raised. Generation after generation of grain passed and perished, yet the unchanging slots forced every surviving crop into the same narrow kernel, and the master's fingers could no longer tell red barley from white, feeling only what cleared the bronze mesh. At the third crown, two files of Brothers walked continuously past one another in opposite directions along a flagged walk, reciting the house ordinances. The walkers never paused, yet their voices met and swelled into audible words only at three chalked flagstones where the opposing chants struck one another full on; the prior sitting beside the chalk marks no longer looked at the faces passing in the gloom, his ear tuned solely to the stationary swell above the stone.

On the third afternoon, Cassian dipped the tip of a wooden measuring-rod into the first water-crown, expecting the ridge to sink by the breadth of a thumb. The crown did not shrink by degrees. At the rod's first touch, all three ridges along the trough collapsed at once into flat, thrashing slop; the drop-beam lost its balance and jammed, the bronze riddle flooded with chaff, and the chanting files stumbled out of cadence until the walk rang with clatter. The keepers had to drop both paddles, drain the trough to the bare stone, and release the twin torrents anew before the three crowns rose again. As the water locked, Cassian watched the porter press his wrist to the brass, the striker drop his shoulder without glancing at the clay, and the prior close his eyes beside the chalk mark.
