# The Strait That Stood Still

Ines came to the crossing to measure it for the crown. Twice an hour, from before light until after dark, a ferry left each shore to meet its opposite mid-strait, and between the two boats a ridge of water stood — a long seam, glassy and low, that never drifted east or west. The water worked everywhere; the shape went nowhere. She timed it for eleven days, and the seam did not move a hand's width.

The old ferryman laughed at her instruments. He had rowed the first boat alone for years, he said, and alone it had been only a boat; the day a second began crossing against his, his crossing stopped being a journey and became half of a figure. The seam needed them both, every day, ever since — let either rest and there was nothing between them but water. Nobody asked what the boats carried. The rowers had long since stopped being travelers; they had become strokes, their shoulders swinging like parts of the figure. The town had married the shape, not the cargo.

West of the landing, a mason named Arno kept a yard of first stones. Of every thousand departures out of the town, nine hundred and ninety-five set out across stones he had cut years before, and for each he was paid as though the stone were new. He had not quarried in a decade. His chisel hand had gone soft as a clerk's, and the travelers, stepping from his stones, believed they had set out; they had only been received.

Up in the high pastures, each spring brought Bella a flock new down to the last lamb, and each spring she sorted it through the same gate in the same order — cull left, keep right — before any other look was taken. The animals changed utterly every year; the choosing never did. She had stopped seeing sheep. She saw the order, and in her sleep her hands worked the gate.

Along the fair-road, families hauled their long benches from market to market, and a bench never rested two fairs in the same place. But the oath-stones stood where they had always stood, tall and grey, and no quarrel could end anywhere except at one. Two traders would fight the whole road, walking faster and faster, and reach the nearest stone suddenly quiet, as though the stillness had reached up and taken the argument out of them.

At the mouth of the freight-yard a small water-engine, no bigger than a boot, hummed on the wicket. Every wagon stopped beneath it. The hum rose or dropped — a thumb on the lip of a jug — and that was the whole judgment: heavy, light, heavy, light. It did not weigh truly and was never asked to; it gave a jug's worth of knowing, quick and coarse, and the wicket took no more than that. Behind it a wide hall lay dim and patient, and behind the hall, twelve doors. Before the hall there was one track only, and no load divided at the wicket; a carter who tried to split his freight on the threshold was backed out whole. Past the hall, the doors took a load any way it cared to come apart. The children skipping there sang it without knowing what they were keeping:

Little hum on the wicket gate,
jiggle the jug and guess the freight;
the wide hall bides, let no wheel divide —
split your load on the far side.

In her second month, the crown's men drove pilings into the middle of the strait for a new pier. Ines watched from the measure-boat. The seam did not grow smaller. It unknit, loop by loop, shore to shore, like stitching pulled at a single loop, and the crossings went on — the boats working harder than ever — and the figure was nowhere. Rowing slower did not bring it back, and rowing faster did not. What the town recognized in the water it recognized elsewhere that same year. A road was cut at last from a place Arno's yard had never paved, and one departure in ten thousand began off his stones; his trade did not dwindle, it unthreaded — prices, runners, the order of his shelves — in a single season.

Ines went home in the autumn. On the road she caught her steps falling into the skipping measure, four beats and then three. She caught herself humming at a heavy wagon as it passed, and she knew, from the pitch of her own voice, which of the twelve doors it would have taken. She had come to measure water. What she carried away was already weighing freight.
