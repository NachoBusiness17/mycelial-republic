# The Channel at the Sluice

Ferris stood upon the granite catwalk where the upper canal narrowed to the breadth of an oarsman’s reach. Below him, the water did not run like an ordinary river. From the north raced a mountain torrent, cold and black, while from the southern reservoir an equal torrent was driven back against it by great subterranean wheels. Where the two contrary floods collided, they did not spill over the parapet. Instead, they locked into five stationary glass-like mounds that hung motionless in the air. The water itself rushed through them at blinding speed, tearing the skin off any floating twig, yet the five ridges never shifted by a hairsbreadth. They were hills of perpetual speed that appeared to sleep.

"Keep your eyes off the crests," the elder warden told him, adjusting the leather strap of his ear-guard. "The eye wants to believe in monuments. It tells you there is stone under the arch because nothing moves. But the moment you look with the eye, you forget the vibration in your ribs. The medium does not rest; it only balances its violence."

At the throat of the channel, before the water entered the five motionless mounds, rested an iron frame no wider than a dog’s crate. Inside it, a tiny brass cylinder spun on a dry pin, singing with a continuous, insect-like drone. It was smaller than a kettle and cost less than an oil lamp, yet every wicker cargo barge approaching the trench had to glide beneath its touch. As each vessel drifted under, an iron feeler tipped with lead brushed against its prow. If the vessel possessed the expected ballast, the feeler skipped across it with a dry click and let the barge pass into the throat. If a barge was overburdened or warped, the feeler caught, tripping a wooden latch that dropped the barge into an outer spillway before it could touch the narrows.

Ferris watched a line of twenty barges approach. Nineteen of them, and nine hundred more behind them in the holding pool, were constructed from the exact same uniform cedar staves, stamped at the prow with the identical copper groove. Because nearly every hull carried that same initial stamping without variation, the singing cylinder barely checked its drone; the feeler kissed each identical prow in turn, and the line slid into the sluice without a ripple or a pause.

"Why not widen the gate so three hulls may enter at once?" Ferris asked, shouting over the mechanical buzz.

The warden pointed his staff past the throat. Beyond the humming frame and the five stationary mounds, the stone walls fell away, opening into a vast vaulted water-house where thirty quiet berths branched out like the ribs of an open fan. But here at the entrance, there was only the single trench. "If you split the current before the spindle has tasted it," said the warden, "the vibration scatters into cross-currents and the hum dies. The fan opens only where the weighing is finished. Before the gate, there is only one string."

Down in the flume, three steersmen in a cedar skiff shot through the five arches. They were rowing fiercely against the downriver flood, their arms knotted and their faces drenched with spray. Yet while the steersmen and their oars swept rapidly through from one end of the channel to the other, the magistrate of the water-house sat upon a wooden bench suspended directly above the third motionless mound. He did not watch the men’s faces or inquire into their cargo. From his wooden perch above the unmoving crest, he called out the harbor taxes and the boundary decrees. The men who heard him drifted past and were lost in the dark water-house within moments, replaced at once by a fresh skiff of strangers, yet the magistrate’s voice hung over the same patch of foam year after year. The law inhabited the swell, while the flesh that obeyed it was washed away.

Along the submerged keel of the skiffs swam schools of pale finches, small river fish that held the hull steady in the foam. Old fish died and tumbled into the churn; new fry took their places between the timbers, but the darting diamond pattern of the school never altered.

Ferris reached out with his pine measuring rod, tempted to touch the glass skin of the fourth mound to see if it felt like ice.

The warden struck the rod from his grip before the tip could graze the surface.

"Touch an ordinary wave," the warden hissed, "and you make a splash that dies in the shallows. But touch this ridge, and you break the balance of the two floods. It will not merely ripple down or grow small. The rhythm will snap, and the entire flume will tear itself apart from one stone wall to the other before you can draw breath."

Ferris looked down at the dark, singing water. He felt the steady drone of the little brass cylinder vibrating through the stone under his boots, remaking the beat of his own pulse, and stepped back from the edge.
