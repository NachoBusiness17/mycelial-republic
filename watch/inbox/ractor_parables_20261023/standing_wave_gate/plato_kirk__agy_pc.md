# The Twin Sluices of the Cavern

Two wardens shared a stone bench at the cavern mouth, three paces apart: the Surveyor, who had climbed the vent-shaft into blinding daylight to see how the mountain's flumes were cut, and the Helmsman, who had refused a rigged trial in the lower lock by resetting its pins rather than drowning inside its rules. Workers deep in the gallery mistook the shadows on the rock for solid pillars, believing they could only endure the dark or flee up the shaft and leave the works running. The wardens knew better: one must first step outside to see how the walls are staged, then return to reset the levers. Before them rose the outer arch. Set into its keystone was a fist-sized brass flywheel, spinning with a low, steady hum. Every carrier from the quarry had to rest his crate upon the flywheel's tongue. With a tiny flick of its spring, the humming wheel weighed the burden at negligible cost, tipping dross into the ditch and letting sound loads pass. When a carrier tried to split his team into three files outside the arch, the Helmsman barred the path. Before the humming wheel lay only one narrow approach; only after a crate cleared the arch did the floor open into the vaulted court, where twelve radial causeways branched outward to the three terraces.

Inside the court, nothing rested, yet nothing appeared to shift. From the snowmelt, a fast water-race drove eastward through a cut trough, while a second pump-race drove westward against it with equal force. Where the two opposing torrents met, tall ribs of white water rose upright like carved marble columns, bearing the floating gangways. A newly arrived mason reached out to lean his shoulder against a rib, taking the stationary crest for fixed stone. The Surveyor pulled him back, pointing to flecks of mica shooting through the foam. The column held its place only because the rush never ceased. That afternoon, an apprentice dropped a hatch over the western return-flume, thinking one strong stream could carry half the burden. The moment the counter-current stopped, the upright ribs vanished; a single stream could only wash downstream and pile uselessly against the far bulkhead. Two opposing drives were the minimum price of a stationary figure, and only when the Helmsman pried the western hatch open did the twin torrents lock again and raise the arches back into place.

Across the three terraces, the work obeyed the same hidden bargain. On the first terrace, stamp-smiths forged iron links for the windlass. Their furnace was tiny, yet their output never lagged: the long root-die of every mold was bolted cold and permanent into the anvil-bed, while only the small terminal clasp at the tip was heated and struck anew. Out of every two hundred plunges of the drop-hammer, a hundred and ninety-nine struck the cold, locked root-die without spending a single coal, and only one blow required fresh fire. On the second terrace, breeders tended troughs of cavern-barley where no individual stalk was ever spared. Each harvest, tenders tore up the beds, crossed the hardy survivors, and cast the seed through a heavy brass riddle cut to an unyielding gauge. The stalks died and recombined every season, yet the brass riddle and winnowing flume never altered by a hair, keeping the harvest's exact measure across generations.

On the third terrace, watch-crews guarded the dykes in two rings marching in opposite directions around the perimeter wall. No worker owned a fixed post. At every stroke of the water-bell, the outer ring stepped one pace clockwise and the inner ring stepped one pace counter-clockwise. Dwellers in the cavern complained that all was flux, while strangers thought the watchmen were statues because at twelve chalked crossing-stones around the wall, two figures always stood face to face, reciting the watch ordinances and passing the sounding-rod. The rows of men traveled without pause; the laws hung stationary at the exact crossing-points where the opposing files met.

When the winter consul sent an inspector to extract heavier tribute, the inspector mistook the three terraces for a dead wall that could be shaved down by degrees. Hoping to divert a tenth of the force to his private mill, he drove an oak wedge into the eastern trough to slow the inward race by a fraction. The Surveyor warned that he was striking at shadows, and the Helmsman reached for the release pin, but the wedge was already home. The upright ribs of water did not shrink by a tenth. They shuddered, lost their crossing-point, and tore apart all at once. In three heartbeats the gangways dropped, the cold root-dies lost their timing, the barley troughs overflowed, and the marching rings broke into a blind scramble. What holds by opposing motion never diminishes partway; when disturbed, the entire figure comes undone at a stroke. Rather than curse the deluge or desert the cavern, the Surveyor called down the true drop-angles he had measured in the sun above, and the Helmsman reset the main sluice-pins to match them, refusing the inspector's rigged measure until the twin currents locked again and the court rose back out of the foam.
