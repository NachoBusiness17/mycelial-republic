# The Wave That Stood

South of the mill town the river met the tide in a strait, and where the two waters crossed, one wave stood. It broke every morning in the same place and rebuilt itself in the same place, though the water pouring through it was never the same water. The ferry was worked by twins: the elder rowed the crossings, and the younger kept the knots and the ropes. They told every passenger the same thing, and it was true — the river had run a thousand years without pausing, and the wave had stood a thousand years without traveling.

Upstream, at the mill, a sifter named Maren worked the morning chute. She panned the first bucket of gravel and set the rest of the load aside, and when a visitor once counted for her across a whole season, nine hundred and ninety-five pans in a thousand came up alike — the same bright flakes settling in the same order. The far end of the claim paid worse and took all day; the first bucket paid at once, every day, and she never dug past it.

Across the bridge lived a keeper of hounds. Each autumn she walked her yard, kept only the swift and the quiet, sold the rest, and bred from what remained. The rule was the whole of her art, and she never changed it. Her dogs were never the same dogs twice, and the line was always the same shape.

Once a year the walkers came down from the pass in long rows, and the road bore them the way a channel bears weather: worn down, re-graveled, washed out and relaid. The rows moved, and the road moved under them. What did not move were the stones where the town's oldest agreements were posted. The stream of walkers parted around each stone and closed again beyond it, and at the stones' base the ground stayed firm while the road between them sank lower every year.

The younger twin, who trusted his hands, cut a flume above the mill to raise a wave of his own for the spring fair. He gave it one current, straight and strong. The water ran through it and was gone; nothing stood. The elder watched a while and said that one water can only go somewhere, and that nothing stays until a second water comes back against it. So they cut a second flume and led it home to meet the first, and at the junction a small wave rose and held — going nowhere, made entirely of passing.

In the dry summer a barge-master moored inside the crest of the strait to spare himself the long row to the quay, meaning only to lean on the wave and shave it down a little. The wave did not grow smaller. It came apart. For a season the strait was chop and confusion, the ferry grounded twice, and cargo went the long way by road. When the two waters found each other again, the wave stood up as before. After that, no one moored in the meeting place.

At the harbor mouth stood a warehouse with a gate so narrow that two carts could not pass abreast. In the gate lived a small engine whose whole work was a hum and a heft: it lifted each cart a hand's width, felt whether it was heavy or light, and set it down. It opened no bales and asked no questions; the judgment cost almost nothing, and it was made at the threshold and nowhere else. Behind the gate the hall was wide as a church, and only there, under the tall windows, did brokers break cargoes and divide them among many buyers. On the road, outside, everything traveled whole to the door.

One autumn the elder rowed out to see where the tide was born. He came back with his eyes scalded white and his hands idle in his lap, and he said only this: that he had followed the current which comes back against the river — the one the wave is made of — and it had led him to a weir their grandfathers had laid across a fork of the sea. The meeting of waters was built, and the wave with it. The younger went up the next morning with stakes and rope. He did not touch the weir. He moved the ferry stones a boat's length, so that the crossing sat differently inside the standing water, and the crossing was easier that year than it had ever been. Passengers still told one another that the river did this by its nature. The twins rowed and knotted and said nothing.
