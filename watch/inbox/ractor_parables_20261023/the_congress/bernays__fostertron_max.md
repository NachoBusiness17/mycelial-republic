# A Congress of Strangers

Each morning the town of Vey raised a pavilion of grey canvas in the arcade, and each dusk it took the pavilion down. The people beneath it were drawn by lot at dawn — a fishwife, a wheelwright, a boy too young to shave — and at dusk they walked out through the ropes and became private again. What they had said under the canvas did not follow them home. A woman who spent Tuesday arguing for the new weir drew a lot again on Wednesday and stood against it by noon, because nothing of Tuesday lived in her; she argued Wednesday's side with her whole chest, and the town called this ordinary.

For the town kept its mind in one place: the long wall at the north end of the arcade, cut line under line by one mason with one chisel. A grain merchant once paid the mason to smooth away a line that shamed him. The mason took the coin, cut a new line beneath the old one saying the old one was mistaken, and left the old one standing — so the merchant paid twice and was shamed three times: once in the first line, once in the second, and once for asking. Beggars earned their bread at the wall, reading it aloud to anyone who could not read. Travelers copied it for amusement, but a copy was paper, and paper had never governed anything in Vey.

Under the canvas, the town arranged itself by furniture and by masks. Along the east side stood the ancient benches, older than any family and owned by no one; they were counted as voices in their own right, and whoever rested on one that day merely lent it a throat — a bench, whatever else could be said of it, never said one thing on Tuesday and another on Wednesday. Along the west side stood the day-speakers of the ten trades — the dyers, the coopers, the tanners, and seven more — each trade with one clear voice for the day and nothing carried over.

Between the halves, on a low stage, five masks hung on five pegs: a coin, a far shore, a hammer, a closed book, and a grin. Whoever drew a mask wore it and spoke for it — the coin for the town's money, the far shore for strangers, the hammer for whatever must stand, the closed book for whatever had already been, the grin for whatever the others had forgotten to doubt. Beside each peg stood a breaker, paid by the town to ruin whatever the mask said. A mask's word counted for nothing until its breaker had swung at it and missed; only then did the mason's chisel get involved. The breakers were liked by nobody, which was the point of them, and they ate well.

Five laws were cut at the top of the wall, above every other line. Four were older than the arcade and concerned blood, bread, bridges, and strangers. The fifth, the newest, said that the cold room beneath the arcade — the fast, cold room where the town kept what must be reached quickly — must always hold more than fifteen pails of emptiness. A boy measured it at dusk with a marked pole. Once, after a fat harvest, the emptiness shrank to fifteen pails exactly, and men carried cheese out into the yard and left it in the rain until the pole called the air lawful again.

The small trades loved the wall best, and for the plainest reason: a great man's promise used to shrink as it traveled, and a cut line did not. Every eye in Vey went to the wall first and to a face second. Believing stone was cheaper than believing neighbors, and the town had paid out the difference too many times.

In the autumn a captain came up the river with forty spears and a paper sealed by a dead king. He stood at the edge of the pavilion and announced that the town was his. The strangers under the canvas looked at him, then past him at the wall, and waited, because in Vey nobody was anything until the wall said so, and the wall had not said so. The coin-mask offered him bread; the grin-mask offered to read his paper aloud for fun. He stayed a week and left with his spears.

One spring night lightning cracked the wall through three courses of stone. The town came out in the rain and stood with lamps along the crack until morning, because an old breaker, speaking to no one, said what everyone was thinking: a spear takes one life, but a wall coming down takes every yesterday a town owns, and there are men behind hedges who would pay to push. The wall held. In the days after, the mason cut the crack itself into the wall, so that the wall now carries an account of its own wound, where anyone may come and read it.

At dusk the masks went back on their pegs and the day-speakers went home to their trades. The strangers walked out through the ropes and forgot the day on purpose; forgetting was the town's oldest courtesy. The boy measured the cold room — fifteen pails and two fingers over — and the mason cut the last line by lamplight. Then the pavilion came down, and Vey slept well beside the one thing it owned that remembered anything at all.
