# The Whitewashed Pillar

A dog sleeps wherever the dust is warm, requiring neither a title nor a stool to prove he is breathing. Men, however, must erect a theater out of timber and gravel before they can agree to dig a common ditch.

In the white heat of the courtyard, they gather only when the iron chime sounds at midday. Inside the ring of chalk, their voices rise like crows over spilled barley. An elder in clean wool argues that the north cistern must be saved for the onions; another shouts that the draft horses must drink first. For an hour, their gathering has the weight of a stone falling through air. But when the chime strikes dusk, the chalk ring is erased with broom-straw. The men step out into the dirt, and at once their majesty dissolves. The elder in the wool reaches toward the stew-pot and tells a boy to fetch him a ladle; the boy walks past him. Outside the ring, the wool gives no orders, the tongue commands no service, and neither man carries rank into the dark. When the chime sounds on the morrow, they will enter an empty ring as strangers, bringing no private debt or yesterday's whispers into the dust.

In the center of the yard stands a square pillar of white limestone beneath an open trellis. Only one person ever touches it: a mute mason carrying a mallet and a point of tempered iron. When the shouting in the ring settles upon an agreement, the mason strikes the rock, cutting the words deep into the stone, always placing new lines directly beneath the old. He never turns the chisel backward, and he never grinds away what was struck before. Years ago, a brush fire swept the roof and cracked an older pillar into lime dust. The elders stood around the rubble and tore their hair, not because the stone was dear, but because forty winters of agreements had vanished in the blaze. What was dust had never been spoken; with the stone gone, no man could prove that any debt or boundary had ever existed.

When the inspectors arrive from the high province, the elders run to the gate with oiled beards, holding out open palms and swearing upon their grandfathers' graves that the granary accounts are just. The inspectors do not look at their faces, nor do they touch their extended fingers. They brush past the elders without a word and walk straight to the pillar in the yard. An inspector licks his thumb and runs it along the fresh cuts in the limestone, counting the chisel marks against the light. To an inspector, the oath of a patriarch is merely hot breath spent on the wind; only the carved rock is real.

Above the yard, on a stone terrace facing the north wall, sit the custodians of the permanent stations. These men do not answer for the people who walk the roads, nor do they speak for families. One represents the deep cistern, another the lime kiln, another the threshing floor, and another the granite marker on the southern ridge. Men rot and are buried, but the kiln and the cistern remain, and these custodians speak only for the unmoving earth of the estate.

On the broad wooden benches below them sit the representatives of the ten working trades. Here the potters, the tanners, the smiths, the weavers, the coopers, the stonecutters, the wheelwrights, the charcoal-burners, the bakers, and the rope-makers each hold a single peeled willow wand. When a question of labor is raised, the ten wands are laid upon the flagstones, one for each workshop, ensuring that the work of the hands is not swallowed by the talk of the terrace.

Beside the water trough stand five counselors who wear carved face-guards painted with ash and ochre. There is the counter of coins, the traveler who knows foreign hills, the builder of cranes, the singer of ancient annals, and a ragged fool wearing dried gourds to mock the others. Behind each masked counselor stands a silent doubter carrying a plumb-bob or a balance scale. The moment the coin-counter declares what the cellar holds, his doubter steps forward to weigh an actual sack; if the counselor's claim cannot survive the doubter's test, the advice is kicked aside like sour melon.

At the base of the pillar, five ancient commands are chiseled so deep that no winter storm can dull them. The lowest of these decrees governs the great dray-wagon that hauls timber and grain from the bottomland. By this statute, the floorboards of the dray must always keep fifteen broad baskets of clear, empty deck, untouched by freight. When an eager drover piled sacks of barley to the very edge of the wagon stakes, the yardmaster climbed onto the wheel, drew his knife, and cut the cords, tumbling fifteen full sacks into the mud. A wagon loaded to its gunwales cannot turn its tongue on the ridge, and when the dray breaks, the entire estate starves.
