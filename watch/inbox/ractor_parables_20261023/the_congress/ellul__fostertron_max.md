# The Book in the Square

In Alderford the bell, not the calendar, decided when the town would think. When it rang, whoever wished to speak came to the hall, took a gray coat from the pegs, and dropped their name into a basket at the door. Inside they were nobody in particular. The long table held twelve places that belonged to families and were never rechosen; ten stools stood for the trades — the millers, the coopers, the ferrymen, and the rest; and facing them stood five chairs that filled only when the officers came hooded: the counter of coin, the greeter of strangers, the keeper of the works, the teller of what happened, and the mocker, whose trade was to laugh at the other four.

A speaker in a gray coat who said "last time we agreed" was made to sit down. Last time was in the book or it was nowhere. Newcomers read before they spoke. When the bell ended the meeting, the coats went back on their pegs, the names were lifted out of the basket, and the members went home as strangers, carrying nothing of it but the mud on their boots.

The book lay open on a stand under the hall's porch, where the rain could not reach it and anyone could read. One scribe held one pen. The pages were numbered and stitched into the spine, and no leaf had ever been cut out; before writing anything new, the scribe read the last line aloud to whoever stood there. When the flood-plan of the hooded keeper of the works was proved wrong — it drained the wrong meadow — the plan was not scraped away. A fresh page was sewn in beneath it, and the error stayed legible above the repair.

Upstream, the town at the ford had let its book burn in a bad spring. The bridge tolls, the mill grant, the boundary stones all turned to shouting. The elders swore oaths over what had been granted, the oaths weighed what oaths weigh, and within a year the bridge belonged to whoever could argue longest. Alderford took the lesson into its sayings: burn the pages and nothing was ever decided here.

Each autumn the examiner came from the county. He heard the richest miller swear to his figures and did not lift his eyes. He heard the hooded counter of coin declare the toll-box honest and did not lift his eyes. When the scribe turned the stitched book to the line and set a finger on the ink, the examiner made his mark. "I take no man's word in this town," he said, "not even yours," and the scribe, who had spoken twice that day, nodded, because it was so.

After the fire at the ford, Alderford tried a second pen for one winter, a twin book kept by a clerk in the granary so that flame could not take everything. By midsummer the two books disagreed over a toll, a nine where the other held a six, and the examiner refused them both, and that year nothing could be shown to have been decided. The second pen was retired to the mill. It was the one contrivance the town ever unmade, and it took only a season.

Behind each of the five chairs stood a breaker, paid from the toll-box. The counter's breaker recounted every figure before it was spoken; the teller's breaker followed the recital with a finger on the pages; the mocker's breaker hunted for where the joke flattered. In the lean year when someone proposed sending the breakers home with the fiddlers, the hooded keeper of the works had his breaker undo the new flood-plan before the hall, and the coin for the breakers was found.

Five laws were nailed to the doorpost. Four concerned tolls, the night watch, true weights, and market days. The fifth said that more than fifteen ells of the drying-rail in the loft — where the wet pages hung — must stay bare. The year the barley came in thick and a clerk stacked sacks to the rail's far end, the ink ran, the pages smudged, the examiner refused the autumn count, and the town lived a season unable to prove itself. After that the farmers stored their grain proudly in the street-side barn with the rats, and cursed the harvest, not the law. Nobody could say who had first written the number fifteen. Someone asked the scribe; he turned to an early page and pointed at a line, and that was the whole of the answer.

Because the meetings began empty, they read before speaking; because they read, the pages thickened; because the pages thickened, the rail was lengthened; because of the rail, the barley went to the rats. Each remedy arrived quietly and stayed as a need.

At dusk the bell ended it. The hoods came off in the entry — the counter of coin was the tanner's daughter; the mocker, the old ferryman — and the breakers left separately, as breakers do. On the porch a child copied the market rules onto a slate, and under the eaves the shutter was drawn over the book, the last thing in Alderford anyone thought to shelter from the rain.
