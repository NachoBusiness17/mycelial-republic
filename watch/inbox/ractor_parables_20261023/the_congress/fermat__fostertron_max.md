# The Horn at the Wellhead

I passed a week in the town below the chalk hills, riding circuit for the parlement, and I set down here what I saw of their custom, as the margin of my notebook allowed.

A horn sounds from the wellhead at no fixed hour, whenever the warden judges there is matter. Whoever hears it may come; at the door of the long shed each comer draws a pebble from a jar, and the pebble gives him his bench. Enemies draw side by side; a stranger may draw between them. When the torches are quenched the sitting ends, and it ends utterly: the pebbles go back into the jar, the people leave by different gates, and no sitting is ever called back. A weaver named Jeannette once began, "as we resolved in the autumn—" and every head turned to her as to a woman describing a dream. What the autumn sitting resolved lay in one place only, and no one's head was that place.

Behind the shed wait the banners. Ten trades keep the town — the weavers, the smiths, the tanners, and seven more besides — and each comes in behind its painted cloth, speaking through whichever of its own hands it has chosen that morning. When the torches die, the cloths are folded, and a trade holds nothing over from one sitting to the next except what stands written.

Five go masked between sittings, and no one asks which neighbor wears a mask this year; a quarrel falls on the office, and the face under it is never invited in. One keeps the coin; one carries the town's words beyond the chalk; one tends the bridge and the waterworks; one reads the old sheets aloud and keeps the story of what was done; one goes in motley, whose office is to laugh at the other four. Behind each walks a lean man carrying a slate, the shadow. Whatever a mask intends, his shadow first tries to ruin it. One works-day the bridge-mask brought his plan to the wellhead, and his shadow called out the flaw he had hunted down in it — the timber count ran short by nine — and the plan went to the scribe mended. What no shadow can break goes in as spoken.

The scribe is Perrin, at an iron stand in the square, and on the stand lies the roll: sheets sewn end to end, longer each year than a man is tall. A cord ties the quill to the stand, long enough to reach every line of the day and too short to leave with. Whoever wins a grant or a right speaks it aloud to Perrin, and Perrin alone writes, and the square hears it twice. Anybody may turn the roll back — the tanners send a boy to read the year's lines at their door of a morning. A draper once, impatient of his turn, took the quill and set down his own grant in his own hand; ever after the town read that line as it read a stain, the readers' voices passing over it, and because his grant stood nowhere else, it did not stand at all. Nothing is ever cut from the roll; when the sheets fill, new ones are sewn on.

Master Gaultier, who had lost a water-right, came one night with a torch, and the watch took it from him gently and told him what fire would buy: not the water-right, but the end of his own mill-title, his father's grant, every judgment ever given him — all of it lived in the sewn sheets, and the sheets burned would leave a town in which nothing had ever been decided, himself a squatter on his own mill-floor. In a festival year a spark did catch the corner of the first sheet, and the town held everything from that scorched span as never having passed: two roads went back to being unowned, and the parties came before a fresh sitting and began again from silence.

Disputes are not settled by memory there, nor by rank. Old Arnaud swore before the whole square that he had heard the mill-grant given otherwise; nobody argued with him; the crowd walked to the stand, unrolled to the year, and read the line aloud, and the line was believed over the man, his oath weighing what a dream weighs. Asking after a road-title, I saw them descend: they stood the claim on the line that gave it, and that line on an earlier line, and so down the sewn years, link after link, until the chain either touched the first sheet or ended in air; and what ended in air was left lying.

Five lines are cut into the wellhead stone. The strangest governs the horn. Before any sitting the warden goes down to the vault where the blank paper lies and sets a marked rod against the clear floor, and unless better than fifteen measures stand open — the rod carries its mark, and fourteen and a half is nothing — the horn stays silent. One spring the town waited eight days in the square while flax was beaten and dried; no horn sounded, and nothing could be decided, because there was not room left to decide it in.

Of all this I saw the horn, the roll, the rod, and the descending chain with my own eyes. Gaultier and the draper I have from the innkeeper, and I give them as they were given to me. I have the custom by neighboring, not by possession; I could set down the whole proof of why they chose to forget, but this margin is too narrow to hold it, and I would rather leave the theorem whole and unproved than set down half of it.

1. The governing bodies are stateless — convened fresh by lot, dissolved into strangers, remembering nothing, so no member's recollection of any past session carries weight. (Jeannette's "as we resolved in the autumn" is received like a dream, and the pebbles return to the jar.)
2. The assembly has three kinds of members: the lot-drawn benches, ten trade-companies that speak as standing bodies behind banners, and five masked offices that carry the town between sittings. (The long shed, the painted cloths, and the masked five.)
3. The five masked offices are coin-keeping, foreign words, the works, the story of what was done, and mockery, each anonymized so authority attaches to the office and never to a face. (No one asks which neighbor wears the coin-mask; quarrels fall on the grain-tax, not its wearer.)
4. Every office keeps a falsifier — a shadow whose sole work is to break the master's plan before it is entered, so only what the shadow fails to break is adopted. (The bridge-plan reaching the scribe corrected by nine timbers.)
5. All decisions live solely in one public, append-only roll written by a single hand; anything written in another hand counts for nothing. (The draper's self-written grant read like a stain; sheets are sewn on and never cut.)
6. Anyone may read the roll, and it is the only thing the town trusts — claims are verified by descending link by link until the chain touches the roll or ends in air, while oaths, memory, and rank weigh nothing. (Old Arnaud's sworn recollection losing to the line read aloud.)
7. Burning the roll unmakes every decision at once — the losers' burdens and the winners' titles alike — so even the powerful depend on its survival. (Gaultier's torch taken gently, and the scorched first sheet returning two roads to being unowned.)
8. Five fixed statutes stand cut in public stone where all may read them. (The wellhead.)
9. One of the five bars any session from convening unless more than fifteen measures of blank room remain — under the mark, no horn, no sitting. (The warden's marked rod and the eight silent days while the paper was made.)
