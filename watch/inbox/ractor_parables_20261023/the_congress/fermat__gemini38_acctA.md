# The Assize of the Chained Roll

Counselor Jean came to the stone courthouse beside the canal when autumn fog had settled upon the water. He was an officer of the Parlement, accustomed to written process and suspicious of any cause that rested upon the goodwill of men. At the threshold he found the oak wicket barred. Before the bolts could turn, an overseer descended to the lower undercroft carrying a notched hazel rod. Jean followed him down to the grain vault, where the overseer laid the rod upon the pavement between the bins and the masonry. Fifteen spans of bare flagstone were required to remain utterly clear of sack, cask, or tool. When a porter attempted to set an iron brazier upon the flags, the overseer struck it away with his staff; if the stones lacked even half a span of the fifteen open measures, no key could turn and no assembly could deliberate. Only when the rod proved the unencumbered floor was the wicket unlocked.

Above, the hall was partitioned into three distinct precincts. In the upper gallery stood the wardens of the city bastions, each beside a numbered stone pillar. In the broad nave gathered the masters of the ten crafts—the quarryman, the smith, the tanner, the joiner, the clothier, the potter, the glassblower, the cooper, the wheelwright, and the miller—each bearing an iron seal-ring upright in his palm. At the far end, upon a stone platform, were five figures whose faces were hidden beneath carved walnut visors: the counter of silver, the envoy from foreign marches, the builder of aqueducts, the chronicler of ancient tombs, and a jester in piebald wool holding an inverted bell.

Yet neither the five masked counselors nor the ten guilds could establish a decree by proclamation alone. Beside each masked figure waited a silent adversary appointed to demonstrate the falsity of his claims before any voice could be recorded. When the counter of silver asserted that the city held sufficient coin, his adversary dropped the sample into a vial of acid and showed the precipitate was false. When the envoy announced a treaty with the hill folk, his challenger presented a torn courier-ribbon showing the mountain pass was closed by spears. When the builder offered a plan for a granary arch, his adversary loaded a pine timber frame until the joint broke under the weights. When the chronicler cited an ancient boundary right, his challenger produced an older cartulary with conflicting seals. And when the jester offered a mocking riddle, his adversary struck chert against steel to show the premise was factually unsound, silencing the jest.

Whatever survived this adversarial descent was conveyed to a single scrivener beneath an oil lamp in the central aisle. He alone held the horn of ink. Before him lay a long roll of sheepskin wound upon an iron spindle and chained to the central pier. The vellum allowed no scraping; an erroneous stroke was crossed with a fine red rule and the correction set beneath it, preserving every defect in plain view. The casement behind him stood open to the public street, where draymen and scholars leaned upon the sill to read the black lines as they dried. When a visiting bailiff asked what would become of their decisions if fire took the spindle, the scrivener replied that if the sheepskin burned, the three chambers and their agreements would dissolve as though they had never convened, for unwritten consensus leaves no footprint on the earth.

At the eighth strike of the cathedral bell, the assembly ceased instantly without conclusion. The bastion wardens stepped down from their pillars. The ten craft masters dropped their iron rings into a common iron chest, turned the latch, and walked out. The five masked counselors unknotted the linen straps of their walnut visors, hung them upon five bronze pegs in the wall, and dispersed into the dusk. In a tavern by the docks, Jean saw two of the counselors eating bread at the same table; they remained as strangers, offering neither word nor sign of what had been debated. When the courthouse was unlocked the next morning, those who entered carried no title or residual power from the day before, but met as newly assembled travelers who knew nothing except what was inscribed upon the chained vellum.

Before the sun rose, an inspector from the royal treasury arrived to audit the grain tax. The governor of the town met him at the wicket, raising his right hand to swear upon the Gospels that the tax had been lawful and that all members had assented. The inspector brushed past the outstretched hand, refusing the oath and ignoring the governor's rank. He went directly to the chained spindle, held his tallow candle to the sheepskin, and verified the entry, the adversary's cancellation mark, and the seals of the ten crafts. "A governor may swear from vanity or fear," the inspector said, blowing out his candle, "but this iron spindle does not flatter, and justice rests only in the margin of what is inscribed."
