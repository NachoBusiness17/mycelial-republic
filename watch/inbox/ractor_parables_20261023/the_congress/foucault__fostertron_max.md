# The Town Between Two Bells

A traveler came to the town in the dry season, having heard that it governed itself without remembering anything. The congress met in the long hall above the market. When the morning bell rang, the summoned walked in — a dyer, a surveyor, two widows, a farrier — and became, for that day, the congress. They argued, weighed, agreed, refused. When the dusk bell rang they walked out, and not one of them could have said what had passed. The next morning the bell rang again, and the ones who came in were strangers to the day before, even in the same coats. Whatever the congress was, it lived only between the two bells.

The traveler expected disorder and found instead a lectern in the open market, under a glass hood, lit by a lamp at night. On it lay one book. An old woman with ink to the wrist was the only hand permitted to write in it, and she wrote while the congress spoke, line after line; when the pages filled she sewed in new ones, and nothing was ever cut away, washed, or struck through. She wrote in the open air, where any passerby could watch the lines appear, and boys copied passages to sell. Once a cobbler leaned over the rail and added, in his own hand, a claim about a debt owed to him. The wardens wiped it away before the ink dried and fined him — not for lying, but for writing. Anyone could read; only one hand could make a thing have happened.

One winter a spark took the hood and the book burned to the rim of its stand. The town did not mourn it; it discovered it. The bridge toll had been set in those pages, and with the pages gone no toll had ever been set, and the carts crossed free until a new line was written. A man who had been declared a thief in the burned leaves was let out of the stocks, since he had never been declared anything. The elders said aloud what everyone already knew: burn the pages and nothing was ever decided. The scribe was given a new book the next morning, and ten years of decisions were remade one line at a time, or not at all.

Each spring an examiner came from across the river. He asked no member what the congress had intended; intention had walked out at the dusk bell and gone home to forget. He asked the book. Two merchants disputed whether a rate had been fixed; the examiner went to the lectern, read the line aloud with its date, and the dispute ended — not because the book was honest, but because in this town honesty had no other address. Where no line existed, he ruled that nothing had occurred, and no memory, however vivid, could plead against the absence of ink.

Inside the hall the traveler counted three orders. Along the back wall stood the bronze delegates, iron figures that could not speak; when a question was put to them each dropped a white or black stone into a trough, and the stones were counted, and the count went to the scribe. In the middle were the ten choirs, each with its own manner — the one that sang of causes, the one that sang of costs, the one that sang of ruin — and they pulled the day's question to pieces. But only the five masks beside the door could put a question at all: the Mask of Coin, the Mask of Far Speech, the Mask of Works, the Mask of Memory, and the Mask of the Fool. When a mask was lowered over a face, that face could propose; and behind each masked speaker stood a second person whose whole office was to break the proposal — to prove it false, to find where it would drown. The Fool's breaker was said to be the gentlest and the most feared.

On the door of the hall five laws were nailed. Four concerned tolls, weights, and market hours. The fifth was the strange one: the cold cellar with the swift shelves, where the town's quickest goods lay ready to hand, had to hold more than fifteen barrels of emptiness, and each dusk a warden went down with a rod to measure the void. When the emptiness shrank to the mark, the morning bell stayed silent; the town spent the day clearing the cellar rather than deciding anything, for the congress could not convene over a full one.

On his last evening the traveler noticed the woman who sat against the square's wall, whom the children called the remembering-woman. She had attended every convening for forty years and carried all of it — every rate, every pardon, every face. She spoke freely to anyone who sat beside her. But no examiner had ever crossed from the lectern to her wall, and when she offered her memory at the hall the wardens let her talk only after the dusk bell, as amusement; her words entered no line and made nothing happen. She was the most complete archive in the town, and by the town's single rule nothing she had ever said had been said. The traveler bought a copy of the pages he had watched being written and left at first light, and by dusk the town had forgotten him.
