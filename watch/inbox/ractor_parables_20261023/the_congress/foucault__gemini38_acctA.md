# The Order of the Great Quadrangle

At dawn, the iron grates beneath the courtyards were raised, and three hundred delegates were conducted through separate limestone corridors into the central pavilion. They arrived without luggage, letters, or family tokens. Before entering, attendants washed each entrant's neck with vinegar, stripped away their cloaks, and bound them in identical mantles of gray wool. None brought memories or journals of yesterday's assembly; the guards ensured that every person carried no prior reckoning into the hall. They spoke only of the petitions presented that morning, meeting one another as strangers gathered in an empty room.

The pavilion was divided into three distinct spaces of observation beneath the clerestory, where every gesture was exposed to the light.

In the southern gallery stood the overseers of the realm's designated territorial posts, each stationed before a stone marker bearing the geographic boundary of his district.

Across the central nave stood the great assembly of artisans, distributed strictly into ten separate brotherhoods. The weavers did not mingle with the joiners, nor the glassblowers with the smiths; the carvers, masons, tanners, dyers, bell-founders, and brass-workers remained within the chalk rectangles of their respective crafts, communicating only through the customary gestures of their trade.

Upon the elevated northern dais rested five magistrates whose visors hid their faces completely. The first bore the visor of the moneyer, painted with coins. The second wore the visor of the foreign envoy, decorated with charts of distant coasts. The third wore the visor of the builder, carved with plumb-lines and brass gears. The fourth displayed the visor of the chronicler, inscribed with ancestral dynasties. The fifth wore the mocking visor of the fool, hung with dried bells.

Directly opposite each of the five magistrates stood an adversary charged with disproving their words. When the moneyer declared the treasury solvent, his adversary cast a lead counterfeit upon the marble to expose the hollow ring. When the envoy announced a border pact, his adversary produced a shattered frontier seal. When the builder attested to a wall's strength, his adversary struck the stone with an iron spike to find loose mortar. When the chronicler cited an ancient lineage, his adversary exhibited an older, contradictory burial urn. When the fool derided the proceedings, his adversary thrust a silver mirror before him to show his laughter was false. Unless the adversary failed to break the claim, the magistrate's counsel was nullified.

Before any decree could take effect, five ancient covenants had to be verified. The chief inspector carried an oil lamp down a spiral stair to the subterranean storehouse beneath the northern gate. He passed through the cellars to the lowest chamber and measured the floor with a notched rod: unless fifteen full spans of granite flagstone were swept completely bare of provisions, crates, and ballast, deliberations above were halted, and no decree could be ratified.

In the center of the nave stood a porphyry reading desk holding an unbroken parchment roll between two bronze spindles. A solitary scribe, his right forefinger stained black with gall ink, held the quill. When an accord was reached, he wrote beneath the previous entry. He never erased a letter or scraped the sheepskin; each entry was added directly under the last in a continuous downward line. When a novice asked what would occur if the roll were burned, the archivist replied that the empire would lose every statute, boundary, and judgment, for nothing had ever been decided except what was preserved in that ink.

At dusk, imperial examiners entered the pavilion. They did not consult the delegates, inspect the magistrates, or hear the oaths of the brotherhoods. Walking past the living assembly, they bent their spectacles over the parchment on the desk, transcribing solely what the single scribe had recorded. What was inked became law; what was spoken into the air ceased to exist. When the bells sounded, the gray mantles were cut away and burned, and the delegates were driven into the street, retaining no memory of the conclave and leaving no successor behind.
