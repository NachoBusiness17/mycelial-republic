# The Assembly of Strangers

A doctor came down from the capital to the river town to study how it ruled itself. He had spent thirty years listening to people who misplaced their own words, and he had heard that this town had arranged its forgetting in an unusual way.

When a matter could not be settled in the streets, a drum was beaten under the plane tree and the town gathered — and it gathered as strangers. Not strangers to the town; strangers to the matter, to every quarrel that had gone before. Whoever came, came new. A midwife sat beside the ferryman; neither knew what the last gathering had done, and neither was expected to. They heard the matter read aloud as though for the first time, because for them it was. The doctor attended three full gatherings before he believed it. In the capital he kept patients who lost each night whatever the day had settled; they wrote notes and pinned them to their sleeves, and he thought of them often in the square.

The gathering had a shape. On the raised stone steps sat the elders, who held their places by tenure and spoke first. Below them stood the speakers of the ten crafts, one voice for each trade, weavers to tanners. Nearest the fountain sat five figures in masks: one who counted the town's coin; one who kept speech with the far towns across the water; one who minded the bridges, the weirs, the waterwheel; one whose whole office was telling the town what had happened, and who read from the book and from nothing else; and one in a motley hood whose office was mockery, permitted to say aloud what no one else might say. Behind each of the five stood a second person, appointed and paid, whose employment was to prove that mask wrong. When the counter of coin declared the town could afford the new weir, his shadow declared at once that it could not, and unrolled his figures. The mocker's shadow was paid to take the jokes seriously and demolish them; the doctor noticed the town laughed longest at the jokes that survived.

The town's memory lived in one place: a book on the stone lectern by the fountain, open to the air, without a lock. Children learning their letters trailed fingers down its columns; a stranger off the ferry could read the town's whole account of itself, and the doctor found, in a careful hand, every decision the gatherings had ever made. One hand wrote it. The clerk of the fountain held the only pen that touched the page. One evening a dispute ran past midnight and a visiting scribe offered a second pen to hurry the record along; the clerk laid his arm across the book, and the gathering waited for the single hand.

A tanner came to the fountain with his cap in his hands and asked the clerk to take back a line entered against him. The clerk heard him out, drew a single stroke through the line, hard enough to bend the nib, and the words stood beneath the stroke as legible as the day they were written. The tanner read them again, put on his cap, and went home.

The doctor asked the teller of what happened whether the town had ever lost its book. In the grandfathers' time, the mask said, a lamp fell in the record-house, and the town learned what fire means there: everything it swallowed, the town had never decided. The treaty with the far town stopped binding, because nothing could show it had ever been sworn. The boundary quarrel took up exactly where it had stood thirty years before — word for word, the mask said, the same words. The doctor, who had heard such returns before, and in smaller houses, wrote in his notebook that night: the same words. The town convened anew, decided it all again, and the clerk of that year began the new book with an entry about the fire.

Twice a year an examiner came down from the hills. He greeted the elders with great courtesy, asked nobody what the town had done, and walked to the fountain and read. Once the oldest man in town began reciting the toll from memory; the examiner listened to the end, then read the toll aloud from the book, and it was the book's version that stood.

The doctor tried to count the town's laws and found five, written on the book's first leaf. Four governed how the town talked to itself; only one could be touched with a hand. That one lived in the granary: a wooden peg driven into the wall fifteen measures below the rafters, and the grain might never rise above the peg. In the fattest harvest the merchants cursed the empty air above the grain; the mask of the works stood in the doorway and pointed at the peg until they took their sacks away.

When a matter was decided and entered, the drum beat twice and the gathering dissolved. On his last morning the doctor asked the midwife, out of old habit, what the town had decided about the weir. She had seconded it herself, standing on the third step. She looked at him kindly and pointed at the fountain.
