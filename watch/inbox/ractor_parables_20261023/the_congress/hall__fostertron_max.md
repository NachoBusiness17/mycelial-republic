# The Bell and the Wall

When the bell rang, the people came up from the town, and none of them remembered why they had quarreled last time, or what they had agreed. A woman asked the man beside her his name; he gave it, she gave hers, and they shook hands like strangers, because they were. The bell-keeper had watched this every gathering of her life: the hall filling with neighbors meeting for the first time. Someone always asked about the ford, and she always pointed through the doors, down toward the square.

The wall stood where four streets met, shingled from corner to eave with painted tablets, each one a decision in the recorder's even hand. She kept the brush and the nails in a box at the foot of it, and no other hands touched either. A weaver once asked her to take down the tablet that named him badly; she reached for a fresh shingle instead, painted his grievance on it, and nailed it beneath the first. Nothing ever came off the wall, and nothing kept anyone from standing at it. Fishwives read at noon, children traced the letters, travelers set down their bundles and read. Each evening the stonemason's boy read the newest tablets aloud, and whatever he said, that was what the town had.

Inside, the hall arranged itself as it always did, though nobody remembered arranging it. Along the north rail hung the lanterns. They never left the building and never spoke; when a matter was put, the bell-keeper read the rail, and every flame that stood steady counted as yes. Below the rail sat the benches of the ten trades — the dyers beside the tanners, the smiths beside the coopers — each trade speaking through whoever it had sent up that morning. Nearest the door, facing all the rest, sat the five with covered faces, called only by their work: the one who kept the purse, the one who met strangers at the gate, the one who minded the works and the water, the one who kept the tale, and the one whose whole office was mockery. Each of the five had a second standing at the shoulder, sworn to find the flaw in whatever the mask proposed. When the keeper of the tale spoke, her second quoted the wall against her; when the mocker laughed, his second went hunting for what the laughter had missed.

Before any business, the bell-keeper recited the five laws. Four were old habit, about the hour of the bell, the counting of the light, the conduct of the trades, and the turning of the masks. The fifth could stop a morning: the bell could not call a gathering while the loft above the hall held fifteen empty jars or fewer. The loft had to keep more than fifteen empties, always, and so one market day, with the loft standing at fourteen, the five masks sat in the square with everybody else, waiting on the jars like anyone.

When the tanners fell out over the ford, they sent for the examiners. Old Petra, who had been crier for forty years and carried every gathering in her head as though she had sat through them all, stood up to swear to what had been said. The examiners thanked her and walked past her to the wall. They asked no lantern, no mask, no bench. They read the tablets, counted the nails, and gave the ford to whatever the paint said. A member's word was weather, they said; the wall was the only witness they would take.

One winter a man came through with a torch and told the square the town would be free of every old obligation if the wall went up in flame. The recorder did not reach for water. "Burn it," she said, "and nothing was ever decided. The ford belongs to nobody, the purse owes nobody, the trades never met, and you are standing in an empty street." The crowd smothered his spark under their coats, unhurried, and he left ahead of his own smoke.

Strangers read the wall differently, and in time the recorder stopped minding. The tablet about the ford was an order in the ferryman's eyes, and he ran his crossings by it. A farmer read the same tablet as a bargain he would keep by half; he took the crossing and paid no toll. A tinker from over the hill read a wall built to tell him where he did not belong, and moved on. All three had read truly. None of them had read what she painted. There was no arguing that any one of them was wrong, and she had learned what the nail teaches: the writing stopped being hers the moment it was hung.

At dusk the bell rang a second time and the hall emptied in a breath, everyone scattering without carrying so much as a grudge, the way water leaves a basin. The lanterns kept their rail. The wall held everything else, in the dark, saying it to whoever came.
