# The Basement and the Masks

Elin walked into the great hall at the crest of the quarry, where the estate settled its affairs. The hall was cold, smelling of tallow and wet lime. To the left stood the high benches where the wardens of the fixed towers sat, bound to their stone stations like fixtures of the masonry. Across the flags gathered the players, divided strictly into ten guilds of wandering actors, adjusting their greasepaint and trading robes of sorrow and triumph. At the center of the flagstones sat five figures whose faces were entirely hidden behind painted masks of lime-wood.

The first mask was gilded like a coin-purse, eyes shut tight to count by touch alone. The second was wrapped in the dust of foreign roads, speaking only the guttural dialects of the borderlands. The third wore an iron visor and carried a plumb-line, measuring the strain of every arch. The fourth wore a hood of moth-eaten vellum, muttering the names of dead kings and forgotten treaties. The fifth mask was split by a red, crooked grin, crowned with dried thistle, laughing when the others stiffened.

Yet none of the five sat alone. Directly opposite each mask sat a counter-witness—a silent challenger chosen not to advise, but to strike. When the gilded mask announced that the treasury was whole, the challenger tossed a split counterfeit onto the tiles. When the border envoy spoke of peace across the river, the woman facing him laid down a blood-stained arrowhead. Against the plumb-line, a boy set a plumb of lead that showed the masonry leaning; against the parchment of history, a clerk produced an older charter; and when the grinning fool mocked the solemnity of the room, the mute girl opposite him simply held up a mirror until his laughter died in his throat. No claim was permitted to stand until its shadow had tried its teeth upon it.

In the center of the hall, at a bronze desk, sat a solitary scribe with an iron bodkin. She did not look up when the speakers shouted. When a young warden stumbled over his own words, flushed red, and reached across the desk with a damp rag to wipe the wet clay smooth, the scribe brought the edge of her wooden ruler down upon his knuckles. "What is pressed into the earth belongs to the earth," she said. "The hand moves only forward. If you erase your blunder, you erase the rot that bred it." Later, an angry elder snatched a torch from the wall and threatened to bake the slab to ash. The scribe did not flinch. "Burn it," she whispered. "Burn the clay, and no man has gathered, no word was spoken, and nothing was ever decided in this house." The elder set the torch back into its iron ring.

When the bronze bell struck sunset, the gathering ended without ceremony. The wardens stood and walked away from their stone stations. The ten guilds of actors stripped off their cloaks, piled their false beards in baskets, and became common laborers once more. Elin caught a warden by the sleeve and asked him what the council had resolved concerning the quarry toll. The man stared through her with vacant eyes, shaking his head. "The bell has rung," he said. "The room is empty, and I carry no memory of what the stone heard. If you wish to know what happened, wait until morning and read the slab. In myself, I have no authority and no recollected truth."

Before dusk fell, an auditor arrived from the capital. He wore boots caked in yellow mud and carried a pouch of seals. Two wardens hurried to greet him, swearing upon their honor and their fathers' graves that the five ancient statutes had been upheld. The auditor walked past them as if they were mist. He did not ask for their names, nor did he look into their faces. He walked straight to the drying rack where the baked tablet was mounted behind iron bars. He placed his finger on the inscribed grooves, traced each mark from head to tail, and stamped his seal beneath the final line. To the living, he gave no ear; only the dead mark commanded belief.

Yet the tablet could not be carried to the archive until the cellarer had climbed up from the deep vault below the floorboards. The basement was where the estate stored its bulk—grain, tallow, and dry pine. Five ancient statutes governed the estate, and the fifth was the most ruthless: the cellarer carried an ash-wood measuring staff exactly fifteen paces in length. He had laid it upon the stone floor between the grain sacks and the northern foundation wall. An elder protested that the winter would be hard and the sacks ought to be stacked to the ceiling. The cellarer pointed to the floor. "A mind that packs its cellar to the beams will choke on its own hoard," he said. "Fifteen paces of bare stone must stay dark and empty, or the gate stays barred and no law leaves this hall."
