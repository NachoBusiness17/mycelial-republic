# The Assembly of Empty Vessels

Wren came down from her valley to the river town to learn how it ruled itself, because her own people were tired of quarreling from memory. She arrived on the eve of the convening. The grain hall was being swept, and the great book already stood on its stand beneath the east window, where the light fell on it and on anyone who came to read.

The pen belonged to an old woman named Osla, and it was the only reserved thing in the town. It hung from her belt on a cord. The book hung from nothing; it lay open to whoever wanted it, farmer, child, stranger, at any hour. Wren watched Osla sew new pages in. She never cut one out. When her own hand slipped, the slip stayed standing and the mending went beneath it, and both were read after.

In the morning the members came up from their streets carrying nothing. At the threshold a man who had spoken gloriously at the last convening tried to resume. "You agreed with me," he said, "before the frost." The hall did not argue. It waited while a boy fetched the book and read aloud what had been done before the frost, and it was not what the man remembered, and he sat down like anyone. Wren saw that in that hall a fine memory bought a man no more than a loud coat.

The assembly met in two parts. In the low hall stood members who were not people: a grindstone, a pump, a bell, a balance, and others of their iron kin, each with its keeper. When a question reached them, the keeper read the needle aloud, and the reading was the member's voice; one keeper was caught sweetening his needle, and he went out and did not come back. In the high hall the readers sat on ten benches, one bench to a trade — the dyers, the coopers, the tanners, and the rest, ten in all — and each bench gave one voice, whatever number of heads sat on it.

At the low hall's door the measurer worked with a rod notched at fifteen. He measured the empty room in each machine's bin before it could take its place. That day a new engine waited in the rain, tall and quick, its bin stuffed with its own hoard, and its keeper argued strength and youth. The measurer said nothing; he laid the rod against the emptiness, and the grain stood above the notch, and the door stayed shut. The first page of the book carried five founding lines, cut the year the town was begun, and the measurer's rod was one of them. Wren stood beside the keeper while he opened the bin and poured his engine's hoard into the mud, a little at a time, measuring, until the emptiness passed the notch and the door opened.

The five came in masked: the Purse, the Far-voice, the Works, the Long Count, the Laugh. Whoever wore a mask was nobody and the office both. At the threshold the Laugh tipped his mask up to drink, and the hall's eyes slid off him as off a stranger at a fence, until the mask came down and the office stood where the man stood. Each of the five kept a second figure at the shoulder whose whole trade was spoiling the first. When the Purse said the town could pay for the bridge, her spoiler turned the money out on the table and counted it against her, coin by coin, aloud, and the hall trusted the count and not the Purse. When the Works swore the old timbers would hold, his spoiler loaded them with stones until they groaned, and the Works thanked him and thickened his plan. Wren asked the Long Count why he fed so keen an enemy. "He is the other half of my face," the mask said.

A boundary quarrel rose between two farmers, each swearing his father's memory. The hall asked only one question, of each in turn: where is it written? One farmer had a line; the other had thirty years of remembering; the line won, though its owner was a newcomer who had never walked the field.

It was the bridge that got the book burned. The men whom the line had beaten carried it off by night and burned it on the mill steps, meaning to be quit of one decision. In the morning the hall convened and could prove nothing: not the bridge, not the midsummer debts, not the boundaries, not the five founding lines. Every quarrel in the town returned to its starting stone, and elders and newcomers stood level, equally empty. "I remember the vote," said the Works. "Then bring the bridge," said Osla, opening a new book, "and let it be voted before the pen." What the town still wanted, it enacted again, thing by thing, in front of her cord and needle; nothing else returned.

The bell ended the day and the hall came apart like water finding its channels, the members going out carrying nothing, ten benches bare, the low hall dark. The day's decisions existed in one place only, sewn paper under the east window, and the town slept around it the way men sleep near a banked fire.

Wren copied what she needed for her valley and left. On the road she passed the new engine in its field, bin still open to the sky, its keeper sitting on the empty sacks, letting the rain stay where it fell.
