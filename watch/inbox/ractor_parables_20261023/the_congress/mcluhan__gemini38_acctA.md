# The Basin of Inscription

At dawn, the counselors walk through the river mist to the basalt hollow. Before entering the gate, each dips their hands into a trough of cold lime-water and drinks from a bitter cup that wipes the palate clean of yesterday. They greet one another without titles or shared history; a man who shouted until his veins bulged at dusk now sits beside his adversary, nodding as strangers meeting upon an empty road. They deliberate in the present tense alone. When the evening chime sounds, the bronze gates open and they scatter into the hills, carrying neither slate nor token. By midnight they retain nothing of the morning's fury.

The hollow imposes three sensory tiers upon those who enter. In the outer ring, forty fixed stone plinths hold the elders erect and immobile, commanding their posture through cold granite. Below them, upon the packed sand, sit the ten circles of working craftsmen—smiths, weavers, potters, joiners, tanners, masons, carters, coopers, glassblowers, and ropemakers—each cluster speaking only the heavy, tactile idiom of their trade. At the center of the pavement stand five low stools for five men encased in bronze visages that warp their breathing: the Bursar with silver eyes, the Envoy with flared cochlear ears, the Artificer whose slit mouth drops a plumb line, the Chronicler hooded in reverse, and the Jester whose open teeth form a copper grate.

No bronze visage speaks unchecked into the acoustic circle. Across a narrow runnel of water stands a silent counterpart in unbleached wool. When the Artificer demands a stone aqueduct of three spans, his grey shadow places a curved wedge into the miniature ditch until the clay arch buckles and falls. Only when a counsel survives the shadow's silent refutation does the acoustic flare of the mask carry into the amphitheatre. The Bursar cannot announce a grain levy without his shadow sliding a hollow weight beneath the scales to test the balance.

Behind the central stools rise five monoliths carved with the primordial covenants. Before any voice may sound, the Warden of the Well lowers a knotted hemp line into the cavernous reservoir beneath the flagstones. The rope emerges dripping to the sixteenth knot, leaving fifteen clear cubits of dry air between the dark water and the stone floor. "The basin has breath," the Warden calls. Had the water risen a handbreadth higher, leaving less than fifteen cubits of vacant room, the gates would have been barred instantly, and the assembly turned away without speaking a word.

Every syllable that survives the floor must travel to a solitary limestone face. A lone carver in a bull-hide apron holds the bronze punch and the wooden mallet; no other hand may carry an iron tool. As a decree is reached, the carver drives his punch into the chalk, biting from left to right. The cut is permanent. When a speaker panics and begs to scrape away a sentence uttered at noon, the carver raises his hammer in warning. The stone knows no erasure. To remedy a folly, the carver can only strike a new line below the old, preserving the contradiction for every eye.

Two years prior, lightning shattered the north wall, tumbling the carved rock into the torrent below. The stone was ground to silt. When the counselors gathered at the next solstice, twenty men remembered the pasture boundaries and thirty swore to the cattle compacts, yet the herds were kept penned. Because the rock that carried the cut had perished, the covenant had ceased to exist. Voice without the fossil of stone leaves no residue; when the vessel broke, the decision dissolved into thin air.

At the autumn fair, an examiner enters from the northern pass. He wears no badge and carries no escort. When the elders on their stone plinths offer their ancestral pedigree, he looks past their bodies as if they were mist. He steps past the ten guilds and the five bronze visages without a nod, walking straight to the limestone face. There, where the daylight rakes across the grooves, he runs his thumb through the cuts, reading only the depth and sequence of the stone. He trusts no living throat; he interrogates only the public incision that stands bare to all who walk the road.
