# The Hollow Hall

Ona came down from the pass at dusk and asked at the first lit window where the valley's business was done. She was shown a hall cut into the hillside, its mouth glowing. Above the door a stretch of cliff had been whitewashed, and on it huge shadows swung — a lifted arm, a banner — thrown by the single lamp inside. A knot of townsfolk stood in the cold, watching the wall. "Is the meeting out here?" Ona asked. "The wall is," a girl said, and did not turn. Ona went past them into the mouth, to see what made the shapes.

On the floor, things were smaller than the wall had promised. Down one side lay a row of flat stones, each cut with an old mark, and on each stone stood one person, with others waiting behind to step up. That evening a woman stepped off her stone mid-argument and would not go back, and the stone said nothing more that night; the hall grumbled at her, not at the stone. Across the aisle, benches ran beneath ten stitched banners — netmenders, charcoal-burners, and the like — and when a banner spoke, it spoke once, whatever the number of throats beneath it.

At the head of the floor sat five figures in woven hoods: one who kept the strongbox, one who carried words to other valleys, one who tended the water and the bridge, one who held the tales of hard winters, and one in motley who was permitted to laugh at the other four. Behind each hood stood a second person, bare-faced, paid from the same purse, whose whole office was to prove the hood wrong, out loud. The strongbox hood said the town could pay for a ferry; the bare-faced one read out the count of coin and showed it could not; both were thanked, and the ferry died where it stood.

Between the stones and the benches stood a lectern turned to face the door, a book lying open on it so that anyone who entered could read the last page without asking leave. One old man wrote in it, with the only pen in the hall. A drover swore his name had been wrongly set down; the scribe did not scrape the page but wrote a fresh line beneath: the line above lies in this matter; the true name is thus. He wrote forward only, never backward, and every line ever set down was still in the book, struck or standing.

When the lamps were snuffed the hall emptied in minutes. Outside, Ona asked the drover what the winter sitting had decided about the ferry toll, for a groom swore the drover had stood the winter through. The drover shook his head. "My legs, maybe," he said. "What stood on the stones stays with the stones. Ask the book." At the mouth the doorkeeper was barring the doors; the sitting, he said, was whoever answered the bell, and when the bell went quiet the sitting was no one, and come spring the bell would call whoever came.

In the morning a carter stood before the man the valley sent for when two people swear differently, about a road he said the town had promised him. The carter brought neighbors. The hood of tales offered his own memory of the promise; he had stood in the hall the night it was made; it counted for nothing. The sent-for man read the book from the last page backward, found no line with the road in it, and closed the cover. "No line, no promise," he said. "The town speaks once, and it speaks there." Ona asked what would happen if fire took the book entire. "Then the hill would be a room where people once stood," he said. "Nothing was ever decided in it. There would be a new book, and a first line."

A browned leaf sat near the middle of the book. A lamp had fallen on it years back, across the lines of a ferry toll, and from that day the collectors at the water took no coin, though half the valley remembered paying; a fresh sitting had to be called by bell, and the toll written again on a new leaf before anyone paid again.

Before any sitting could open, a warden went down the stair beneath the hall to the cold bins and counted the empty ones aloud to the doorkeeper. That evening the count was fourteen. The doors stayed open. No stone was stood upon, no banner argued, the five hoods waited on their bench, and carters rolled casks out into the yard until the warden called sixteen. Then the doors closed, and the hall began. Ona asked the one in motley why. "Sixteen sits better than fourteen," he said, and would say no more.

Near the door, cut into the jamb, ran five lines of law, no more, worn dark by hands. A tanner moved that a sixth be cut beneath them, and though his own brother stood that night upon a stone, not one stone took up the motion, and the only line the scribe wrote was that it was refused.

Ona left at first light. The whitewashed wall stood gray and blank in the daylight, the townsfolk gone from it, and the shadows had been taller than the arms that made them. She had been inside, though. She had seen the lamp, and the floor, and the one pen.
