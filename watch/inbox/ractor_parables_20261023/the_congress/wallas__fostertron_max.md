# The Fifteenth Measure

In a town between two rivers, the hall bell was hauled on no fixed day. When it rang, the market was emptied by lot: whoever drew the marked beans climbed the steps — the tanner one week, a drover, a widow who sold eels — argued the town's business until dusk, and went home empty. Not empty-handed; empty. By supper the drover could not have told you what he had argued; by spring he would swear he had never climbed the steps at all. The town counted this no affliction. It kept one servant for whom nothing faded, a scribe who sat beneath the bell with a wide book across his knees, and whatever was decided under that roof went down under his hand, line after line, and no line ever came up again.

The book had enemies and admirers but only one hand. A draper, anxious to be seen deciding something, sent his clerk at noon to add a flattering sentence in the margin while the scribe ate. The whole town came out to look at the sentence as at a wound. The scribe could not lift it out — nothing is lifted out — he could only write beneath it, "this line is none of mine," and the two lines lie together still, and the draper sold his shop and moved to the coast. At nightfall the book was carried to an iron stand in the market and chained open, and the beggar read it first if the beggar arrived first.

Two neighbors once fell out over where the boundary ran. Both had sat in the hall the day it was walked, and both remembered it clearly, one of them falsely, and it made no difference: the assessor asked neither man anything. He had lived too long in that town to ask a body to be a page. He sent a boy to the stand, the boy read aloud, the boundary ran where the reading said, and the man who remembered falsely paid for the reading.

In the fever year a man who had lost the vote on the toll came by night and fed the book to his stove, and he got the whole of what he wanted. Through that winter the town had decided many things — a pardon, a boundary, the price of salt — and it had now decided none of them; whatever had lived only in the pages was never decided at all. The pardon dissolved like smoke. Nobody could even name his crime, since the only witness the town would hear was ash in his stove, and he walked free into a town that had quietly begun again from line one.

After the fever year the hall was set in order, and the order stood on three legs and five laws. In the high room the benches voted, each bench one voice, older than any sitter; whoever the beans set there in the morning spoke with that voice, and next day a different body sat in the same place and the bench said its say in a different mouth. A man once grew vain of his bench; he was reminded that it had said its say since before he was born and would go on saying it after. On the floor stood the ten troupes of speakers, each keeping to one fixed manner — one troupe told everything as a mourning, another as a sum — so that you never learned the mouth but always knew the music. Nearest the door hung the five masks: the Counter, who minded the purse; the Greeter, who met the strangers at the gate; the Keeper, who watched the works; the Teller, who carried the long story; and the Laughing One, who mocked the other four. Whoever took a mask down put on its wanting — the purse kept safe, the gate kept warm, the bridge kept standing, the story kept whole, the joke kept at everyone's expense — for the town had noticed that its five standing wants outlived every particular wanter. Each mask kept a shadow, and the shadow's whole office was ruin: when the Keeper pronounced the new bridge sound, the shadow loaded it with stone until the timbers cried, and only what could not be ruined was called the mask's word. The shadows were paid the same whether they broke the word or could not, and nobody in the town thought the money wasted.

The five laws were read from the first page before any talking, every ringing of the bell, for no head that climbed the steps had carried them up. One of them concerned the cold cistern under the hall, which was to hold better than fifteen measures of clear water at all times; the year the spring shrank, the town let the hall stand dark on fourteen rather than ring once against the law.

What the members kept of their service was the appetite only. They came down the steps unable to say what they had voted and certain the day had gone better for their being in it, and toward dusk you could find them at the iron stand, reading for their own names in the lines. The scribe, who had watched this hunger longer than anyone, gave each of them a name at least once — a crumb to every appetite — and the town called it kindness, which was also the oldest trick in town, and it worked daily. The book alone wanted nothing; everything that wanted ended by quoting it.
