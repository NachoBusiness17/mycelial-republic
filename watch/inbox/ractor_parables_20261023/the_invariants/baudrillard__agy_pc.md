# The Conservatory of the Third Reach

At the mouth of the grey estuary stood the Conservatory of Surveys, where draughtsmen laboured to make the painted plaster relief in the hall more convincing than the silt outside. Every autumn a new carver arrived to embellish the basalt tide-column on the quay. The latest, a gilder from the metropolis, climbed the scaffold with pumice to burnish smooth the jagged frost-cut of the ninth winter and recarve the equinox mark in wide lettering instead of the cramped three-notch gauge. The chief cutter caught his wrist. Every mark since the founding followed one iron template, with old scars left raw, and the chisel could bite only into blank stone above yesterday's highest line.

Inside the hall, four senior cartographers stood around the plaster basin of the delta. They had tinted its miniature channels with verdigris and shifted a tiny cedar block to show how dynamiting the south salt-weir would scour the harbor. Persuaded by the miniature current poured from a brass ewer, all four signed a demolition warrant and marched to the weir with powder-casks. The sluice-warden looked at their signatures, then pointed to the empty mortise in the iron housing. On the plaster table, a cedar block could be replaced ten times before noon; here, once granite was blown, the tide would drown the pastures forever. Until the Director herself set her notched iron bar into the mortise, the pin held.

That afternoon the fog-skiff returned from the middle shoals with its young sounder shivering at the oar. A whirlpool had overturned his basket; his waxed tallies had sunk into the peat-trench, and the cold shock had driven the morning's twelve soundings clean out of his head. He offered to sketch the channel from conjecture so the chart would show no gap. The chief cutter took his charcoal, knowing a guessed contour would pass for measured stone within a week. Instead, she sent him back to drive a slotted bronze pipe into the mud where his memory failed, so the rising tide whistling through the slots warned night-barges off the hidden bank.

Before sundown a coast-runner arrived carrying a vellum sheet stippled in sepia. It depicted a newly risen shingle-spit across the north channel, shaded with such microscopic care that the draughtsmen crowded round as if they could hear surf on the parchment. The runner demanded that the spit be chiseled onto the column before the packet-boats sailed. The assayer ignored the shading. She held out her palm for the sounding-lead's tallow-cup, which alone brought up bottom-gravel, and signaled the tug to haul a toothed iron harrow across the vellum's bearings. The runner had no tallow-cup, and the harrow dragged open water without snagging a pebble. The assayer burned the vellum unread by the chisel.

When the equinoctial squall struck at dusk, the storm-bell set the compound running. From the inner Assay Vault—where the confidential copper-and-bismuth alloy of the standard counterweights was mixed behind double oak doors—three smelters sprinted into the crowded Draft-Court carrying the master balances toward high ground. Moving at a dead run while shutters banged and porters shouted haul-counts, the youngest smelter opened his mouth to call out the bismuth ratio so the porters would know which chest was heaviest. His boot struck the raised lead sill between vault and court, and his teeth clicked shut; even at full stride, vault speech died at the sill.

Up in the meridian turret, the old night-observer died of a stopped heart with his cheek still resting against the brass transit. His two assistants did not cover the glass or descend to compose a eulogy. While the brass remained warm from his skin, they drew the three braided silk plumb-lines of his unfinished star-sweep together and bound them in a single tight hitch around the locking-pin, capturing the exact azimuth and tension where his breath ceased. Ten minutes later the relief observer climbed the stair, laid her palm on the still-warm brass, slipped the hitch onto her fingers, and called the next star transit mid-arc.

In the spring an envoy arrived from the ministries bearing a lacquered scroll of thirty-six ordinances, complete with vermilion seals, and asked why no copy hung on the Conservatory walls. For answer, the chief cutter locked away every chart and brought five newly hired quarry-boys into the yard, handing them the three-notch template, the mortise-bar, the slotted pipe, the tallow-lead and harrow, the lead sill, and the hitched plumb-lines. By noon, without instruction, the boys had reconstructed from the tools and stones alone every working custom of the quay—while none could guess a single ministerial ordinance concerning festooned barges or scarlet cuffs.
