# The Public Colonnade

Julian came to the colonnade to inspect the preparations for the provincial fair. As an advance agent, he understood the staging of belief: crowds did not gather for foundations; they gathered for gilding and promises. But this courtyard was paved with unpolished granite, and the men who worked it moved without trumpets or heralds.

In the central portico, an artisan stood before a bronze pillar whose faces recorded every consignment of stone, every wage, and every split timber. A young secretary with a scraper was pleading with him. A cross-beam had cracked two days prior, and the regional commissioners were due at dawn; the boy argued that a quick stroke could smooth the fracture away and replace it with an ornate flourish to reassure the patrons. The artisan did not pause his mallet. He set his punch against the bare metal at the lowest edge of the column and drove the mishap into the bronze in the same plain lettering that began forty cubits above. The pillar, he said, moved only downward toward the flagstones; it accepted no backward scraping, and every failure entered the record in the exact format of the harvest.

Beyond the stables, rain had swollen the mountain torrent until water lapped the south revetment. If the embankment gave way, the main hall would be submerged. A crowd of forty carpenters gathered at the heavy windlass controlling the upper sluice. Tripping the pawl would divert the torrent into the valley orchard, permanently drowning three generations of almond trees to preserve their timber. The crowd shouted to trip the catch; two foremen wedged crowbars under the locking ring. Yet the chain was secured by a tumbler with no keyhole on the working yard. Though forty men threw their weight against the levers until the ash groaned, the catch held fast. They stood drenched in the mud until the warden walked down from the ridge, examined the watermarks with his lantern, and turned his private key. Until his hand turned, the crowd could only hold their shovels.

In the northern yard, an entire bay of scaffolding had collapsed in the gale, dropping pine timbers and split slate into the mire. Julian expected the yard-hands to cart the wreckage to the ravines before visitors arrived to conceal the failure. Instead, the steward ordered the carts emptied. Teams were set to plane the shattered beams into leveling laths for the masons, the fractured slates were crushed to temper mortar for the foundation trench, and the bent nails were drawn at the forge into ties. Not a single barrow of debris left the gates; every fragment of the ruined bay was converted directly into the tools required to raise the next wall.

At midday, an ambitious contractor appeared at the paymaster’s bench, boasting that his crew had cleared five leagues of forest and inspired hundreds of volunteers to join the enterprise. The chief assessor did not return his smile. She set a brass scale on the counter and demanded the timber tallies and the stamped receipts from the charcoal-burners. When the contractor admitted he carried no tallies and spoke only from the cheering of the countryside, she closed the register without lifting her pen. When an assistant whispered that they might record a round approximation to bolster public confidence, the assessor snapped the assistant's quill, struck his name from the guild roll, and had guards escort him outside the palisade before the noon bell ended.

Toward dusk, Julian followed the directors into the curtained room behind the rostrum, where a dispute broke out over an unrecorded debt and a private betrayal among the founders. Voices rose to fury in the dim chamber. Two couriers stood waiting for the night work-orders. When the assembly trumpet sounded outside, the curtain was torn open, and a rushing crowd of townsfolk and stagehands swept into the corridor clamoring for directives. The couriers were caught in the surge, pressed shoulder to shoulder with citizens demanding news of the council's decisions. The boys shouted orders for the lanterns and the cattle-gates, their voices hoarse above the roar. Yet through all the crush and momentum, neither boy let slip a single syllable of what had passed behind the curtain. The rush carried their bodies, but the private words died at the threshold.

Above the main arena, an ironworker slipped on a frosted strut and fell through the mist to the flagstones. The fall was fatal. The work did not halt in shame, nor did the masters throw a canvas over the arch to hide the loss from the public. The master rigger climbed down, unbuckled the dead man’s harness, and spliced the severed end of his rope into an eye-knot on the central hoist. When the replacement arrived at dusk, the crew made room for him by the brazier, handed him the knotted line, shared their loaf, and pointed him back to the iron.
