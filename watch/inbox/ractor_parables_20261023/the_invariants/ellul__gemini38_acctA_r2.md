# The Pillar and the Sluice

Beside the canal intake, an oak pillar carried the cut marks of forty seasons. Each dawn the elder pressed an iron gouge into the grain, incising the water's height and the baskets of silt lifted from the race. When an apprentice miscounted at midday and took a rasp to scrape the notch away so his afternoon sum would appear seamless, the warden caught his wrist. "The wood does not un-happen," he said, taking the file. He set the chisel an inch lower and carved the true count beneath the blunder, leaving the crooked notch untouched above it. For two generations every entry had preserved the same three vertical slashes and crossbar, marching strictly down the trunk toward the earth; no stroke was ever planed away to give the apparatus an unblemished face.

By afternoon the runoff gorged the timber flume. The diversion wheel spun until its bronze counterweights flared wide, trembling against the trip-levers built to drop the storm gate and sacrifice the lower orchards to save the race. The boys backed away, expecting the cogs to release the weight on their own. Yet the gate hung immovable on its chains. The works had been constructed to measure the flood, but the release-pin rested inside an iron hasp that answered only to the warden's heavy key. The apprentices cried out that the stone flume would burst if the machinery was not permitted to save itself. The warden walked through the flying spray, felt the mortar seams with his bare hand, and only then turned the iron. The works could groan, but they were never granted the right to drown the valley of their own accord.

Before dusk a carter brought casks from the pine kilns, demanding grain vouchers at the gate. He swore the resin was boiled thick to the bung, and a youth reached for the marking chalk to credit him. The elder thrust an iron rod through the stave; it emerged dripping thin creek water beneath a film of pitch. When the carter argued that the sun had separated the oils, the elder did not debate the heat. He unhooked the team, backed the wagon into the road, and barred the timber against the man for good. What had not sunk the scale or left its mark upon the rod did not enter the yard; unmeasured assertions were treated as trespass.

In the damp cellar, two bins of winter rye had sprouted into black mold. A shoveler began loading the spoiled mass into barrows to heave it down the ravine. The elder stopped the barrow at the sill. "We do not shovel our failures into the river," he said. He had the rotten mash dumped into the charcoal pits, where its fermented starch bound the charcoal dust into dense fuel blocks that fired the lime kiln hotter than dry oak. The mortar that sealed the new masonry was baked by the heat of the ruined harvest; every loss was broken down until it yielded the binding grit for the next wall.

When the flood surged at twilight, messengers ran between the inner counting cellar, where personal pledges and village debts were weighed behind heavy felt curtains, and the outer weir where sixty men hauled timbers against the drift. The work was fierce, and the roar of the water tempted men to shout whatever crossed their minds to save a step. Yet the runner who burst through the curtain with mud on his knees spoke only of the timber shoring. The whisper he had heard in the small room concerning an elder's broken pledge died behind his lips. Even under the violent momentum of the river, the curtain held; what was spoken in the quiet was never carried into the yard.

Near midnight the hemp haul-line that hoisted the weir beams snapped under tension with the crack of a dry branch. The severed cord lay limp across the gravel. The crew rushed toward the storeroom to haul out fresh rope, eager to abandon the broken length. The warden barred the door. He knelt in the mud, drew the frayed ends together, and spliced them into an interlocking crown-knot. He plunged the spliced joint into a pot of boiling tallow until the hemp drank the fat, and while the cord was still steaming, laid it back over the bronze sheaves. The cable groaned as the strain returned, but it held, turning the drums with the hard, swollen knot riding in plain view.

At daybreak an inspector from the capital walked the dikes, demanding the written code that governed the sluice and authorized the carter's expulsion. The elder pointed to the notched pillar, the chained release-pin, the weighted iron rod, and the knotted haul-line. "If three men who work this weir cannot look at these timbers and deduce why the key hangs from a human belt and why the pillar is never scraped," he said, "then the practice is only the vanity of an overseer, and the river does well to wash it away."
