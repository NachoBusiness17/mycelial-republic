# The Margin and the Day-Book

I came to the house on the bend in October, riding the circuit with two letters and a judge's distrust of testimony. My trade keeps me among people who say things warmly; I had heard this house said things coldly and then kept them, and I wanted to see the instrument that did the keeping.

The steward received me in the counting room, where the day-book lay under a brass frame that had ruled its pages — line, column, margin — since the founder's day; four hands had written across sixty years, and every page matched the first. Each evening he entered the day's grain and moved the ribbon marking the living page one leaf toward the back. On my third morning his son blotted a figure and begged to scrape it out; the steward ringed the blot, set his mark beside it, and wrote the true figure on the line below. A page torn by a dog years before hung sewn home with thread, a note of the tearing beside the seam. I never saw the ribbon move backward.

The yard worked otherwise: the crews stacked and unstuck, sewed, hauled and unhaulled all day on no one's leave, but acts that could not be taken back were not theirs. The stamp that bites copper, the axe at an elm's root, the kiln's firing, salt on a field — these lay behind the wicket, where an old woman kept the keys, turned them only when asked, and never in the dark. One night a rider came at the gallop crying rot in the west granary and wanting it fired before dawn on his own word; she heard him out, set his torch behind her stool, and told him morning was six hours off. By daylight the rot sat in the sacks nearest the door alone; those were burned one by one, and the granary stood.

Fire took the west barn in my second week, frost the orchard in my third. The household mourned on Sunday and worked on Monday. The ash was weighed, the nails counted, and both went into the book. The charred beams became the lintel of the new gate; the dead trees were cut into rakes; the last apples of the killed rows were pressed, and that cider was drunk at the lintel-fitting with the trees' names said over it. Asked why he weighed ash, the steward showed me the line where the fire had bought back its own nails.

I am fond of marvels and gave him one: no pile of yard stones stacked in a triangle could be counted into a perfect square, though I possessed a demonstration so short it wanted only the breadth of a margin — his guest-book's was too narrow. He brought a page as wide as a tablecloth; my demonstration grew no shorter, and it stayed in the margin, where he said margins belonged. The shepherd swore the new ram outweighed the bull; the steward handed him the balance, and until the beam tipped, the claim was a sound, not a figure. At the feast, two bowls of hare went to the judges with a blank slate, the naming card locked in the stillroom drawer; a bowl no tongue could tell from its fellow was not entered as better. A peddler's boast at the gate was answered with a demand for a smaller instance, and a smaller still, until the man stood with nothing under his claim.

At midwinter a messenger brought word of riders on the upper road, and the yard turned all hurry — bells, dogs, steam. The household crowded into the small room behind the kitchen and shut the door, and what was said in there did not come out, though the yard stayed loud until dawn; when the master emerged, no one asked him anything. At the feast I began in the hall a phrase caught through that door; the maid set down her jug, and the sentence would not finish itself in that room. The drunk carter, pressed to tell what passed in the stillroom, laughed into his cup; his mates laughed too, and nothing crossed over with him.

In February the old woman at the wicket died in her sleep. They tied her bell-cord into a hard knot, worked tallow into it, and hung it above the fire; no one touched it for the rest of the winter. On Candlemas the warmth had got into it; the youngest dairymaid worked it loose over an evening, the cord went back to the bell, the chair was turned to the warm side of the hearth, and in spring her name was given to the filly at the home farm.

That dairymaid had arrived in November knowing nothing, and the house taught her nothing but the work. She learned the rest by living in it: she ringed her own blots, she carried the branding-iron to the wicket and waited with it, she left in the small room every word she found there. The steward, though, had salted his threshold every Tuesday for thirty years; she, deriving the house from the house, salted nothing, and watching her not salt it, he gave the practice up that week as something the house had never asked of him.

I rode away in spring and wrote to my correspondent in the capital that I had neighbored that house's reasons all winter without possessing one of them — and that the possession was the house's to keep.

1. The house's book takes only additions: the frame that rules the pages was never altered, blots are ringed and marked rather than scraped out, corrections are new lines beneath, a torn page is sewn home with a note of the tearing, and the ribbon marking the newest page moves only toward the back (the counting room, the son's blotted figure, the dog-torn page).
2. Deeds that cannot be undone — the stamp, the felling, the firing, the salt — are reserved to the single keeper at the wicket, and the crews never perform them on their own word, however fast the errand arrives (the rider's torch set behind the stool, the granary that waited for morning).
3. Every loss is made to work and made to enter: ash weighed, nails counted, charred beams raised as the new lintel, dead trees cut into rakes, so nothing is merely mourned (the barn fire and the frost, the cider at the lintel-fitting).
4. A claim enters the book only after it is weighed or survives blind judges, the naming card locked in another room while a blank slate goes to the table; what cannot be shown stays spoken or in the margin, never entered (the ram and the balance, the two bowls of hare, the tablecloth page).
5. Boasts are broken by demanding a smaller instance of them, and a smaller still, until the claim stands with nothing under it (the peddler at the gate).
6. Words stay in the room where they were spoken, even with the yard at full gallop and the wine out, and a thing raised in the wrong room will not finish there (the messenger night, the unfinished sentence in the hall, the carter's laugh).
7. A death is tied into a knot kept by the fire through the winter, untied in its warmth at the year's turning, and the name returns to use warm (the wicket keeper's bell-cord, Candlemas, the filly).
8. A custom is a law of the house only if a newcomer taught nothing but the work can arrive at it again from living there; what she cannot arrive at, the house gives up (the dairymaid who salted nothing, and the steward's thirty Tuesdays abandoned).
