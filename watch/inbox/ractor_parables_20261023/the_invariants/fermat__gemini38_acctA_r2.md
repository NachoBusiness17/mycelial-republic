# The Measure of the High Basin

In the cloister wall, an unbroken beam of heartwood was mortised into the masonry. For seventy seasons, the estate's yields had been incised into its grain with a three-pronged iron gouge, every notch three barleycorns wide. When an apprentice, dismayed by an errant tally of rye, brought a draw-knife to shave the timber flush, Arnaud stayed the youth's hand with his cane. He pointed to the unbroken row of marks stretching seventy paces from the dark corner toward the sunward door. Where a false measure had been struck three years prior, the wood still bore the flawed groove, with the true count chiseled immediately to its right in the same three-finger width. The apprentice put away his blade, took up the gouge, and cut the day's delivery at the far right edge of the grain.

Above the terraces stood the retention sluice, where mountain runoff pressed against the oak gates. When an autumn torrent swollen by sudden rain threatened the causeway, twelve draymen ran to the capstan to throw open the flood. Together they had the force to snap the pawl and release the deluge into the gorge. Yet they stood still in the downpour. The iron windlass was bound by a hardened padlock, its key hanging from the surveyor's belt alone. Though every man on the gang shouted to loose the river and had the muscle to do it, not one laid a hand to the chain. They waited in the driving mud until the surveyor walked down from the high cottage, unlocked the shackle with his own key, and threw the counterweight.

At the press-house, a cart overturned three earthen jars of walnut oil, shattering the clay across the stones. The steward did not lament the ruin, nor did he sweep the slick into the ditch. He bade the boys shovel dry wood-ash over the pool until every drop was bound into paste, packed into fir casks to feed the stable lamps through the winter frost. The clay shards were carried to the kiln, ground to grog, and worked into the mortar of the bread-ovens. By nightfall, the yard was clean, the lamps were filled, and the oven bricks were drying in the hearth.

At noon, an itinerant builder appeared before the council, displaying a block of grey mortar that he claimed would endure twenty winters and bear five hundred quintals without a crack. He demanded silver and immediate inscription upon the rolls. Arnaud listened without dipping his pen. He took a two-inch cube of the mixture, placed it beneath the iron screw-press, and directed the smith to turn the lever. At eighty quintals, the cube sheared and burst into dust. The builder stammered an excuse of damp weather, but Arnaud wiped the powder from the iron face, capped the ink, and turned the man away without writing a single line upon the parchment.

Two couriers galloped into the yard with urgent news regarding the hidden debts of the grain syndicate in Toulouse. The common hall was loud with sixty reapers eating noon soup, and the riders, breathless with haste, began to blurt the sums aloud. Arnaud checked them with an uplifted hand. He drew them behind the double-bolted oak door of the vaulted archive, shutting out the hall. There, behind stone walls three cubits thick, he examined the parchments, burned the wrappers in the hearth, and set the figures into cipher. Returning to the hall to dispatch the wagons, he spoke only of harness-leather and road gravel, leaving the reapers to finish their bowls without ever learning why the horses had run so hard.

On the high terrace, old Master Raymond, who held the sighting-rods for the stone levels, collapsed quietly against a cairn as dusk fell. His breath ceased, and his plumb-line dropped into the grass. His apprentice did not abandon the post or flee downhill. With steady hands, the youth tied a scarlet cord around the wooden peg where the master had taken his final sight, and inscribed the angle upon his slate. He drew a cloak over the old man, carried the brass sighting-rod to the hearth, and set it among the warm ashes. At sunrise, another surveyor came up from the village, drank broth by the fire, lifted the warm rod, and walked straight to the scarlet cord, continuing the slope without a degree misplaced.

On the last evening of the season, Arnaud gathered the apprentices in the chapter-house. He took away their inscribed slates and books of precedent, leaving them only a tray of fine sand and a wooden stylus. He commanded each to demonstrate from first principles why the water-races must never diverge at an acute angle, and why the terrace walls must taper by two fingers for every cubit of rise. Five youths drew the triangles in the sand, deriving the angles from the weight of earth and the rush of water. But the sixth could only say that his grandfather had always dug the ditches so. Arnaud struck the grandfather's customary mark from the estate roll before nightfall, leaving the sixth apprentice with an empty tray until the boy could discover the geometry for himself.
