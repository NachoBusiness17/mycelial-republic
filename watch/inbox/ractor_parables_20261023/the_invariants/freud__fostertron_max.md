# The House That Repeated

I came to the house at the river bend in a wet October, because the town had diagnosed it for me: its people did the same things at the same hours, they repeated, something in them could not stop. I brought a notebook and my patience, as one does.

The first repetition I was shown belonged to the mason. Each evening she set one brick at the far end of the long wall — never in the middle, never nearer the house, only at the growing end. Every brick carried the same marks in the same order as the founder's, which still stands at the far end, worn soft. One evening I watched her set a brick and stand over it a long time: the count was wrong; the flock had been fewer than the herdsman swore. She did not take it up. It stayed where it lay, and the next evening she set the true number against it, and both are there now, so that anyone walking the wall can read the whole quarrel.

Numbers did not reach that wall by mouth. The herdsman brought his claim to the kitchen door twice; the third time the mason handed him the counting-cord and pointed him back toward the field, and only what passed before the wall, counted where the wall could see it, was ever laid into it. Years before, a man had brought a hollow weight to the autumn weighing. The household fed him that night, and in the morning a boy walked him to the road, and he was never taken back; his bricks are still in the wall, hollow weight and all.

In my second winter the granary sickened. By midnight everyone knew: the sick grain must burn before dawn or the sound sacks would take the rot. The fire was laid in the yard — and then the strangest thing I saw: they stood in the cold and let it be. Every hand was willing; they had agreed among themselves; waiting cost them. A boy had gone out into the dark at a run. At first light a man came in from beyond the field, snow on his shoulders, heard the count of it, said, "Light it," and stayed while it burned. When I asked the cook why they had waited, she looked at me as if I had asked why the wall does not walk backward.

It was not helplessness. They bought, planted, quarreled, hired, lent, and forgave on no word but their own. Only the acts that could not be taken back — the burning, the felling, the selling of ground — waited for the man from the road. In four seasons I never saw that line break, not even the night the river rose and half of them wanted the dike opened; they argued through till he came.

What was said in the small blue room stayed there. Money is fought over in that room twice a year, with old scores, and not a syllable crosses the threshold — not at the table, not at the well, not at the spring feast when the brothers came out red-faced, one foot already in the yard, and had to stop at the doorframe and turn back in. When I asked the youngest what they quarrel about in there, he said, "In there," and gave me more porridge.

Nothing was let go unmade. The flood took the footbridge in spring; by autumn its planks were the long bench under the pear tree. The grey mare died; her harness came back as the strap of the well-bucket. When fire took the loom-room shelf, the charred beam was shaped into the lintel of the new door, black side inward, so the house passes under its own history every day.

When the old mason died — not my mason; her grandmother, who had laid the founder's shape into the first two hundred bricks — they tied a knot in the thick rope that hangs beside the hearth, and named it aloud, and no knot there is ever untied. In spring her grandniece walked up from the south with the same square hands. I braced for suspicion. Instead the girl ate first that night and slept by the fire, and for two days no one asked her anything. The knot, the cook told me, was why: the rope knows where the old one ended, so the new one comes in free.

At midwinter the household did the thing the town would have found strangest of all. They walked the newest member, that summer's boy, from one end of the wall to the other, through the rooms, and had him tell them the rules of the house. He got them all out of the bricks and the rope and the benches and the doorframe. Then the eldest said there had always also been a rule about the high branch of the pear tree — and they looked, and no one could find it anywhere, and the eldest laughed at himself, and the high branch was let go like a bird that had never been in the hand.

I left in spring, having cured no one. In the city now I write my day at the bottom of a page and never once cross out; when I am wrong I set the correction on the line beneath and leave both. Last week a colleague read over my shoulder and asked what this compulsion was. I heard myself answer, "The growing end," and closed the notebook gently, the way one closes a door in that house.
