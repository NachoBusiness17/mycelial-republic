# The Decoding Chair

The sender always imagines the message belongs to him, but meaning is made only where the runner halts and the stranger decodes the mark. Across the headlands of the Sound, the watch had learned this early: a fire on the ridge is merely heat until the receiving bench reads the smoke and sets its hand to work.

The first rule was cut into the great pine pillar of the harbor house. Each day’s account of wind, water, and timber was notched into the wood with a gouge three fingers wide, always in the same vertical channel, cut one below the other down the grain. When an apprentice made a crooked incision and reached to shave the surface flat, Elin caught his wrist. What entered the pine remained in the pine. If an error had been cut, the correction could only be gouged into the fresh wood below, moving only toward the earth. The spacing and breadth of the cuts remained identical to the first notches struck twenty winters before, for a reader cannot decipher a record that bends backward to flatter its maker.

The gales of autumn tested the stations. When a storm surge breached the breakwater, the signalmen shouted through horns that the sluice had to be dropped and the bridge burned to save the grain. Any watcher along the cliffs could have knocked out the iron pin holding the counterweights. The salt tide swirled past their knees, yet no watcher raised his mallet. They lit the yellow beacon and waited. Only when the overseer arrived by skiff and placed his bronze disc upon the stone sill did the men drive the pin. The towers could broadcast danger to the horizon, but they were forbidden to grant themselves the power to destroy what could never be rebuilt.

When the fourth tower collapsed beneath black ice, carrying its lamps and oil into the ravine, the crew did not abandon the post or rebuild blindly on sliding rock. They dragged the splintered cedar beams to the windward rim, pinning them into a breastwork that shielded the trail from the gale. The charred tallow from the spilled casks was boiled into a dark pitch that waterproofed the tow-ropes for the winter. Every loss was converted into a working shield for the living, so that even disaster was made to pay tribute to the post.

At midwinter a courier rode through the gate, shouting that five hundred cattle had crossed the shallows and the famine was broken. Elin did not dip his reed in ink. He held out his palm for the tally-sticks notched at the ford and the counter-stamped bone tokens from the scales. When the courier admitted he had only seen the dust from a bluff and guessed the herd by its cloud, Elin closed his inkbox. The courier was stripped of his horn, cast outside the palisade before sundown, and dismissed forever. No claim entered the pillar without its physical counter-weight in measured bone; an unmeasured boast was barred from touching the wood.

Behind the great stove, the elders met behind hangings of doubled wool to weigh which storehouses were empty and which families must take half-rations. Messengers hammered on the outer doors, shouting rumors and demanding grain warrants. The wool curtains did not stir. Within the circle, the elders spoke in flat murmurs; not a whisper of their reckoning slipped into the hall, even when the crowd shook the iron bolts. The momentum of the panic died against the heavy cloth. What belonged to the room remained inside the room, and no secret echoed beyond its boundary.

When old Kael died during the freeze, the watch did not leave his post vacant or wipe his mark from the board. They took his braided hemp line, bound it into a tight double bight, and hung the knot above the hearth embers. When a girl from the high pastures arrived three mornings later to take his horn, they did not send her cold into the storm. They lifted the knot from the chimney, loosened the bight before the fire until the hemp smelled of tallow and peat, and settled the warm cord into her hands. She stepped into the drift with the stride of someone who had kept the watch for forty years.

In the spring, a traveling magistrate arrived with three chests of parchment, asking why the boys always slacked the tow-lines when the river met the ocean bar. He demanded to know which charter commanded the pause. The boys did not know the magistrate’s books, nor could they name the founder who first built along the Sound. Instead, the youngest girl dropped two pine cones into the current and pointed to the eddy where fresh water met salt, showing how the line snapped if pulled taut across the shelf. The magistrate closed his chest. Because the crew could re-derive the rule from the water itself, the law was alive; had they only known how to recite the decree, the post would have failed long ago.
