# The Wall That Only Grew

A woman walking the river road came at dusk on a settlement she had not known was there. Behind the houses stood a wall of fired-clay panels, longer than any street, and an old man was cutting marks into the last of them by lamplight. His hand slipped, and a false stroke went in deep. She waited for him to press the clay flat and begin again. He did not. He cut the true stroke beneath the false one and went on. "It went in," he said. "It stays. The next line says what it should have said." She saw then that all the lamplight and all the cutting were at the near end of the wall — the newest, which moved each year a little farther into the field.

At the far end the clay was gouged. One panel had been scraped nearly through, as though someone had tried to drag the marks out, and the panels on either side were scorched. A grandmother stood there with her bread. "We did that," she said, "the year we tried to live without writing anything down. Ask the granary." The granary was new, but its longest beams were old and charred along one face, and they had been set with the burned side outward, so that all who came for grain walked past the black of it. The traveler put her fingers in the scrape. Nobody had ever filled it.

In her second spring the river rose brown and fast, and the word went round that the bridge must be cut or the fields would drown. Men ran with axes and rope. At the bridgehead they all stopped — mud to the knee, shouting — and stood away from their own tools, because the woman who did such things lived beyond the fence, of no house there, and she was sent for. She came down unhurried, asked them three times whether they asked it of her, and cut the bridge when they had answered three times. That night they mended fences with their own hands. The bridge they left alone; it was hers to have cut, and it could not be uncut.

That summer a fisher claimed a sturgeon longer than his boat, lost overboard. The keeper of the wall asked him where it was; he had nothing to show, and nothing was cut. In autumn a potter was caught at the wall by moonlight, pressing a stamp of a good yield into clay that had never been weighed. He was not beaten and not forgiven. His stamp stayed in the wall; beneath it, next day, a mark was cut naming what he had done; and he walked out of the valley with his tools and never came back. They still read the potter's mark to children.

During the flood the household had met in the small room with the door shut, and by the low sound of it the traveler understood that hard words were being said in there about who had let the weir rot. The words never came out. Years later, in a quarrel of her own, she found them in her mouth, ready — and saw everyone in the square holding theirs in too, and the quarrel had to stand on its own strength. What the shut room swallowed, the shut room kept.

When the storm felled the elm beside the school, the trunk did not go for firewood. It was squared and set on four stones, and the children learned their letters on it before winter; the mark cut for the elm named not its death but its table. The stones hauled out of the drowned meadow became the new weir. Nothing that broke, sank, or burned left the settlement without becoming something with a use, and the wall said what it had become.

The old carver died in the deep of winter. They tied one hard knot for him in the hearth-cord, let the fire down to a single ember, and sat the night through in the cold without speech, the knot in view. At dawn they fed the kept ember, and the bread went in, and the room warmed, and by noon the young man who had been given the chisel was cutting at the newest end, his marks in the same shapes as the oldest marks on the wall.

In her fifth spring the traveler was called to the middle of the houses and asked to say what she had watched, for no one had ever taught her. She spoke of the slipped stroke and the line beneath it; of the bridge and the woman from beyond the fence; of the sturgeon that never entered the wall and the potter who walked out of the valley; of the door that had stayed shut; of the elm that became a table; of the knot, the cold, the kept ember. The elders listened and corrected nothing. That year they had argued for a bell to end the markets early; no newcomer ever arrived at that bell by watching; and the bell was left unrung, and no one spoke for it again.

She stayed, and in time the chisel was hers. She cut at the newest end while behind her the panels ran back and back to the gouged one and the scorch, which no one had filled in, and no one was going to.
