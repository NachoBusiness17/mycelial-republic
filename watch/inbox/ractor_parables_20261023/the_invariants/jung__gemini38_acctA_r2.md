# The Undercroft

Eber walked down the granite stairs where the mountain held its damp. No light came from above; the only glow seeped from the vent-slits of the lower works, smelling of bitter tallow and coal. He had come to see whether the house still rested on stone, or whether it had begun to deceive itself about its foundations. The men upstairs spoke of clean columns and unblemished silver, but Eber knew that every structure casts its heaviest weight into what it refuses to own.

At the base of the shaft stood a black basalt pillar rising into the rock vault. An artisan knelt beside it with a hammer and a square punch. Along the dark stone ran a continuous groove cut into identical flutes, each stamped with the day’s measure. A flake sheared off crooked, but the artisan did not scrape the mark smooth or plaster over the nick. He did not alter the width of the furrow or turn the chisel back toward the ceiling. He seated his punch at the lowest edge and struck the next blow, advancing the furrow an inch deeper into the rock. What had been struck crooked remained crooked, visible to any palm that traced the cut.

In the second gallery, the furnace belly groaned under slag, choking the chimney. The floor boss wiped grease from his neck while three young smelters gripped an iron sledge. Between them and the spill-drain hung an iron bolt pinned through the hinge by a wooden peg. A single blow would have shattered the wood and emptied the crucible, yet the boss caught the striker’s shoulder and held him fast. Neither he nor his crew would raise an iron to that pin. Instead, an apprentice ran up the ladder to the high grate where an old warden sat in the mountain wind. The boy called out the measure; the warden studied the smoke and dropped his copper mallet upon the lanyard to shear the peg. The gate swung, and the slag poured out.

Where bronze had run past the trench, a vat had burst, dumping melted metal into the ash pit. Eber expected to see the gray sludge shoveled into the ravine to hide the loss. Instead, an older woman stepped into the trough with an iron ladle. She skimmed the crusted dross, ground it into powder, and stirred it into the wet clay being pounded for kiln bricks. She worked the ruined melt into the paste until the earth turned slate-blue. The failure was not washed away; it was baked into the firewalls that would line the next furnace.

Near the sorting bench, a journeyman set down an ingot shining like mirror-glass, declaring to the room that his pour had reached absolute fineness. The woman with the ladle did not answer his smile. She laid the ingot on iron scales, checked the level, and dropped a hardened punch onto the metal's flank. The skin parted to reveal a pocket of trapped sulfur beneath the polish. She did not debate the man or permit him to explain. She swept his ingot into the discard heap, struck his name from the tally board, and motioned him past the gate before he could speak another word.

A cry broke from the deep sump. Two runners came hurtling down the ramp, shouting that the lower dike had split. They tore through the outer room toward the inner chamber where the elders met in quiet consultation. Yet as they crossed the sill, the foremost runner caught the heavy curtain and drew the oak door shut behind them with a muffled thud. Though the gallery shook with the water’s rumble, not a single syllable of what the elders were weighing in that alcove escaped into the corridor. The urgency of the flood did not pry open the seam between the rooms.

Behind the cooling racks, an old master collapsed over his blowpipe, his chest going still. No one wailed or threw down their tongs. A girl beside him uncoiled dyed wool, tied a firm knot around the dead man’s wrist, and anchored the end to the roof timber. Before the dead hand could stiffen or the pipe lose its glow, she slipped her own fingers into the warmth where his had just rested, lifted the iron to her mouth, and drew the molten bubble into symmetry without letting the glass chill.

Before Eber turned to climb back, a stranger was brought down the stairs, blindfolded and bound. The escort removed the cloth and left her alone at the basalt pillar. She had never set foot in the valley and knew none of the names carved above the lintels. For three hours she walked the galleries in silence, tracing the flutes on the stone, examining the blue mortar between the firebricks, and weighing the knots against the roof beams. When she came out into the courtyard, she stood before the company and recounted every law that governed the fires, deriving the whole design of the works without having heard a single voice teach it.

Eber climbed the stairs into the cold light, leaving the weight where it belonged.
