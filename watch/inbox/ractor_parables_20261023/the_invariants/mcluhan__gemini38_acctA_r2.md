# The Battery at the Cape

The beacon tower stood on the headland where the tide rasped against basalt like a file on iron. Inside the drum-gallery, the medium was not the fuel or the glass, but the mechanical pace that reshaped every man who entered it. The apparatus worked the muscle, and the muscle surrendered its habits to the turning gears.

Along the curved wall ran the cold-stamp drum. A ribbon of heavy lead foil unspooled from a boxwood crate, passing beneath a bronze stylus that punched impressions at each swing of the pendulum. A young striker, noticing a jagged gouge where the hammer had stuttered during a squall, drew a scraper to shave the lead smooth. The master struck his arm down before the edge bit. The pawl beneath the drum was cut at an angle that allowed no retreat; the drum turned only toward the sea, teeth biting tooth, and the furrow had remained three barleycorns wide since the tower was mortared. The gouge stayed in the soft metal, and the lead moved only onward under the stylus.

Below the gallery, the flood-sluice guarded the lower boilers against the winter surge. When the tide rose above the mark, the pressure whistle screamed with high fury, deafening the room and rattling the marrow of the tenders. The apparatus could drop its drafts, vent its excess steam, and sound the warning gongs without a human hand. Yet the iron wheel that drowned the intake and severed the station from the mainland had no mechanical release. Its chain was padlocked through an iron staple, and the single brass key rested on the belt of the shepherd who lived half a league down the salt marsh. Even when brine washed across the stone flags and the steam pipes shook violently, the crew kept their hands behind their backs, watching the black water mount the sill until the shepherd's boots sounded on the gravel and his fingers turned the lock.

When the midwinter gale parted the deep sounding cable, a mile of copper wire snapped at the headland and plunged into the abyss. The runner wept at the severed strand, but the elder did not cast the fractured brass coupler into the scrap heap. He carried the torn terminal to the balance bench, placed it in the counterpoise of the surviving relay, and filed the bent metal until its dead weight compensated for the lost tension. What had broken was not discarded; its remaining heft held the live wires steady against the storm.

At sunset, a lookout came bounding down the iron stairs, shouting that five merchantmen had cleared the southern reef, his eyes wide with the flash of white canvas. The master did not lift his glass. He laid his palm upon the slate and tapped the copper hydrometer in the saltwater trough. "Show me the salt-crust on the float," the master said. The lookout had brought only his sighting; he had gathered no brine, weighed no sand, and recorded no drop in the glass. The master lifted the brass horn from the lookout's neck, hung it on the empty peg, and pointed him down the mountain road. The entry slate beside the window remained untouched.

In the lower relay vault, the copper clickers operated in acoustic frenzy. The chatter of maritime signals bounced from the granite until the air tasted of copper and sparks. Yet the doorway was guarded by a double curtain of boiled horsehair and heavy wool. Messengers lunged through the hanging cloth at full gallop, boots sliding on the wet stone, but the boundary held. Outside the felt, in the narrow corridor where the watchmen sat, the world was quiet enough to hear the wick draw oil. Not a syllable of the interior vibration escaped into the passage.

Toward midnight, the furnace keeper collapsed against the hopper. His heart stopped, and the skin of his face went the gray of wet slate. The watchers did not wail or let the fire bank. They unknotted his hemp girdle, looped it through the overhead toggle to pin the draft-gate open at his dying measurement, and eased his shoulders to the flags. Before the shovel handle had lost the warmth of his fingers, his daughter stepped into his wooden shoes, took the scoop, and cast fresh coal onto the embers. The draft breathed without a flutter.

In the morning, an official arrived from the capital with a leather portfolio, asking for the written manual of the cape. The master took a sponge soaked in turpentine and wiped the white plaster wall until every painted maxim and numbered decree dissolved into gray streaks. He then walked out onto the terrace, leaving the official alone with the three apprentices. Guided only by the pitch of the drums, the balance of the brass weights, and the cold resistance of the lead ribbon, the youths tended the fire, marked the foil, and kept the beacon turning without a single misstep.
