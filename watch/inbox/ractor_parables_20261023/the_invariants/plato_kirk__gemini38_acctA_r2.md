# The Cavern Rig

In the sounding vault beneath the headland, the watch recorded the tides on a copper band stamped by an iron punch. When a junior steersman struck the wrong fathom mark during a gale, he reached for a file to grind the copper flat and turn the ratchet back. The watch officer caught his wrist against the casing. The teeth on the drum were cut in one slope only; the band moved forward, or it did not move at all. What was bitten into the metal stayed in the metal, and the steersman had to stamp the true sounding further down the strip, leaving the gouged error plain to every eye that unrolled the spool. For forty seasons the width and gauge of that strip had never changed.

At midday, a lookout hurried down into the chart chamber, calling out that the outer shoals were clear and the passage open for three leagues. The chart-master did not take up his ink. He pointed to the runner's empty hands and demanded the lead-weight with the sand of the reef-bed stuck to its tallow, and the notched candle that timed the run. The lookout insisted he had seen the water with his own eyes from the gallery, blue and unbroken. "A sight without a weight is only a shadow on the rock," the chart-master said. He refused to draw the course upon the linen. Instead, he took the runner's bronze badge, snapped the pin, and sent him out the sea-gate to walk the shale, his name struck from the day's relief.

Before the third watch, a heavy swell cracked the lower sea-valve, and brine surged into the powder vault. The crew rushed to the emergency lever, whose shear-bolt was painted red to drop the iron portcullis and seal the inner bastion. The helmsman took up a maul to shatter the seal and drop the gate on his own command. The watch officer seized the maul by the haft. Even with water swirling over their boots and the deck groaning under the surge, the rig permitted no hand inside the hull to drop the portcullis on its own say-so. A runner was sent up the chimney to the high beacon where the keeper stood above the storm. The crew waited in the rising water until a brass cylinder clattered down the tube, carrying the keeper's counter-weight that unlocked the lever. Only then was the gate dropped into the sea.

In the plotting cell, heavy felt curtains kept out the roar of the surf and the shouting of the gunners on the upper tier. A messenger burst through the drape, shaking with panic, blurting out what the commander had muttered on the parapet about the failure of the south wall. The chart-master did not flinch. He struck the table with his open hand and pointed back to the threshold. "Whatever wind blows in the gallery dies at the curtain," he said. The messenger was made to stand outside until his breath slowed, and when he entered again, he was permitted to report only the compass bearing of the smoke. Not a syllable of the parapet's panic crossed the felt, nor did the plotting officers repeat it to the crews below.

When the heavy sounding-drum broke loose from its carriage during a sudden roll and plunged into the abyss of the intake shaft, the crew stood silent before the empty stanchion. The drum was gone, irreplaceable before winter. The officer did not curse the dark or write an excuse into the margin. He descended into the spray with a grapple, fished up the sheared axle-bolt, and carried the twisted iron to the forge. Before the metal cooled, the smith beat the ruined bolt into a counter-poise for the balance beam, stamped with the date and fathom of its snapping. The drum was gone, but the iron that replaced it now measured the strain of every rope in the battery, so that the loss itself became the standard by which all future loads were checked.

Near midnight, the pump-tender succumbed to sulfur damp in the deep sump, his hands slipping from the brake and his chest stilling. The men hauling beside him did not scatter or abandon the stroke. They caught his shoulders, took his hemp lanyard, and tied a double knot around the brake handle at the exact notch of his last down-stroke. They carried the cold body up the ladder to the hearth in the warming ward, wrapping him in heated wool and rubbing his limbs with oil until the breath rattled back into his throat. When the blood found his cheeks, they walked him back down into the dark, placing his fingers upon his own knot, which he unpicked with living hands before resuming the stroke.

At dawn, the old surveyor gathered the recruits in the middle of the courtyard. On the stone flags lay the bronze quadrants, the sight-tubes, and the vellum charts of the shoals. With a single kick of his boot, the surveyor sent the instruments clattering into the cistern and kicked dirt across the vellum. The men gasped, reaching toward the water. "If you cannot lay out every boundary, every danger, and every channel using nothing but the shadow of that gallows post and the interval of the breakers against the cliff," the surveyor said, leaning on his staff, "then what you were following was a drawing in a cave, and not the coast itself." The recruits turned their backs on the cistern, picked up pieces of slate, and began to reckon the shore from the ground beneath their feet.
