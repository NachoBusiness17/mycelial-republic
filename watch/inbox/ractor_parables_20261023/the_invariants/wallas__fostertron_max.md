# The Weir at the Narrows

Nora came up from the capital in the wet month, with a commission to write down how the village was governed. She carried a notebook and a private theory that places are held together by their arguments. The village stood around a weir that took the river's push and turned it, patiently, on its own wheel. The water did all the wanting; the wheel only rode it.

In the toll-house an old clerk kept the day-book under a flat stone. Its columns had been ruled the same way since Nora's grandmother was a girl, and each day's crossings were written under the last day's. A grain merchant came in one afternoon and asked, not quite casually, whether a certain line might be mended — it recalled a cargo he had sworn to and never delivered, and his sons were old enough to read. The clerk dipped his pen and entered the afternoon's rain below it instead. When a volume filled, they began the next by copying its final line at the head; and the black ribbon that rested at the newest page had never been seen to climb.

Across the yard stood the sluice-house, and this was the scene Nora's first letter described. Every few years the channel silts, and the deep sluice must be opened to scour it, which drowns the mill meadow downstream for good — a thing done once and never called back. In the flood autumn the young men, the strongest and loudest of them, gathered at the sluice-house by night with lamps, hungry to be the ones. But the iron key hung on the keeper's neck, and the keeper was three days off at the falls. They waited. They cursed, and squared up to the door, and one set his hand on the bar, and the others pulled it off. When she rode in she turned the key herself, the meadow went under, and afterward nobody quarreled with the water.

In Nora's second month the river tore the ferry from its mooring and broke it on the weir. The village mourned for a morning. Then the sound planks became the reading-desk of the schoolroom, the broken keel became the lintel of the smoke-house door, and the ferryman's daughter carried the first plank herself. Nora wrote in her notebook: nothing here is carried off; it is carried over.

Market was held on the stone flat by the bridge, and the custom of the stone was older than the bridge. A fish-seller cried the largest sturgeon since the war; it lay on the flat iron, the beam settled at forty pounds before any praise was spent, and only then was it entered. Another man's record eel, when called to the stone, slid away with him into the crowd, and the day's page closed without it. The butter-woman who had slipped an iron plate beneath her crocks to swell the scale was not fined; she was simply never sold at that stone again, and crossed the river each week to a colder market, and stayed there.

That winter two families quarreled over the drowned meadow, and the winter court sat in the smoke-room with the door shut. Half the village pressed at that door, wanting to hear, wanting to be first to know — Nora felt the press in her own ribs — and the walls did not widen by a finger. The insults stayed in the smoke, and so did a confession of an old theft. Afterward, at the well, the tanner's wife retold the confession to two listeners. The village did nothing to her; it simply stopped telling her things, and by spring she had moved across the bridge to be near the butter-woman.

The old ferryman died in the hard frost. By the toll-house door hung a cord thick with knots, one for each death since the founding, and a new one was tied that evening while the clerk read aloud the line of the man's first crossing as a boy. Then the grave was allowed one hour, and the fire got the rest of the night: the widow was walked back to the warmest kitchen, given the dead man's bread ration and the chair facing the hearth, and at dawn the boy who had trained under him rowed the crossing alone, the old man's oar hung above the flames.

At midwinter Nora asked the keeper where the village's laws were written down, and offered to have her commission print them as a code for the capital. The keeper said they were written nowhere, and called the children in from the sledge-hill, and asked them one by one why the ribbon never climbed, why the stone weighed before the market spoke, why the cord, why the shut door. The children answered, each in their own words, the newest arrival among them as fluent as the miller's girl. One custom failed the asking: the bonfire lit each autumn on the far hill, for which nobody could give a reason. It was not lit again, and nothing fell.

Nora went down the river in the spring with her commission unspent. The water went on wanting; the wheel went on riding it.
