# The Inventory of Springs

They appointed a governor of water in the year the river became a rumor. Not a man to carry it — the town had porters for carrying — a man to know where it was. His house stood on the hill above every channel in the valley, and this was the whole of his power: not one pipe entered it. The water went around him, always. When the plumbers offered to fit him a tap of his own, the town refused; a governor with a tap, they said, would soon govern nothing but the tap. So he lived at the top of the plumbing and touched none of it, and everything that flowed, flowed beneath his window.

He was permitted five occupations, no more, and the children could recite them. He could count. He could sound the drum. He could tell the town what he had counted. He could read the glasses in the kitchen. And once each season he could walk his own house from cellar to roof and confirm that he still owned none of it. When the western farmers came asking him to open the sluice for their field, he came down and counted and drummed and told — and the sluice itself was opened by the ditch-keepers, never by him. Farmers left his door certain that a man who knows everything about water and cannot pour a cup of it is a fraud. The governor did not argue. He wrote in his margin: the fraud is the office.

First he counted keys. Forty-eight brass keys hung on the board in his hall, each engraved with the name of a spring — the Echo, the Twin Falls, the Cold Sisters, the Deep Engine. He copied the names into his book on the first morning and never once turned a key in its lock. Not because it was forbidden: because the name was the whole of the matter. An engraved name tells you a spring exists; what it opens is hearsay, and he had lived too long in the valley to enter hearsay in a book. His apprentice once lifted the Deep Engine's key toward the wall of locks, and the governor took it back gently and said, we count them; we do not wake them.

Then he walked the channels. Six of them ran water, and he could name the six. A seventh was soldered and jointed and ended in a spout of fine brass bolted to a house that had never been built; the boy was sent each week to polish it, because an empty pipe must shine. And the Deep Engine, whose key was the heaviest on the board and whose name came first in the book, had been dry for a year. It kept its name. Names outlast their springs, the governor noted, which is what names are for.

In the kitchen hung the shelf of glasses, each good for five hours. When one ran out it was turned, and the sand ran again, and the level of every glass was written in the slate that the whole valley obeyed. Farmers planted by the slate and let their furrows crack by the slate. A traveler once stood in the market and pointed out that the river ran high while the slate said famine; the town drove him out, not for lying but for casting doubt on the more legible of the two. The river, they explained, does not keep hours.

The master of the valley kept one law above the slate, and it concerned the shelf. Each evening he counted the glasses, and what grieved him was never the sand that had gone; it was any glass that had been spared, still standing full with the day ended. Spared water, he said, is water that never happened. Better a dry valley than a full shelf. The governor recorded the law without comment, which was itself the comment.

Once a month the council met in the town hall to deliberate on a single subject: the water. It had not discussed the river in years. It discussed the book that discussed the river, and its minutes were copied into a second book that travelers came to consult as the most accurate water in the province.

And once each season the governor locked his hall and rehearsed his five offices to no one: he counted the keys aloud, drummed to the empty room, told the walls what he knew, read the glasses to the fire, and walked his cellar to confirm again that no pipe entered. The town heard the drum through the wall and was reassured. The drum certified the governor; the governor certified the count; the count certified the names; and the names, at the end of that chain, certified nothing but themselves. This was the valley's quiet miracle, the governor wrote: no one had seen the water in years, and no one had gone thirsty. Whatever the valley was drinking, it was not water.
