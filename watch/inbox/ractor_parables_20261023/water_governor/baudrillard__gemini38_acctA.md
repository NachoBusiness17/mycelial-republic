# The Inventory of Empty Flumes

The registrar presided in a square pavilion perched upon the dry crest above the valley, surrounded by shelves of marked parchment and balanced plumb-lines, yet through the flags of that floor ran neither conduit, sluice, nor siphon. The villagers who climbed the long switchbacks in seasons of drought invariably searched the room for some master valve, an iron wheel whose turning might loose a torrent into their parched furrows, but they found only whitewashed stone and drafts of wind. The pavilion had zero couplings to the masonry below. The registrar did not release water, could not open a ditch, and commanded no courier to run down the hill with orders. His labor was bounded by five strictly separated gestures: at daybreak he numbered the catalog of names; every hour he struck the bronze basin to mark the steady pulse; at noon he posted the public accounting for the valley to read; he tracked the five-hour passage of water through the shifting jars; and before dusk he weighed his own balance-arms and plumb-bobs to ensure the scales had not drifted. Beyond these five gestures, he possessed no power of dispatch. The estate was governed without an arm that touched the ditch.

Along the colonnade outside hung forty-eight bronze markers, each stamped with an ancestral name and sorted into distinct orders according to origin and rank. Every morning the registrar walked their length, touching each bronze face with a wooden wand and recording its presence in his book. Yet behind every marker was a leaden cylinder whose seal remained unbroken; the registrar never broke the resin, never pried open a cap, and never weighed whatever salt or oil might rest within. The names alone were classified and preserved; their interiors were an unread depth that the pavilion declined to investigate, for the system operated upon the catalog of designations, not upon substance.

Further down the rim, where the aqueduct departed toward the slopes, the true anatomy of the works revealed itself. Though many stone cradles were carved into the parapet to receive the conduits, only six carried lead pipes that actually ran unbroken to the downstream settlements. Flanking them were other cradles, carved with equal precision and polished against the wind, which held iron collars bolted to nothing, terminating in open air above the gravel. At the far corner of the terrace stood an ancient stone tower known to every herdsman; its throat was split from crown to base, choked with brambles and centuries of dust, a channel utterly dead. Yet in the register it was inscribed with identical care alongside the six working pipes, its title preserved in the rolls of account, circulating as a sign whose original spring had vanished from the earth.

In the center of the hall rested the drainage vessel, an apparatus designed to measure not the rain from heaven, but a rolling depletion of five hours tracked upon a scored slate of allowances. Whatever volume had been drawn four hours prior pressed upon the margin of the current draft, pinching the permitted flow of the afternoon. But the moment the fluid passed the fifth mark, that ancient withdrawal evaporated from the tally entirely, leaving the margin restored. The estate lived within a sliding corridor of time, indifferent to the stores of last winter or the needs of next month, occupied only with the five hours immediately trailing its shadow.

Over this table hung the law of the estate, chiselled into lintel stone by the founder: it was better that the cisterns be drained to absolute dust and the vines drowned in surplus than that a single drop of the allocated measure remain unspent upon the shelf when the window closed. To leave an allowance unconsumed was considered an unforgivable forfeit of stewardship. When the furrows were sodden, the husbandmen were required to pour the remainder across the hot scree, exhausting the allotment so that no margin could be found lingering in reserve.

At the fifth hour, three delegates from the Committee on Water ascended the hill in clean linen robes. They did not descend into the mud to touch the leaves or taste the wells. They entered the dry pavilion, reviewed the five-hour tally, verified that the forty-eight plaques hung in their prescribed orders, and certified the balance. In their eyes, the valley was neither wet nor dry, but fully accounted for. The referent of the river had dissolved into the rhythm of the slate, and the estate flourished in the clarity of an apparatus that had successfully replaced the territory it claimed to quench.
