# The Water Warden

The waterworks of the hill estate stood behind a round tower, and in the tower lived the Warden, whose whole appointment was to keep the count of the waters. The tower had been built without a door: walls, a roof, a smoke-vent, and no opening wide enough for a letter. Men who wanted something from him stood below and shouted, and the rain ran their words down the gutter. In thirty years nothing handed up had reached him, because nothing could be handed up at all.

At first light he walked the gallery where the estate's forty-eight channels were cut into the wall, each mouth under a plate bearing a name and a kind. He copied the words from the plates — spring, rain, sweet, mineral, brown — and sorted the mouths by them. He never lifted a cover, never dipped a finger, never put his ear to a spout. When the merchant who owned the ninth mouth swore that his water ran sweet, the Warden wrote "sweet, so says the plate," because the plate was all he read.

Mid-morning he struck the bronze bowl that hung by the stair, once every hundred breaths, and the estate set itself by the sound — loaves drawn on the stroke, pages turned, guards changed at the tower's foot. At dusk he stood on the walk and gave the telling: how many mouths, how many flowed, what the bowls showed. On the sixth day he tested nothing but his own instruments — filled his cup to the line, read it back, climbed his rope — and marked that his eyes and hands still agreed.

He owned no crank. When the granary fire came in the dry summer, the crowd gathered below and begged him to open the great channel and send the north water over the roofs. He came out, showed his two empty hands, and told them what the bowls said. The valves of the estate were turned by other men in other yards, or by no one. Water went where it went without ever passing through him.

Six of the forty-eight flowed; that was in every telling. Nine more were fastened into the wall with good iron, paid for by the foot in a prosperous year, and joined to nothing — walk behind the plaster, as the Warden once had, and the bright pipe ends in cold stone. The first mouth, the oldest, whose plate the founder's family came each spring to polish, ran dry nine years ago when its spring failed. The plate still promised abundance, and the mapmaker's chart drew the north water in confident blue all the way to the fountain in the square. The family's name stayed on the gate, the chart sold for a silver, and the fountain had long since been tiled over and planted with thyme.

On the shelf above his hearth stood a row of thin bronze bowls, pierced at the bottom, fed drop by drop from the cistern above. Each emptied itself in five hours and began again. The height of water in a bowl was the estate's permission: while the level stood, water could be drawn; as it fell, the allowance fell with it; when it emptied, a new measure started and the old one was gone as though never poured. Ask him what had been drawn at breakfast and, five hours later, he could only point at the bowls and say they held the whole of what anyone was permitted to know.

The Master of the estate kept one saying about water and gave it at every feast: better the cisterns run empty than a drop fall asleep in a tank. Each evening the gardeners poured the day's remainder over the lawns, so that no water should lie saved while the estate slept. A dry cistern at midnight was a day honestly spent; a full one was counted as theft from a future no one was permitted to save for.

The council kept a bench of three elders for the water alone. They sat below the tower at dusk, heard the telling, and copied the numbers into the town's book; they held no cranks either. In his last year the Warden's apprentice asked for the bench's evening and gave the long telling instead of the short one: the dry first mouth, the nine ending in stone, the bowls that forgot anything older than five hours, the two empty hands. He spoke for the length of a meal. The elders thanked him, and at the next dusk asked for the short telling, the one the town could carry home. The long one was true, and was never asked for again. The page with the short numbers is worn soft from touching; the page beside it, where the apprentice's count was copied, has kept its edges.
