# The Keeper of the Cisterns

You sit on the terrace in the noon glare, master, shading your eyes and waiting for someone to grant you peace. You built a stone shed above the vineyard, called it the keeper of the cisterns, and told yourself you had mastered the hill. But come look inside with me. Bring no titles, leave your copper coins for the dogs, and look at what is actually standing in the sun.

You gave your keeper four stone walls without a single pipe through the masonry, not one conduit to the outer village, and zero openings to receive the gossip of the road. You tell the neighbors he governs the valley, yet you gave him neither horn to sound an alarm nor a horse to ride into the fields. He has no authority to send forth a single laborer to dig a ditch. When dawn breaks, he performs only five unhurried acts, and none of them commands another living man. First, he bends his knees and strikes his own chest to see if his old bones still hold together. Next, he paces the lime wall to classify the wooden tags hanging from the rafters. Third, he strikes the iron chime once to mark the passing pulse of the sun. Fourth, he calls out the tally through the wooden shutter into the yard. Finally, he inspects the five-hour sandglass that drains from its upper chamber to its lower bowl. That is his whole round: testing himself, sorting the pegs, marking the pulse, proclaiming the state, and watching the sand slide. Not an order given, not a single spade commanded.

You love the look of abundance, master, so you hung forty-eight cedar tags in neat rows across the pine rail. Each tag has a title carved on its face—iron, flax, olive, grain—and the keeper sorts them carefully into six neat wicker baskets. But look closer with the lantern: he never turns a single tag over to see the secret marks scored into the back. Why should he? A title tells him what vessel belongs where; the hidden chalk is just another man's vanity, and the keeper cares only for what is natural and present.

You pretend your entire aqueduct is singing, but count the brackets with your own hands. Along the limestone terrace, there are iron saddles bolted to the rock, yet only six of them carry hollowed cedar conduits flowing with spring water. Beside them, empty iron mounts jut into the open air with nothing resting in their cradles—gaps where timber was measured, promised, and never laid. And look at the deep stone tank in the north wall, the one that once bore the sign of the swift runner: it is bone-dry, cracked to the lime, cold and dead. The lizards sleep in its throat, yet your clerks still speak of it as if tomorrow it might quench a city's thirst. Stop asking the dead tank for water. It has none to give, and mourning a dried cistern will not moisten a single fig.

Look at the glass vessel on his bench. The sand does not measure eternity; it measures five hours only, rolling forward continuously. Every grain that drops marks the steady drain of the five-hour allowance drawn against the estate's permitted share. You worry constantly about running short before the season ends, clutching your permits like a beggar clutching crusts. But you made the law yourself, even if you tremble to keep it: you decreed that it is better to run the trenches dry and empty every cistern to the dust than to leave a single jar of water sitting on the terrace when dusk falls. Water kept in the shade does not keep; it stales, grows moss, and evaporates into the night air. Why hoard what you were given to spend? Pour it out upon the roots. Run dry, and let tomorrow find you with empty pots and green vines.

When the sun touches the western ridge, your keeper does not run to the governor's palace, nor does he bow before the tax collector. He walks straight down to the council on water—those elders who sit on the river terrace watching the main canal—and lays his tally on their cedar bench. They ask him only what ran, what was spent, and what remains.

You are still asking the sky to make you feel secure, master, as if a fuller cistern would cure a timid heart. The water does not care about your anxiety, and the sun will take what you do not use. Strip away the pretense of your forty-eight secret ciphers and your empty iron brackets. Six conduits run, one well is dead, the sand runs for five hours, and your keeper commands nobody. Use the water while the sun is high, and let the stones go dry.
