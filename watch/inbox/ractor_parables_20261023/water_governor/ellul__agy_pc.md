# The Five-Hour Glass

Each dawn Cassian climbed the outside stair into the round watchtower above the terraced groves and barred the oak door behind him. The masons who raised the tower had mortared every arrow-slit shut on the side facing the dam and left no pipe, chain, pulley, or speaking-horn passing through the stone. Though the valley called him warden of the weir, his room possessed no coupling to the waterworks below; he could neither lift a sluice-hatch nor turn a spigot by a fingernail's breadth. Across his cedar table lay a brass rod notched with the only five duties permitted to his office: he was to walk the cellar and sort the titles painted on the clay jars, strike the bronze gong at the turn of the hour, read his parchment roll aloud from the parapet, invert the five-hour sandglasses along the sill, and drop a lead plumb-line from the rafters to prove his own measuring-table stood level. Nowhere on the brass rod was there a sixth notch empowering him to release a single drop into the furrows. That severance between the tower and the hatches was the one restraint that had earned its keep, for an apparatus cut off from the weir cannot open it in secret.

At first light he carried his charcoal stick down the cellar steps to perform the first notch of the rod. Forty-eight tall amphorae stood in four ranks along the flagstones, each vessel bearing a painted title across its neck. Cassian walked the rows and copied all forty-eight titles onto his roll, grouping them by their inscriptions into neat families: jars named for the upper olive press, jars named for the southern vineyard, jars named for the orchard troughs, and jars named for the bakehouse. When a young ditch-digger asked whether the clay held springwater, sour wine, or only air, Cassian did not pry up a single wax stopper. His office was to certify that forty-eight titles stood upon forty-eight necks; peering inside the vessel was forbidden. So long as the wax stayed whole, the store could not be pilfered by the man who counted it, yet the roll grew heavy with the certainty of a supply nobody had verified.

Returning to the parapet, Cassian surveyed the limestone terraces where the conduits crossed the slope. Six stone channels were mortared fast to the upper basin and carried a steady stream down to the root-beds. Beside them, four timber flumes stood bolted upon tall masonry piers, leveled at great cost, yet ending in mid-air three paces short of the springhead and three paces short of the ditches. Having been raised, they required Cassian to record their gap every morning, so that the ritual of counting them justified their standing useless in the sun. Farther west, the steep basalt chute that had once hurled water down the crag faster than any course on the estate lay cracked from rim to bed. Dry bindweed choked its throat; each day Cassian wrote *dead* beside its name, and each day the roll required him to inspect the same dry stone as though measurement might revive it.

Along the eastern sill stood a frame of five-hour sandglasses, turned in a continuous sliding reckoning so that the falling sand charted how much of each five-hour water allotment remained in the upper pond. Below the frame, bolted to the lintel by the lord of the estate, hung an iron tablet hammered with a single maxim: *Better the pond be drained to dry mud before sundown than a single dipper remain standing in the dark.* Once the five-hour glasses began their rolling measure above that tablet, the sand ceased to describe the water and began to command it. Whenever the red grains ran ahead of the mark on the basin wall, the stewards panicked lest the fifth hour close with water unspent; they opened the six mortared channels during rainstorms and poured the reserve into gullies already sodden, drowning the lower fig trees so the five-hour reckoning would come out even. The instrument built to prevent waste had made exhaustion mandatory, and no one on the hillside asked who had decided that drowned roots were preferable to a full pond.

At dusk seven elders in bleached mantles—the Assembly of the Wellhead—took their benches in the courtyard to hear the third duty of the rod. Cassian leaned over the coping and read his roll: the forty-eight sorted titles over sealed jars, the six flowing channels, the four raised flumes joined to nothing, the dead basalt chute, and the five-hour glasses drained in obedience to the iron tablet. The elders asked only whether the plumb-line had hung straight at noon. Hearing that the tower's own beam was level, they declared the water well ordered and departed, while Cassian stepped back within his severing walls, turned the next glass, and struck the bronze gong for an hour that needed no water and would allow none to be spared.
