# The Registrar of Names

When the master of the valley could no longer walk the waterways himself, he did not send for a digger. He sent for a registrar, and the commission he gave had five lines in it: the man would walk, he would knock, he would read, he would speak, and he would rehearse. Nothing in the five lines let him draw, and nothing let him pour. He was given no bucket, no dipper, no key to any sluice, and in all his years on the estate his hands never once touched the water.

Each morning he made his round. There were forty-eight mouths of water on the estate — springs, wells, cisterns, tanks — and at each he stopped, read the name cut into the coping, and wrote the name in his book. That was all he read of them: the names. Whether a spring ran sweet or salt he did not know and was not asked; he had no instrument for tasting and no line in his commission that wanted one. He sorted the names by what the carving promised. Six of the forty-eight truly sent water down the channels when the miller called for them. A dozen more had pipes laid in and lids fitted and paint fresh on the stone, and no water came; he wrote them each morning among the others, in the same even hand. One, the grandest, under a carved arch with iron fittings, lay beneath a black stone; it had gone dead before his commission began. He counted it every morning, and the pipe-fitters still oiled its joints, and no one told them to stop.

As he walked he knocked each coping twice, in the same rhythm. Men had once tapped stone that way to hear whether a shaft was hollow underneath. The estate no longer listened for hollowness. The miller opened his sluice at the sound, whether the wheel wanted water or not; the cook lifted the kettle at the fourth knock and set it back at the fifth. On the day the registrar was kept home by fever, the mill stood until noon with its sluice shut, waiting for a sound that had nothing to do with the river.

Beside the great cistern hung a dark slab on the wall. Every drawing of water was chalked on it, and every mark was washed away five hours after it was made; the registrar kept a sponge for this and wiped nothing before its hour. What the estate knew of its thirst was what stood on that slab. When the council argued about water, they argued over the slab; when the master asked whether the day had been dry, he meant whether the slab was clean. Old men who could remember whole seasons were no longer asked. A season did not fit on the slab.

The council kept benches for grain, for roads, for flocks, and one bench apart where only water was spoken of. To that bench, at dusk, the registrar spoke his book aloud: forty-eight names, six carrying, the rest fitted or dry or dead. The bench never once asked him to move water. In time it stopped occurring to the bench that moving water was a thing a man could be asked for. When the east channel burst, they sent menders within the morning, because the registrar's six had become five on the page and the page had shown it by first light. The bench raised his fee that year, and it was deserved.

The master had a saying that his father had used before him: better an empty cistern at dusk than a full one at dawn. Let it be spent, not stored. He said it at the bench and at the mill and at his own table. In the driest month the yard fountains ran all afternoon, because the water had come that day and to leave it standing overnight was, in the master's word, waste. Whether the fields had wanted it that afternoon was not what the saying asked.

On the emptiest day of the drought the registrar rehearsed. He walked the full round over dry ground, knocked at each coping in order, read each name aloud, wiped the blank slab at the blank hours, and spoke his count to the empty bench. The round held perfectly. Nothing in it had ever needed water.

A child asked him once why he wrote the names and never tasted the water. He turned to the first page of his book to find an answer and found instead a hand that no one living knew, older than his commission, already counting forty-eight in faded ink. He showed the child the page. The child asked who had decided the count. The registrar knocked twice on the coping, said the hour was late, and went on to the next name.
