# The Proportion of the Cisterns

Master Laurent kept his vigil in the octagonal stone chamber above the estate's reservoirs, a room built without casements, postern gates, or speaking-tubes to the outer roads. No courier rode from his threshold, and no bell-rope or conduit passed through his masonry to the distant fields; he was a geometer of interior measures, stationed where the waters were reckoned but never drawn. Around him lay the instruments of a counselor who studies a game in motion without ever casting the dice. Though the bailiffs in the courtyard below often waited for him to order ditchers to the sluices, Laurent possessed neither horn nor baton. By the charter of his office, his hand might never lift a floodgate or send a bearer toward the troughs; to command the stream was a power withheld from the man appointed only to declare its true proportion.

Five operations alone were permitted to him, executed in strict succession, as one reduces a stubborn equation by measured steps. First, he walked the timber racks to number and group the vessels stored for the season. Second, he laid his palm against the main bronze trunk to count the rhythmic pulse of the current beneath the flagstones. Third, he inscribed a plain summary of the day's standing upon a parchment scroll. Fourth, he observed the glass clepsydras that divided the day into shifting spans of usage. Fifth, he tested his own brass scales with known leaden grains, descending from the pound to the scruple until he was certain no warp in his beam could falsify the theorem of the domain. Beyond those five acts, his margin was too narrow to contain a sixth.

Along the western wall stood forty-eight stoppered phials of dark green glass in four tiers of twelve. Each phial bore a strip of vellum glued across its neck, inscribed with the title of the spring or cistern from which its warrant derived. When Laurent performed his numbering, he read every one of the forty-eight titles aloud and sorted them into their proper genera upon his tally-sheet—some belonging to the upper snow-melt, some to the deep chalk wells, some to the mill-ponds. Yet he never once twisted a cork, broke a thread of wax, or held a phial to the candle to see whether the liquor inside was clear, brackish, or absent. By the statute of his chamber, the reckoner must sort every vessel by its superscription alone, remaining forever blind to the draught stoppered within.

From the lower reservoir, six leaden aqueducts ran fully soldered to the main header, their joints tight and their channels humming with live water for the vineyards. Beside them, however, the masons had bolted four heavy bronze spigots directly into the dressed stone of the wall, polished and gleaming, yet left them with their tail-pieces hanging in empty air, uncoupled to any pipe behind the masonry. A stranger entering the vault would have sworn ten conduits served the estate, when only six carried a drop. Worse still was the seventh channel on the southern side, the narrow race once praised across the province for the violent speed of its torrent; its stonework had split at the foundation, its bed lay choked with dry chalk, and Laurent marked it on his scroll as extinct beyond recovery.

To judge the spending of the six living streams, Laurent consulted a single wax tablet on his trestle, where the outlay of water was scored across a sliding span of five hours. As each quarter-hour chimed, the oldest mark at the tail of the five-hour span was smoothed away and the newest draw was cut at the head, so that the reckoning glided perpetually forward. In calculating the expectation of what remained before the five-hour glass turned, Laurent followed the blunt maxim carved by the lord of the estate above the lintel: it was far better for the vineyards to drink the reservoir down to the wet gravel before the fifth hour expired than to leave a single gallon standing idle in the vats when the new allowance poured in. Stagnant abundance was a failure of geometry; an empty cistern at the turn of the glass proved that every drop had done its work.

At dusk, when the five-hour tally showed the troughs nearly spent and the six live aqueducts running at a thin trickle, Laurent wound his parchment and laid it in the wicker tray beside the stair. He did not address it to the general assembly of the barons, who argued over grain tariffs and boundary ditches, but directed it solely to the Chamber of the Springs, that select bench of the estate's senate charged with governing the watercourses alone. When the elders of that chamber climbed the stair to read his figures, they found Laurent already wiping his stylus, content to stand at the edge of the proof—having demonstrated to the last ounce where the stream had gone, while leaving to others the hand that turned the wheel.
