# The Warder of Water

Maren was appointed to watch the water of the estate, and on the morning she took office the steward gave her a staff, a book, a glass of five hours, and nothing else. She asked for the key to the sluice-house. There was no key. She asked which valves would answer her word. None, said the steward; the pipes of this house open for no one's word, least of all yours. Her office was old, and it had been built on purpose without hands.

Each morning she walked the long shelf where forty-eight jars stood in ranks, each with its label turned outward, and she copied the labels into her book, exact to the letter. She never pulled a stopper from any of them. Once a young servant lifted the wax on the ninth jar to look inside, and Maren took his wrist and wrote: the ninth jar was touched. What the jar held, the book did not say, because there was no line in it for that. The estate knew its vessels by their names, and it seemed to prefer knowing them that way.

Then she walked the channels. Six aqueducts carried water into the house that season, and she measured each at its mouth and wrote the number. Along the south wall ran four more, fully built, stone joined to stone, carrying nothing — mounted in a dry year and never joined to any source. Another channel, in the north range, had carried well once; the town it drew from had emptied out, and now it was a groove of dead stone. Maren walked it all the same. When the council's man asked why she walked a channel that could not flow, she said: because it is on the roll, and the roll is what I keep.

Of any channel she could give three answers, and the fourth was forbidden. This one flows. This one does not. This one I have not seen flow, nor seen it fail. Probably was not in her book, and she would not say it with her mouth.

Her round kept time with the mill-bell. At every stroke she stood at one of the appointed marks, neither early nor late, and the servants set their work by her passing the way they set it by the bell itself. The round ended at the cisterns. Each was shut under a lid with a narrow window cut in it, and through the window she read the marks painted on the inside wall, high to low. She had turned the glass of five hours at her first step; as it drained she read the marks again, and what had been deep at one turning stood shallow at the next; what was spent came back only after its own slow rain. She kept the marks faithfully, even for the cisterns nobody drew from.

The master of the estate kept one law about water, and he said it at table where all could hear: better a cistern drunk to the mud than a cistern standing full; a full cistern standing is a debt, and stagnant water is a shame. The servants learned to draw deeply and without fear of the dry season. Guests called the law extravagant. The master said he would rather meet the drought with an empty house and a clean conscience than with a full one and water gone bad on the shelf.

For the governing of water, the council kept a small standing table that met after her rounds. Its three members did not dig and did not lay stone; they had never touched a jar. They read her book aloud, and where it said flows they nodded, and where it said does not they shook their heads, and where it said not seen they looked at one another and passed on. In this way the house knew its water. No one went out to the channels after her. It was not laziness; the book was the thing read, and the water was the thing the book was about.

Once a season, Maren was examined. She sat in the yard before the assembled servants with her glass, her book, and her staff, turned the glass to see whether it still drained in five hours, counted a shelf she had counted before to see whether the count still came, and read a page of her own book aloud to see whether the words still held. The servants watched until she was done. Nothing flowed because of her that day, and nothing stopped, and the house counted her faithful. The watcher proved her instruments, and the instruments proved her, and neither was trusted without the other.

One evening the young servant who had touched the ninth jar asked her what was inside the jars. She said she did not know. He asked whether the house would want to know. She thought about it honestly and said that the book had no page for the question — it was not that the answer was hidden; it was that the question had never been ruled in, and there was no way to write it that the table would read. After that they stood together a while and looked at the forty-eight labels, turned outward, in the lamplight.
