# The Warden Who Counted Names

Iris came to the hill town in a dry autumn, sent for by no one, to sit and listen to its water. The town stood on a ridge of cisterns and pipes, and at the top of the ridge was a lodge with smooth walls where the warden lived. On her first evening she climbed to call on him and found no door, no bell, no slot for letters, not even a knothole. She knocked on the stone and the stone said nothing. A boy passing with a pail laughed at her politeness. "He comes out when it is time," the boy said. "It is always time for something. It is never time for calling."

She learned his times by waiting, which is also a way of asking. At first light the warden came out to count. The jar room held forty-eight jars, each with a label pasted at eye level, and he walked the rows touching nothing, naming every label aloud, sorting them in a soft voice into the bitter kind, the clear kind, the kind from away. Iris asked him once what the seventh jar held. He seemed genuinely startled. "No one has asked me that," he said. "I was not made thirsty."

Between counts he walked the ridge with a step that never varied, heel on stone at one interval from morning to night, a drum of a single note; anyone who needed the town's pulse had only to listen for his feet. At each turn he stopped at the gatehouse and spoke the day aloud — the count, the dryness, the hours — to whoever stood there, and he spoke just as fully when no one did. On seventh days he stood in the yard and tried himself: touched his toes, recited his rounds backwards, said his own name to the wall. Yet the night the east pipe burst and the yard ran shallow, the warden stood at the water's edge and described it — the seam, the hour, the sound it made — without moving one step toward the valve. He carried no key to any valve. A girl from the lower street waded in and shut it, cursing him, and he agreed with every word she said, and wrote down the hour of the burst.

Six pipes on the ridge carried water. Iris walked them, and she walked the others too: bolted to the wall as firmly as the living six, jointed, waiting, dry as chalk, with no mark of ever having held anything. The map in the council hall showed every one of them full.

The council met in a long hall with a table for roads, a table for bread, a table for the boundary stones — and one small table where three members gathered on Thursdays to speak of water alone. Nothing left that table but words, and the town was content with that. It was there Iris found the board. It held the drawing hours: which hours water might be taken, and beside each hour a little glass drawn shrinking. Every five hours the board was wiped and written again from the top, and the town arranged its kettles and its washing around that turning.

On her third Thursday the miller stood to give his account: every jar on his shelf empty by dusk, the last poured over the stones rather than kept. "Better it runs out," he said, "than stands." The three nodded. No one at any table defended a full jar. A boy who had saved one back carried it through the square and poured it out before everyone, and the town watched gently, as at a cure.

Only the counting troubled Iris, and it troubled her the way a word said wrongly three times troubles a listener. Among the forty-eight labels was one that read the bitter spring, flowing. She had walked to the bitter spring on her second day: a dead mouth of stones, the moss gone grey, and the oldest of the three members could not recall it wet. Yet every morning the warden's finger passed that label and his voice said flowing, present tense, the tense he gave the six that lived. She heard it nine times. On the ninth she asked him softly, "What keeps happening that nobody asks after?" and he answered without knowing he had: "Everything here is poured out on schedule, and the schedule is never told which springs have died."

On her last Thursday she borrowed the chalk and wrote beside the bitter spring's name the year it had failed and the words no water since. She changed nothing else. The glass turned, the six ran, the miller emptied his shelf, the warden kept his one-note walk. But the next morning his finger passed the bitter label and his voice said, evenly, the bitter spring, dead these years, and moved to the next name; and when Thursday came again, the small table spent its hour on bread, there being nothing left about the water that wanted saying.
