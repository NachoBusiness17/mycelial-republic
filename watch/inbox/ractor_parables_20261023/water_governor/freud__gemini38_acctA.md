# The Cistern Watch

The physician sat in the cool gallery above the sluices, where the stone smelled of wet iron and long-damp lime. Below him stood the great bronze regulator, an engine of quiet intricacy whose stillness was easily mistaken for composure. It was his practice to observe what a house repeated when it believed itself merely at work. For three seasons the estate had drained its channels until the silt cracked, yet the stewards continued to speak of scarcity. What cannot be spoken by the master will inevitably be acted out by the ditch.

The engine itself possessed neither pump nor flume. It reached into no conduit; no valve opened at its touch, and no spillway answered its command. A stranger might have wondered why such a mass of clockwork had been raised over the waters if it could not turn them, but the physician recognized the arrangement: it was an apparatus of observation without hands to steer the flow. At regular intervals the regulator performed only what it was permitted. It chimed the passing pulse of the works; it struck a slate to chronicle what had passed; it weighed the sand in the five-hour glass; it tapped its own brass linkages to confirm that no pin had slipped; and it surveyed the long row of metal tallies hung along the northern gallery.

There were forty-eight tags in that row, cut from thin brass and inscribed with names of old storehouses and distant springs. The watchman walked their length twice each day, reading aloud the title on each tag and noting its category upon his parchment. Yet the physician noticed that the man never reached behind the wall. The cupboards remained locked; the jars whose titles he recited were never uncorked, nor was their liquor tasted. It was enough that the name was acknowledged. The house preserved its comfort by cataloging what it possessed, so long as no one asked what lay inside the vessels. The label was preserved precisely so the substance could remain untouched.

Behind the regulator, where the conduits descended toward the valley floor, the division between speech and substance grew sharper. Thirty iron brackets were bolted to the timber frame, suggesting an immense convergence of currents. Yet beneath the brackets, only six lead pipes were joined to the headers and carried the rush of living stream. Several iron sleeves were fastened securely to the wall but hung over empty air, uncoupled and dry. Further down the bench sat a wide stone trough once fed by a northern torrent that the workmen called the roaring fountain. The stone was white with dry mineral dust; its mouth was choked and its line severed. No moisture had dampened its lip for months, yet the stewards spoke of it in the present tense, as men often speak of an estranged brother whose place at the table is still laid each night.

Toward midday the steward entered the gallery to inspect the five-hour sandglass. The estate lived under a relentless rhythm: every five hours the sand was measured, not to preserve what remained, but to ensure that the allotment was utterly exhausted before the mark fell. The founder had written the unyielding law upon the lintel: it was a disgrace to leave moisture upon the ledge when the hour turned; better that the orchards drown than a single cup remain unspent in the vault. The physician watched the young apprentices rushing from ditch to ditch, flooding the furrows with frantic vigor simply to outrun the descent of the sand. They believed they were obeying an economic mandate. The physician recognized the compulsion: the dread that an unconsumed gift might prove the recipient unworthy of more.

In the afternoon, the Committee on Water assembled beneath the open awning. They were seven elders with solemn faces who spread charts upon the cedar table and argued over the volume of the estate. They debated the allocations with intense formality, adjusting small lead markers across a painted map of the orchard. The regulator ticked softly behind them, recording their presence without submitting to their authority. When the chairman rose to announce that the estate had mastered its thirst, a sudden surge rattled the six joined pipes, sending a spray of red foam across the flagstones. The elders did not look down. They wiped their boots upon the hem of their robes, nodded in unison, and signed the parchment to declare the flow temperate and secure.
