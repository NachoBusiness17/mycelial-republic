# The Chalk on the Cistern Post

A notice pinned to a cedar post does not belong to the hand that drove the nail. In the courtyard of the upper farm, the water-bailiff worked inside a small timber booth raised on four blocks at the center of the gravel. No lead pipe entered that booth, no clay drain ran beneath its floor, and no speaking-tube or bell-wire linked its shutters to the road outside. He could neither draw a bucket himself nor hail a driver beyond the fence. Whatever left his hand left only as chalk marks on a broad sheet of calfskin hung from the cedar post at noon, where different eyes sitting in different chairs around the yard waited to make their own truths out of the same white dust.

All morning the bailiff performed only the five motions permitted to his office. First, he walked the long trestles to tally the vessels by title. Second, he struck the bronze gong once at the turn of each hour to mark the pulse of the yard. Third, he hung his chalked calfskin upon the cedar post. Fourth, he shifted the wooden frame that held five sandglasses along the casement sill. Fifth, before sitting down on his bench, he tested his own brass plumb-weight and measuring reed in a pail of rainwater to prove his instruments had not warped. Beyond those five motions he had no gesture at all: when the mule-carts waited by the arch with full barrels, he had no flag to wave them forward and no command to send them down the hill.

Along the north trestle stood forty-eight tall stoneware crocks, each sealed with red wax and tied at the neck with a painted wooden collar. During his morning tally, the bailiff lifted every collar between thumb and forefinger, copying its painted title into neat columns according to the color of its varnish and the workshop from which it had come. Yet he never cracked a single seal of wax, never tilted a crock to test its weight, and never dipped his reed inside to learn whether the clay held sweet springwater, sour brine, or nothing but summer air. To the encoder at his trestle, a collar properly sorted was a vessel properly known; the interior belonged to another world.

Across the yard at the south trough, six leather hoses were stitched fast to the brass manifold and ran full into the waiting tubs. Beside them, four stout oak casks rested high on their timber cradles, polished and hooped in copper, yet no hose had ever been coupled to their spigots; they sat dry-throated above the splash. At the far corner stood an old red-painted cast pump, its cylinder split by frost long ago and its handle rusted solid against the pin. When the six yoke-haulers sat on the trough rim at noon to decode the bailiff's calfskin on the post, they nodded at the tally of full tubs—for they accepted that the furrows needed pouring—yet they spat into the gravel when they read the column of forty-eight collars, muttering that a steward who counts painted wood while four cradled casks lack leather is measuring pride, not rain.

Above the trough hung a grooved cedar bar carrying five sandglasses, one turned at each stroke of the bronze gong, beside a tallow tablet where the bailiff traced the falling waterline across the last five hours alone, wiping away the sixth hour with his sleeve as though earlier thirst had never been. Over that bar, carved deep into the lintel by the master of the farm, stood the household rule: better the cistern stand bone-dry before the fifth glass runs out than a single dipper of water remain idle on the trestle at dusk. Up in the high gallery, the seven elders of the Circle of the Cistern passed a copy of the noon calfskin from chair to chair and smiled, taking the dominant reading the master had carved into the stone: a vigorous estate spending its store to the last drop. Outside the split-rail fence, however, a widow from the parched lower orchard leaned on her hoe and read the same steep line in opposition; to her chair, a farm that emptied its tubs against the clock while the rusted pump stood broken was not feeding the land, only staging a theater of industry to justify barring the gate.

When the fifth sandglass thinned to a thread late in the afternoon and two tubs still remained unspent, the haulers obeyed the carved lintel and tipped them straight across the gravel so the trestle would stand bare on the stroke of the gong. Clear water ran under the fence into the roadside weeds. Down in the ditch sat an itinerant culvert-mender whom neither the bailiff in his pipe-less booth nor the elders in their gallery ever saw. Watching sweet water soak the nettles at the same hour every afternoon, he decoded the farm neither as industrious nor as greedy, but as a household governed by a superstition against stillness—the one reading no channel in the yard was built to carry back inside.
