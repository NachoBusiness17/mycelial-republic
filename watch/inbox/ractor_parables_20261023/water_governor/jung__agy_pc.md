# The Steward of the Sealed Cistern

Upstairs in the bright hall, a gilt portrait of the Water Governor hung above the dry washbasin, painted with a great brass wrench in his fist. Yet because the kitchen troughs remained parched at noon, Clara took a lantern and went down the cellar steps beneath the floorboards, where the house kept what it refused to look at in the light. The vault she entered was built of seamless granite without a single outer culvert, drain, or conduit piercing its foundation walls; nothing entered from the hills, and nothing left by any hidden channel. In the center of the cold floor stood the Governor in a dark wool coat. Before him rose the iron spindle of the main sluice, hammered smooth and stripped of any wheel or lever. He could not open a gate or send a single drop toward the scullery, for the spindle had been forged without a handle from the start.

When Clara asked what duty kept him in the vault, the Governor silently performed his five appointed tasks. First, he walked the stone racks with a piece of chalk, counting the vessels and sorting them into groups by their painted titles. Second, he pressed a wooden ear-horn against the flagstones to time the slow, rhythmic pulse of the cisterns underneath. Third, he wrote a neat summary of the vault's condition upon a slate for the upper stairs. Fourth, he climbed three steps to read the sliding brass frame that measured the five-hour ration spans. Fifth, he struck an iron fork against his plumb-line and tested the balance of his own horn and chalk to verify that his instruments were unbroken. Having completed the fifth task, he folded his hands inside his sleeves. He had been given no sixth motion; the office named to govern the water possessed no means to pour it.

Clara walked to the north rack, where forty-eight stoneware jars stood in classified rows, their necks sealed with thick black wax. The Governor had arranged all forty-eight strictly by the family titles painted across their clay bellies—hearth, table, orchard, reserve—without ever once prying up a stopper. When Clara lifted two jars and felt how light and hollow they were, she asked why he never broke the wax to see what lay inside. He answered that the rule of the cellar permitted him to classify the forty-eight names on the clay, never to inspect or taste the dark interior. Across the aisle, only six yoked water-bearers actually had leather hoses coupled from their casks to the house's rising main. Above them, four heavy oak barrels hung in iron wall-cradles with faded celebration ribbons still tied to their hoops, brimming and wet, yet their brass spigots jutted over bare flagstones with no tubing attached. The builders had rejoiced in hoisting the barrels into the cradles and had walked away before laying the humble pipe to join them to the trough.

At the far end of the rack stood the carved stone mouth of the Swift Spring, once boasted of as the fastest torrent on the estate. Clara reached into its throat and drew back fingers coated in white lime dust; the spring was completely dead, its vein collapsed and bone-dry, yet its polished brass plaque remained on the wall because no one in the house wished to strike a dead source from the roll. High on the landing above them ticked a five-hour water-clock hung over a permanent scroll of ration-spans. Its sliding frame crept across the parchment, tracing a continuous five-hour depletion of the manor's allowance. Beside the stair-tray, a written order in the Master's own hand was nailed into the oak post: he declared that he would far rather see every vessel in the manor drained to dry clay before dusk than discover a single full pitcher left sitting untouched on the ledge.

Clara unpinned the Master's order, took a pinch of white dust from the dead spring, and carried the Governor's slate up into the oak parlor. There the seven elders of the Water Council sat around an empty carafe, convened as the estate's assembly on the supply. They leaned forward to praise the Governor's careful slate and mourn the scarcity of the season. Clara laid the Master's parchment and the dead lime beside their cups, and asked why men who recited the Master's fierce thirst every morning kept forty-eight jars sealed, left four cradled barrels unjoined to the trough, and employed a Governor whose spindle had no wheel. The seven elders flinched and looked away from the portrait on the wall, unable to face the famine they had carefully built with their own hands.
