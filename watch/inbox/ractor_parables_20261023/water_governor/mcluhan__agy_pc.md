# The Dry Pavilion of the Sluices

High above the terraced orchards stood the octagonal pavilion where the bailiff, Toma, tended the brass index of the irrigation works. Earlier bailiffs had walked the ditches bare-legged, knowing the temper of the mountain snowmelt by the ache in their shins and the gravel-roar in their ears. Once the brass index was bolted to the cedar table, Toma never stepped into the mud; the instrument extended his eye across the valley and, in the way of all such vessels, numbed his heels and stopped his ears. No pipe entered the pavilion, no wire ran from its casing to the oak headgates below, and no lever on its face could lift a weir or release a single drop into the furrows. Its whole frame allowed only five motions of the hand: a notched wheel for calling the roll of the cellar casks, a reed that trembled with the pulse of the hill-springs, a horn through which Toma spoke the morning summary, a rack of five-hour sandglasses, and a small ivory striker with which he tapped the brass ribs to hear whether the instrument itself had cracked.

Each dawn Toma turned the notched wheel and descended with his chalk into the vaulted cellar where forty-eight stoneware amphorae stood in four cold ranks. Every vessel wore a clay collar stamped with the title of the orchard plot or vintage it was meant to feed, and every mouth was sealed with thick red pitch. Toma walked the ranks, reading the stamped collars aloud and sorting the forty-eight names into chalked columns on the masonry—some marked for the high terraces, some for the presses, some for the lower arbors. He never pried up a stopper, never dipped a reed into a neck, and never shook a belly to hear whether wine, spring-water, or only stale air lay beneath the wax. The collar-mark alone satisfied the wheel upstairs, and so a man trained by the wheel grew blind to the weight of the jar.

Along the south ridge, bronze troughs had been bracketed into the cliffside to carry water from the main mountain flume to the outer groves. Only six of those troughs were actually collared into the flume so that live water raced through them and turned the mill-paddles below. A dozen others hung gleaming on their iron hooks, bolted fast to the rock yet stopping a hand's-breadth short of the main stream, their mouths open to the wind while spiders spun across their dry throats. Further down the ravine lay the old lightning-chute, once the swiftest raceway on the estate, which used to hurl water down a polished basalt groove in the blink of an eye; a winter frost had split its bed from crown to heel, and now weed-stalks rattled in its dry trench while the bailiff's horn named it dead every morning without sending a mason to mend it.

Back in the pavilion, Toma's gaze rarely left the wooden frame where five-hour sandglasses turned on a sliding track. The glasses did not measure the depth of the mountain tarn, nor did they recall what had flowed the week before; each bulb showed only how much of the current five-hour allowance remained before the sand ran out and the track slid a fresh glass into place. Watching that falling thread of white grit remade Toma's sense of time. The long seasons of rain and drought vanished for him, replaced by a perpetual five-hour twilight in which the only urgency was the narrowing waist of the dust inside the crystal.

At midday a rider came up from the master of the vineyard bearing a cedar tablet carved with the rule of the estate. The master forbade any bailiff to finish a five-hour glass with water still standing in the holding basins; he had sworn that he would rather see every trough sucked dry and the ditch-men standing idle under a parched sky before the fifth hour ended than find a single unpoured measure left resting in the cool stone dark when the glass turned. Because Toma still had no cord or crank to open the headgates himself, he could only lean over the sill and blow his horn down the slope, shouting the master's decree to the ditch-men until they pried open the six live sluices and let the water roar wide over the roots.

Every seventh evening, the elders of the Council on the Watershed climbed the dry stair to deliberate around the cedar table. They never paused by the six roaring troughs, nor looked at the unjoined brackets on the cliff, nor laid a hand on the forty-eight sealed amphorae in the cellar below. They sat in a circle around the brass index, listening to its reed hum and watching the five-hour sand sink in the bulb, governing the streams in the dry, measured cadence that a room without water teaches to those who gaze upon its dial.
