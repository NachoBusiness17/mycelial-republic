# The Five-Hour Glass

The town stood at the bottom of a dry valley, and its water arrived from far away. In the middle of the town was the water yard, and at the back of the yard stood the counter's house, which had one wall with no opening in it at all — no letter-slot, no bell rope, no knocker. Nothing came to him that he did not go out and meet. Twice a day he made his round at the same hours, whether or not anyone was waiting, and those who needed him learned to stand where the round passed, because there was no other way to be found.

His round had five shapes and no more. He walked the yard. He counted. He told the elders what he had counted. He stood before the great glass. And on the last evening of the week he filled his own bucket at the spigot and weighed it in his hands, to know that the bucket and the hands still agreed. When the dry spring came and Sela asked him to drive the north water up to her terraces, he heard her out and shook his head. "I can tell you what runs and what does not," he said. "Sending is not one of my shapes." No one in the valley had ever seen him touch a sluice.

He carried a book in a greasy cloth cover. In it were forty-eight springs, each under its name, and under each name a mark of what sort of name it was — deep, seasonal, mineral, sweet. He had not tasted one of them. Six channels were joined to the yard and running; he tapped their spigots each round and listened. Along the north wall lay the mounted pipes, fitted and sealed and strapped to their trestles, joined to nothing at either end. At the bottom of the book one name stood with a cross beside it, its channel broken past mending; it stayed in the book, because a name once counted is never dropped, only crossed.

In the tower above the yard hung the glass. It ran five hours from crown to crown and was turned the instant it emptied, at whatever hour that was, in dark or daylight, so that it never began at dawn and never ended at dusk; it only ever showed the last five hours of drinking. The allowance was read off the falling level, and the town had learned to read it. Thirst, which had once been a feeling in the throat, had become a position of sand. Farmers came to the yard not when they were dry but when the level said so, and went away content if the sand permitted, even with their tongues dusty.

The master of the works came at evening with a stick and knocked over any jar that still held water. Better, he said, that the cisterns stand empty at moonrise than full at morning. Every drop brought into the valley was to leave it again by dark — into the fields, into the vats, into the gutters of the houses. The town ran dry most nights, and this was called right, and the master slept well. Nothing stood on a high shelf long enough to grow old.

Once a season the water committee of the council met under the fig tree, seven elders who had never walked to the springs. They received the counter's telling, read the falling level, argued over the crossed name, and set the size of the next day's allowance. They knew the water as a book, a level of sand, and a telling, and they governed it so, and the valley throve or dried according to how well those shapes fit the far springs none of them had seen.

Sela went out once, against the season, and walked the three days to the deep spring whose name was marked sweet. She found it loud and cold and wider than its mark. She put her hand in and held it there a long while, and the water was colder than any line in the book, and no one had written that down. The day she came home the glass was being turned again at noon, the master was tipping the last jars, and the counter stood in the yard weighing his bucket. She went and stood where his round passed, and waited, because that was the only place a thing could be said.
