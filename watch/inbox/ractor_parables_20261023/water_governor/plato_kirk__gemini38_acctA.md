# The Governor of the Cistern

The high reservoir stood inside a limestone cavern between the quarry and the sunlit terrace. In its entry chamber sat Julian, who occupied a high wooden chair set three paces from the northern wall. Torches burned behind him, casting the shapes of workers and timbers onto the damp stone. Those who labored below believed the flickering shadows were the movement of the water itself, but Julian kept his eyes fixed upon the stonework before him. He had been taught that there were only two kinds of men: those who fled the cave into the blinding heat of the noon plains, and those who remained to rewrite the rules of the chamber. Julian did neither in haste; he sat in the chair to study the premises of the room.

The hall was called the governor’s station, yet it possessed neither wheels, valves, nor spillway levers. Julian held no spigots in his hands and could not direct a single cup of water down to the thirsty terraced gardens. When the elders from the Committee on Water came up the stairs with their clay jugs, demanding that he release the stream, he showed them his empty palms. He had only five permitted actions, and diversion was not among them.

His first action was to walk the gallery and inspect the great timber board where forty-eight bronze markers were hung on brass pegs. Julian touched every marker in turn, grouping them by their stamped emblems—springs, cisterns, wells, and snowfields. Beneath each marker lay a recessed wooden cup plugged with wax, containing the secret cipher of the source; Julian never picked at the wax or read what lay inside, for his duty was solely to classify the names upon the metal plates, leaving their hidden depths untouched.

His second action was to strike the copper gong once each turn of the glass, sending a single clear vibration down the shafts so the diggers below knew the house was awake. His third action was to chalk the current depth and balance of the basins upon the public slate hanging by the gate, making the condition of the reservoir plain to any passerby. His fourth action was to turn the five-hour sand-clepsydra, watching the fine red powder drain through the narrow neck to measure the rolling exhaustion of the estate’s allotment. His fifth action was to take his iron compass from the table and fit its points against the master score lines chiseled into the threshold stone, ensuring his own measuring tools had not warped in the humidity.

Along the curved vault ran the aqueduct cradles. Six stone conduits were fully strapped with iron hoops and lead caulking, channeling spring water from the high hills into the pool. Yet beside them hung empty cradles—notches cut into the granite and iron brackets bolted into the rock that held no stone or tile at all, gaps where pipes had been mounted in the architecture but never joined to any stream. Of the six working channels, the one carved from dark slate lay completely cold and silent; dust clung to its dry lip, and dead moths gathered in its trough, for its source had failed long ago and no moisture ever reached its mouth.

The elders of the Committee on Water gathered around Julian’s chair, murmuring of drought. They urged him to hoard the remaining barrels, to hide the slate, and to treat the scarcity as an unalterable fate of the mountain. They spoke of the shortage as a trial that could only be endured by starving the vines.

Julian pointed to the arch above the threshold, where the decree of the master builder was carved deep into the lintel: it is better to drain the basins to the silt than to leave water sleeping on the shelf while the earth thirsts. The estate had not been designed to praise caution or to congratulate keepers who died beside full jars. The test of the mountain was not whether a man could survive inside a darkened vault by clinging to shadows of plenty, but whether he had the nerve to spend every measured drop within the five-hour span before the sand ran out.

He did not open a valve, for he possessed none. He merely chalked the five-hour depletion upon the slate and struck the copper gong. When the council saw that the dry basalt conduit was useless, that the forty-eight names were accounted for, and that the water on the shelf was meant to be poured without fear, they ceased praying to the shadows on the wall and climbed out to lay pipes across the empty cradles.
