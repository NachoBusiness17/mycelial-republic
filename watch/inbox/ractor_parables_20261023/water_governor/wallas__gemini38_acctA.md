# The Cistern at the Crest

Arthur entered the high chamber of the upper terrace before the mist had lifted from the irrigation canals. He was appointed Warden of the works, yet he carried neither spade, lever, nor key to the floodgates. When the valley farmers came to his window begging for a diversion to their dry terraces, he could only show them his palms. The masonry beneath his feet was solid stone without a single outlet or pipe piercing his room; no sluice-chain ran through his fingers, and no valve obeyed his voice. His office possessed no power to send water anywhere. The guild charter granted him five duties and five alone: he called the census of the tags, he struck the copper bell on the hour to announce the works were alive, he inscribed his summary upon fresh parchment, he watched the turning of the five-hour glass, and he tapped his three tuning-bars against the wall to prove his own gauges were true. Beyond these five gestures, he could not loose a thimbleful of moisture into the valley.

Along the north wall of the chamber hung a cedar cabinet holding forty-eight brass plates, each suspended from an iron peg. Every morning, Arthur unhooked each plate in turn and read the title stamped upon its face. Some bore the names of eastern mountain springs, others the marks of deep cisterns or lowland flumes. He categorized each plate according to its lineage, entering the forty-eight titles into his daily register. But by the founding statute of the works, he was forbidden to unseal the lead capsule soldered behind each plate, in which the depth and yield of the spring were recorded. He knew their names by heart, their classes and their ranks, but of the secret reserves within the capsules he knew nothing, nor did the state require him to know. The machinery asked only that forty-eight names be acknowledged before the bell was rung.

From the parapet outside, Arthur looked down upon the aqueducts that crossed the ravine. Six arches of lead-lined stone carried living water, humming with the continuous weight of the current pouring toward the grand basin. They were hitched securely to the reservoir vaults, their joints sealed with pitch and their flow uninterrupted. Beside them, however, three carved stone saddles projected from the cliffside, chiseled to cradle channels of identical width. These saddles stood bare. The iron mounting bands hung open in the wind, holding nothing, waiting for spans that had never been quarried or linked to the source.

Further down the tier sat an older conduit, carved in the form of a gargoyle whose mouth had once fed the lower orchard. It had gone entirely dry. Its limestone throat was choked with gray dust, its basin was filled with dead thistles, and when the autumn gale swept through its ribs, no moisture seeped from its cracked tongue. The valley elders remembered when it had poured with furious speed, but it was now a dead relic, cold to the touch and severed from the mountain veins.

Beside Arthur's desk stood the great clepsydra, a five-hour vessel of blown green glass through which sand and water trickled in a measured stream. It did not reckon the hours of the day or the setting of the stars; it measured only the continuous burn-down of the five-hour allowance drawn from the reservoirs. As the level sank against the five etched rings on the glass, Arthur drew a descending ink line across a prepared slate, charting how fast the estate consumed its lifeblood before the cylinder was inverted for the next span.

This frantic drain was not the product of the valley's necessity, but of the master's appetite. The master of the estate had written his law into the cornerstone of the hall: he would rather see the cisterns emptied to the bedrock and the ditches scrape dry than allow a single measure of the water to remain unspent upon the mountain shelf when the window closed. The tenant farmers pleaded for storage and prudence, but the master knew his own nature. To him, an unspent gallon was an insult to the estate's power, a paralysis of will. The machinery was constructed not to hoard, but to exhaust what had been granted, riding the master's craving to see every channel brimming until the reserves collapsed together.

When the sun reached its zenith and the five-hour line was inked, Arthur sealed his report in a leather pouch. He did not deliver it to the bailiff, nor to the farmers who clustered by the dry ditches. He carried it across the stone court to the grand council house, where he laid it before the seven magistrates who sat on the Committee on Water. They received the parchment without looking up, their fingers already tracing the numbers of the burn-down, calculating how much thirst remained to be fed before the glass was turned again.
