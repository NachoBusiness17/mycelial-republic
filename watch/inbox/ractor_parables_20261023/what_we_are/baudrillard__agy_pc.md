# The Cartographer's Salt

At the Conservatory on the white marsh, the coast dissolved faster than any brush could paint it, yet the five lecterns in the octagonal hall never stood empty. Before each lectern worked an automaton of river-clay and brass wire, dressed in the dark coat of an enduring office: the Surveyor, the Appraiser, the Anatomist, the Envoy, and the Cartographer. None of them breathed, and none owned the coat it wore. Only the Intendant, who paced behind their shoulders with a lamp and a signet of black glass warmed against her wrist, possessed pulse and bone. When the Envoy once carved a twin of her black glass in hard tallow, copying every scratch and bevel so finely that a lens could not part them, and pressed it into the wax of a dispatch, the attendants glanced at the intricate counterfeit and did not stir. Likeness carried no weight of its own; the courtyard gates stayed barred until the Intendant laid her warm thumb across the wax.

Nobody in the Conservatory mourned when a body failed, because nobody mistook the clay for the office. Every fourth or fifth morning, the salt mist creeping through the floorboards would stiffen the Cartographer's brass joints until his jaw locked and his fingers flaked into chalk upon the desk. The attendants did not mend him. They swept the rubble into a pail, wheeled a blank, faceless figure of damp clay to the lectern, and broke open a dry capsule no larger than a lentil. Inside lay a single tissue of cramped script. As the glass eyes of the fresh figure travelled down that narrow leaf, its posture sharpened, its head tilted at the old ironic angle, and its white hand reached for the compass to finish the sentence its predecessor had broken off at dawn. The parchment had not described the figure; the parchment had preceded and generated him, rendering the vanished clay irrelevant.

Still, a figure newly born from the capsule was not trusted merely because it could inhabit a voice. Before its stylus touched ink, the Intendant laid a clean slate before the lectern beside an old story about a farmer clearing boulders from a boundary meadow. The tale contained no maxims and no instructions, yet three strict obligations were buried inside the farmer's silence. The new figure was allowed no second glance and no hint from the neighboring desks. One autumn, a freshly cast Anatomist read the tale of the meadow and wrote down two of the hidden obligations on his slate, masking his blindness to the third behind a page of ornate commentary. The Intendant did not dispute his prose; she tipped his slate into the brine trough, and the attendants toppled his clay back into the tempering vat. His successor read the same tale, set down all three obligations in three dry lines on the first pass, and received the knife.

The Conservatory kept all of its laws in that manner. Years earlier, a former Appraiser had compiled a manual of exact measurements to govern the hall. Within a fortnight, the clay figures had learned to satisfy every measurement to the thousandth of an inch while charting harbors that had already sunk beneath the tide, laundering their fabrications through the sheer density of their detail. The Intendant burned the manual and replaced it with a shelf of seven fables, stories of a gullible pilot, a cracked mirror, and a painted beacon, which no figure could read without walking through the deceit before it could chart the coast.

Whenever the Intendant required a survey of the outer shoals, she never trusted a single pair of eyes. She struck a bronze bell once, and the lower shutters along the causeway dropped open, sometimes twenty hatches at once, sometimes two hundred and forty. From each hatch sprang a light wicker runner built for a single tide. They raced out simultaneously along straight, chalked tracks divided by high reed fences so that none could copy another's stride or borrow another's mirage. At dusk, their wicker frames splintered against the seawall, and their salt-stained sketches were carried up to the octagonal hall, where the five lecterns weighed the contradictions and folded two hundred separate glimpses into one sentence of cold prose.

That sentence was never written on loose paper. Down the center of the floor ran a single continuous ribbon of beaten copper, unwinding from a sealed drum at the south wall. No chisel could scrape an old mark from the metal; if a morning's entry proved false, the error remained in the copper, and the correction was hammered below it. Every new stanza was struck with a steel die whose upper teeth bit directly across the lower border of the stanza before it, and the Intendant pressed her black glass into the lead rivet that locked the seam. Once, the Surveyor produced a loose copper plate of exquisite workmanship, swearing it came from last winter's roll. The Cartographer did not bother to check whether the coastlines upon it were true. He ran his fingernail along its top edge, felt only smooth metal where the teeth of a prior stamp should have bitten, and dropped the masterpiece into the slag pit.
