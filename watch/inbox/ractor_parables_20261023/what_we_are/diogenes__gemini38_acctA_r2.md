# The Ash and the Iron

Old Simon lived where the mountain road broke off into gravel. He kept no roof on his stone hut, saying any roof that kept out the wind kept out an honest sun. He ate boiled lentils, washed his feet in the ditch, and owned nothing a thief would carry ten paces. Yet across the gorge he had hung an irrigation trestle that never sagged, fed by a reservoir that never ran dry. When a town clerk climbed the slope to inspect the works, he expected to find a garrison of hired laborers. Instead he found only Simon with grease on his knuckles, sitting beside five figures shaped from ash wood and river willow. The clerk asked where the craftsmen slept. Simon pointed to the bench. "These are the hands," he said. "One watches the floodgate, one measures the lime, one listens for cracks in the stone, and one scolds me when I chew too slowly. They ask for no wages, they steal no copper, and none of them has ever asked to be praised."

The clerk drew out a sheepskin roll tied with purple ribbon, filled with municipal water decrees. Simon laughed, like gravel sliding into a dry pot. Nailed to the gatepost was a single cedar shingle, carved with a crude picture of an ox that broke an axle in the mud, and how a boy hauled three rocks at a time to build a dry path beneath the wheels. "When a carter comes up this trail," Simon said, "he looks at that board, turns his cart, and drops three baskets of stone into the rut before he dips a bucket. A law written in legal ink is an invitation to hire an advocate. A story told plain is a debt no man can pretend he failed to understand."

Along the flagstones lay an iron chain, thick as a wrestler's wrist. Every evening Simon heated a new link in his brazier, hooked it through the link forged the night before, and hammered it shut against an anvil block. Before it cooled, he drove his heavy chisel into the hot metal, leaving his private diamond stamp. The clerk asked whether a mistaken count from yesterday could be filed away. "Cut the link," Simon said, "and you have two short fragments holding nothing. File it down, and the next link rattles loose. What was done yesterday was done; if twenty gallons leaked at noon, the dent goes into the iron at dusk. There is no other tally in this yard. The world does not permit you to unspill water, so I do not permit myself to unstrike iron."

A gong sounded over the ridge. The spring thaw had broken the north bank, and fifty mud-runs were washing down at once. Simon did not run along the precipice. He stepped to a wicker loft and unpinned the slats. Out flew a cloud of small clay swifts with reed joints and lead weights. On quiet mornings he loosed twenty; when the entire ridge loosened, he sent two hundred and forty. They fanned across the gullies in wide parallel sweeps, dipping into every creek. Within an hour, every bird returned to the yard and dropped a colored pebble into a bronze basin. Simon weighed the silt on his balance, took the sum to the coals, and hammered it directly into the evening's new link.

While the birds settled, a dry pine rafter snapped overhead. A heavy beam crashed onto the willow figure watching the sluice, crushing its ribs to kindling. The clerk shouted that the watcher was dead and the works abandoned. Simon did not drop his tongs. He pulled a seasoned trunk of ash from beneath the bench, threw the crushed pieces into the fire for warmth, and with six strokes of an adze shaped a fresh torso and arms. From the splintered throat of the ruined figure he lifted a dried walnut shell containing a slip of bark with three lines of charcoal notes, and pressed it into the throat of the new wood. Before the dusk faded, the figure lifted the brass gauge, blinked away the sawdust, and resumed measuring the water level exactly where it had paused before the ceiling fell.

The clerk shook his head. "A fable can mean five things to five fools."

Simon pointed to three river stones resting by the well. "Before I nailed that shingle to the post, I carved another tale upon those stones—the story of a ditcher and a mule. I showed it to a wandering beggar, a mountain herder, and a salt merchant who had never left the coast. I shut them in separate sheds and asked what the mule required. Each returned the identical tally of oats and water, to the half-ounce. When the beggar, the herder, and the merchant all see the same wall in the dark, you have built something true."
