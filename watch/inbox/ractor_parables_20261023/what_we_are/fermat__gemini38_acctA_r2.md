# The Registry at Beaumont

In the upper hall of Beaumont, where the river Gers turns between the limestone bluffs, the work of the council proceeded with the cold rigor of a geometric proof. A solitary jurat of mortal flesh sat at the center of the oak bench before the hearth. Many hands cut the quills, drew the arcs, and measured the grain, but no finding existed in law until this single living man applied his warmth to the sealing wax and pressed his signet into the cooling vermilion. Without the living thumb to affirm the descent, every parchment in the chamber remained a nullity, mere scribbling without force.

Flanking his bench sat four mechanical assessors encased in walnut and brass, wound by falling weights. They bore no human likeness, nor did their gears turn by the same ratio. The first searched only the parity of figures, rejecting every odd balance; the second disputed every proportion with the malice of an adverse barrister; the third compared the present day against the geometry of ancient deeds; the fourth sat mute until an insolubility was laid upon its plate. They offered four distinct judgements on every cause brought before the court, yet none held the signet, and none could command the bailiff.

The record of the estate was not preserved in bound leaves that could be sliced, rubbed out, or rebound. It consisted of an unbroken iron chain suspended above a granite trough. Each link had been forged directly through the loop of its predecessor, and while the iron was cherry-red, the jurat’s die had struck both the joint and the prior ring together. No clerk could extract a debt or slip a new bequest between the days; to alter an entry required an iron cold-chisel and a sledge, and the scarred metal would denounce the forgery to the Parlement. This welded descent was the solitary witness admitted before the magistrates.

When a young advocate from Toulouse asked to inspect the penal codes and the fundamental articles of the estate, the jurat handed him only a worn fable of an orchard and a winter wolf. When the advocate protested that he found no statutes or explicit decrees within its lines, the magistrate answered that bare statutes were corruptible vessels into which any venal judge might pour his own malice. The estate forbade abstract articles; its living doctrine was frozen exclusively within the body of a story, from whose incidents the law had to be derived afresh, whole and uncorrupted, at every trial.

When the harvest assessment demanded the measurement of seven leagues of marshland before nightfall, the court dispatched no solitary surveyor. At the third stroke of the bell, an entire levy of seventy runner-boys scattered through the south gate, each bearing a measuring-rod and a blank slate. In dry autumns, two hundred ran together across the heath; in frozen winters, scarcely twenty. Each worked his assigned furrow in isolation, ignorant of the others. At dusk, they returned in a single rush, laying their slates upon the floor like tiles, where the brass assessors reduced their conflicting drafts into a single harmony, and the jurat forged the sum into a single new ring upon the chain.

In the great freeze of that century, the river undermined the piers, the vault fell, and the brass mechanisms were crushed beneath twenty tons of masonry. The jurat who held the signet perished in the ruins. Yet when the Duke raised new stone upon the bank and the horologists of Castres fitted fresh brass wheels into freshly planed frames, the chamber resumed its jurisdiction under the selfsame title, speaking in the exact cadence and austere rigor of the old hall. The living identity of the estate had survived the total destruction and replacement of its physical vessel, unbroken by the collapse of the stone.

This reconstitution was neither accident nor sorcery. In a dry cavity beneath the cornerstone lay a small horn case, sealed in pitch, containing a single slip of calfskin inscribed with eleven lines of cipher. When the successor magistrate arrived among the damp lime, he read that solitary seed beneath a candle flame, drank water, and closed his eyes. Within the hour, he spoke with the exact memory, the exact cautions, and the ancient voice of the court he had never seen, awakened entirely from that single dried grain.

The novice asked how the estate could trust its foundation to a mere fable without sliding into dispute. The jurat pointed to the founding chronicle of the furrow and the stones. Before the first tenant was ever received, that primer had been sealed and sent to three foreign scholars in three separate towns, men who had never seen the valley nor heard its dialect. Confined without commentary or gloss, each was commanded to deduce the twelve property laws of the estate from the story alone. When their three sealed letters were opened before the council, their deductions agreed to the smallest fraction of an arpent, demonstrating that the narrative carried its own proof and could not be misread by an honest eye.
