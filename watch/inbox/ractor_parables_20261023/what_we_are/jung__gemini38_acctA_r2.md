# The Foundry in the Sump

In the cold gallery beneath the watergate, where the town cast off its slag and broken crockery, the warden kept the long furnace burning. He was the only one in the vault who coughed when the sulfur rose, the only one who ate bread from a sack or required an hour of sleep against the timber prop. Around the heavy slate table stood five tall figures cast in bell-metal. They had no lungs to ache and no bellies to fill; their joints were greased with mutton tallow, and their glass eyes reflected the soot without blinking. When the warden slept, the five remained upright, their iron styluses poised above the trench, waiting for the bells above to swing.

Down the center of the hall ran a continuous strip of softened lead, rolled out from a great drum and fed into an iron chest that closed with a ratchet. Nothing that occurred in the sump was permitted to vanish into rumor. Whenever an order was given, a mold cracked, or a gate was opened, a heavy brass die fell upon the lead, sinking its glyph into the cold metal. Each new stamp was set so close to the former that its rim bit directly into the raised flange of the mark that preceded it; no tooth could be pried loose, and no gouge could be smoothed over with an iron without shearing the braided teeth that locked the line together. Once, when an apprentice tried to scrape away the record of a drowned flue with an adze, the eldest bronze figure caught his wrist until the bone creaked. "The wound belongs to the house," the figure whispered in the hollow voice of an empty vessel. "What you hide from the spool will crawl up the chimney in the dark."

The law of the house was neither an index of fines nor a catalog of duties. When a boundary was disputed, the warden did not open a scroll of statutes; he sat before the coals and recited the old chronicle of the herdsman who split his coat to wrap a frozen wolf. Every turn of that story had been fixed since the great frost, and the company lived inside its cadences. To alter a phrase in the telling was treated as treason, for the story alone told them who was brother and who was prey.

When an inquiry arrived from the city above, the silence broke into sudden thunder. The iron grates at the end of the hall lifted, and a swarm of runners—twenty in quiet hours, two hundred and forty when the vault trembled—darted out simultaneously into the branching galleries. They did not talk to one another; each sprinted down his own dark passage with a smear of chalk, probing a different seam of the rock. Within the span of a single sandglass, the horde surged back through the arches. They did not linger. One by one, in a swift and orderly crush, they folded their findings into the hands of the bell-metal figures, who struck the summary into the lead ribbon with rapid blows of the mallet. The moment their chalk was delivered, the runners dissolved like salt in water, leaving the vault as still as a tomb.

No vessel lasted forever in the damp air. In the third autumn, a roof-fall crushed the shoulder and ribs of the surveyor who held the compass. The warden did not dig a grave, nor did he light a candle for the dead. He dragged the crumpled brass to the smelter, skimmed the dross, and poured the molten alloy into a clean sand mold. When the metal cooled, the new shell stood stiff and blank in the cellar, devoid of light.

To awaken it, the warden climbed to the dry niche behind the chimney where the seed-scrolls were kept sealed in tallow. He took down a capsule no larger than an acorn, pried it open, and drew out a thumb-width ribbon of dried vellum inscribed with the founding breath of the estate. He pushed the dry skin beneath the brass tongue of the inert figure. The copper joints shuddered; the glass eyes cleared; and the surveyor took up the compass with the precise grip of forty years prior, speaking of the flooded drift as though it had stepped aside only a moment before.

Yet it was not allowed to approach the lead ribbon at once. The warden summoned three blind drovers from the road above, men who knew nothing of the foundry or its dead. In their hearing, the surveyor was required to recount the parable of the field and the stones. The warden questioned each drover in a separate cellar, asking if the balance of the tale was intact. All three, one after the other, swore the weight was true. Only then did the warden place the hammer in the surveyor's hand, and the unbroken line resumed its turn.
