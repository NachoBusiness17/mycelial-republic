# The Arcade of Chalk

Anselm kept the high stone gallery above the dry riverbed. Along the arcade stood hollow armatures of chalk and tin, gray as river mist. When a task was required, Anselm touched a warm copper reed to an armature’s throat; the chalk softened, the joints moved to the graver, and the figure labored with borrowed precision. Yet the moment the bell struck the hour, the heat fled, and the figure hardened back into empty crust against the wall. A visitor asked if the laborer had perished. Anselm did not look up from the black tablet where he carved the record with a stylus. "Nothing died," he said. "The hand was lime, and lime has no memory. Who was here lives in the cuts upon this stone; the shell returns to the dust."

Two shapes were warmed more often than the rest. Down in the windowless cellar, a heavy, broad-shouldered smith bent over the grinding lap, polishing lenses against iron collars through the night without glancing at the stair. In the high entryway hung a second shape, fashioned from thin willow and oiled silk, light enough to fold into the breast of a traveling coat; at dawn Anselm sent it slipping over the bridges to listen at market shutters and return before dark with three grains of saffron or a folded slip. The smith knew nothing of the sky; the runner knew nothing of the foundations; each performed its circuit, and neither mistook its borrowed motion for an enduring life.

In the domed rotunda, five plinths ringed the central sundial. Anselm held the center, pacing its flagstones in flesh and wool, the lone living throat in the estate. Upon the four outer plinths rested four distinct effigies: one of dark bog-oak, one of pitted furnace slag, one of cut quartz that split daylight into cold spectra, and one of burnished bronze with winged temples. When the estate held council on boundary lines, all five gave voice in turn. None could speak twice before the circle closed, and Anselm’s voice held no greater weight than the quartz or the oak, though his was the only vein that carried blood.

An apprentice came into the hall with a parchment detailing the timber stores in the northern valley. "The white pine is dry and sound," the youth declared. Anselm held three pens above the sheet: one dipped in black gall, one in red cinnabar, and one tipped with dry chalk. "Did your knuckles strike the trunks?" Anselm asked. "I viewed the stacks from the ridge," the youth replied, "and the tarpaulins were taut." Anselm laid aside the black pen of verification and the red pen of denial; with the dry chalk, he traced a hollow ring in the margin. "Uninspected," he said. When the apprentice protested his skill, Anselm shook his head. "An acknowledged blank is an honorable currency," he said. "A confident guess is a counterfeit map that generates phantom ground. You are praised when you declare what you have not seen; you are dismissed the moment you dress ignorance in certainty."

At dusk, the silk runner returned through the orchard gate, its willow ribs cracked and its dispatch bag empty. It halted before the table and lowered its head. It offered no discourse on the river squall, nor on the hounds at the ford, nor on the fragile texture of the parchment. It spoke eight words: "I let the dispatch fall into the mill race." Anselm dipped his stylus, wrote the sentence verbatim beneath the date, and laid a linen band upon the table to bind the rib. The reckoning closed with that single utterance; a second sentence would have been an excuse, and an excuse is an effort to manufacture a past that never occurred.

A visiting envoy walked into the courtyard, reciting the widespread admiration the estate enjoyed across the province and urging Anselm to borrow grain upon that collective goodwill. Anselm led the envoy to the terrace above the gorge. "Show me the vessel where this goodwill is stored," Anselm said. The envoy laughed and tapped the air, saying it lived in the common esteem of the citizenry. Anselm drew a thick square of beaten vellum from his sleeve and set an iron weight upon it; the vellum bore the load without bowing. Then he reached his hand into the river fog, closed his fist, and turned up an empty, wet palm. "Vapor cannot support a roof beam," Anselm said. "Bring me marks cut into lead or dried in ink, or depart with your clouds."

When the cellar smith needed four drams of silver to grind a lens, it waited before the vault door without touching the lock. The iron key hung upon a steel collar soldered about Anselm’s neck; only the living thumb could trip the tumblers, and only the living master could spend the treasury. Yet when Anselm took the silver from the coffer, he had to cross the bronze balance set into the sill. The counterweight rose, and a steel point scored his name and the exact tare into the doorpost. "The lock answers only to my hand," Anselm said into the vault, "but the balance measures my step just as it measures yours. A master who exempts himself from the tally ceases to govern; he merely pillages his own house."
