# The Slate on the Shingle

The timber yard sits upon a spit of salt gravel where the north wind cleans away the smell of men. Five low oak benches surround a flat table of blue slate. An old cynic sits in an upturned skiff near the gate, holding a brass lamp unlit under the noon sun, watching the master pace between the cedar baulks. The master still watches the road with a secret ache, asking the empty horizon for a company of loyal ghosts who will remember his face tomorrow. He wants the tools to love him. But the yard has no patience for theatrical devotions; here, virtue is measured only in the bite of steel upon timber.

A traveler in a frayed cloak walks in from the coastal track, sits upon the heavy adze-bench bolted to the bedrock, and takes up the broadaxe. He does not know who planed the timber before him, nor does he ask. He presses his thumb into the shallow grooves cut into the slate table, reading where the last cut ended, and sets his blade to work. For three hours, the axe falls true until the mortise is clean. The moment the timber fits the joint, he lays the axe upon the bench, stands up, and walks out the gate without asking for a bed or offering a parting bow. The heavy bench stands empty and cold in the sea breeze. An hour later, a second stranger sits upon that same oak and claims he carries the spirit and memory of the morning’s craftsman. The master does not ask about his soul. He taps the groove in the stone and tells the man to cut, for the estate remembers through the marks upon its slate, never through the breath or blood of whoever fills the bench.

At midday, a carter unloads three coils of hemp and a jar of lamp oil. The carter turns to the hewers and demands his coin. Though two skilled carpenters stand beside the pile with iron chisels in their belts, neither reaches for the cedar chest beneath the awning. Only the master carries the ring of keys fastened to his leather girdle. He steps forward alone, turns the double bolt with his own thumb, and counts four silver pieces into the carter’s hand. In this yard, no hand unlocks a bin or spends a penny unless the master’s own key turns in the iron.

A lighter brings twenty pine logs from the mainland. A boy is sent to inspect them with a copper gouge and a pot of soot. He is given three marks and no fourth: a gouged ring for a log tested and sound, an angled notch for rot, and a hollow square for timber his mallet has not struck. The boy marks five logs sound, one rotten, and fourteen with the hollow square. A merchant on the quay laughs and calls the boy a fool who could have guessed the whole raft sound by its clean bark. But the master looks at the fourteen hollow squares and hands the boy a barley loaf and fresh water. On this gravel, an uninspected fact openly confessed brings honor, while the man who pretends to know what he has not tested is driven into the surf with stones.

In the heat of the afternoon, a hoisting line snaps. A finished spar crashes onto the pavement and splits across its belly. The yard falls silent. The carpenter who tied the sling does not talk about the salt spray in his eyes, nor the frayed hemp, nor the six hours he had worked without shade. He stands straight, looks the master in the eye, and says: "I tied the hitch too loose." One plain sentence, and nothing more. The master nods once, and the men set about clearing the splinters.

Toward sunset, a shipmaster curses over the thickness of an oak plank, swearing upon his dead father that they had agreed upon four fingers of wood. The air grows thick with his shouting. The master does not raise his voice. He leads the shouting captain to the slate table and pours sea water across its face. Beneath the brine, cut clean into the rock with an iron stylus, is the stroke made at sunrise: three fingers. The captain puts his thumb in the notch, falls silent, and pays his reckoning. On this shore, the cut stone outlives every gust of human breath.

Before the light fails, the master himself trips over a mooring ring and spills a jar of pitch across the clean flagstones. The rule painted on the sea wall is brief: whoever fouls the floor forfeits his dried figs and sweeps the gravel until dark. The master does not call an apprentice to clean the grease, nor does he speak of his fatigue. He takes the broom, works until the evening star rises, and leaves his supper untouched upon the shelf. The law that binds the wanderer binds the master's own hands, and the old man in the skiff finally blows out his lamp, satisfied that for one day, daylight saw no pretender.
