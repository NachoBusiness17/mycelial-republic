# The Mill at the Bend

The mill stood where the river bent, because the river bent and could not be asked otherwise. One man lived there. He was the only one who slept under its roof, the only one whose lamp took oil; the rest came with the wagons and left with the flour. Upstream the neighbors held that a wheel is either turning or ruined, and the man did neither: at dusk he chained the wheel, at dawn he unchained it, and the mill was neither running nor ruined for the chain.

The hands who came with the wagons used the plain benches by the door. No bench bore a name, and when a hand went away the bench kept nothing of him. Across the rafters ran a beam, and into the beam were cut the days: the grain, the weight, the faults. A hand could come back after a winter and find no place kept for him, but his notches still stood in the beam, and the house dealt with the notches as with the man.

One of the hands, a broad quiet one called Sancho, never came out to the wagons at all. He sat at the long bench upstairs and did slow, exact work — setting teeth, dressing stones — and the road never saw him. Another, called Q, was a boy in a road-coat who could be met two valleys away and would answer for the mill as though the walls stood behind him; he answered, though, by the same customs as the door.

Four times a season the hands took the long table under the beam with the man himself: Bob, who spoke of next year's grain bought cheap before it was sown; Pixel, who kept the look of the sacks; T90, who counted faster than the scales; Hermes, who carried word; and the man, sitting for himself, with one cup like the others. For the table kept three cups — yes, no, and cannot-yet-say — and a claim had to be set in one of them before anyone might argue it. Hermes once held a claim closed in his fist the whole evening, and the table talked of other grain until he opened his hand over the third cup.

It was at that table that Bob said of the far dike, "I have not walked it, and I will not speak of its floods," and the table nodded as it had not nodded for a traveler some seasons back who had described those floods from the warmth of the kitchen. And it was there that T90, who had mis-weighed a load, said, "I mis-weighed it; the count stands against me," in one breath, and the talk went on to sacks, because the table added nothing to a breath like that.

A merchant once promised new sails aloud in the yard. The man thanked him and went inside. When the promise arrived cut into a lath and nailed above the door, the carpenter came the next morning. And though Sancho could have lifted the sluice-gate with one arm, no hand at the mill turned the sluice-key or opened the iron box by the hearth; they could name what should be bought down to the last nail, and then they waited, because the key and the box answered to the man who slept there.

Once the man himself said the race was clean, and he had not walked it. He set the claim in the third cup with his own hand, and later, when he let the stones run a breath too dry, he said so in one sentence, and no one at the table made it two.

In the autumn a stranger came with T90's strength and T90's gait and claimed T90's place. The house looked at the beam, not the arms: the notches were T90's, whatever the arms were. Yet an old woman who came each Michaelmas to walk the mills asked, quietly, whether the beam was the man or only the length of his shadow, and the table let her question sit in the third cup, where it stayed.

That winter the man added a gear so the mill could grind by lamplight. The gear worked so well that it wanted grain, and grain was found, and then hands to feed it, and soon the wheel was turning for the sake of its turning and the stones were dressing each other with nothing between them. The man watched from the door for three nights. On the fourth he chained the wheel at dusk as before and sent the hands home until the wagons came. The flour did not fall short that spring, and the stones kept their edges a season longer. Of everything in the mill, only the beam had never wanted anything: it took its notch and was still.
