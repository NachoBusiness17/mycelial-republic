# The Inquest at Beaumont

In the high chancery at Beaumont, the floor was paved with grey flagstones that stayed cold through August. Along the wall stood five high-backed chairs carved from chestnut, arranged about a trestle of smoothed slate. The chairs were never claimed as possessions. A clerk would arrive from town, take the heavy place near the hearth to engross a contract until his fingers stiffened, and then stand, leaving the timber bare. Another, a courier with dust upon his boots, would slip through the postern with a folded paper tucked into his coat, deliver his figures against the casement, and depart before the dust settled. The chairs cooled quickly; no one lingered once the ink was dry.

At the head presided the magistrate in the black wool of the provincial court. At his girdle hung an iron key, the only key that turned the bolt of the strongbox in the wall. When money was needed for lime or parchment, his hand alone lifted the lid; when coin was returned, his stylus made the tally. Those who came to assist him understood that they might hold the ruler or sharpen the quill, but the purse remained behind his latch.

On the third Thursday of the month, the four other witnesses joined him at the slate: an elder surveyor who knew the acreage of the valley, a limner who measured curves and shadows, an artillery captain who knew how stones fall, and an envoy from the lower river. When a decree was set before them, each man approached in turn. None signed for his neighbor; each laid his own brass rule upon the figures, marking only what fell under his own divider.

A clerk was summoned to the trestle concerning a boundary along the marsh. In his hands he carried ancient charters wrapped in flax and tied with unbroken cord. The magistrate asked whether the ditch belonged to the parish or the bailiff. The clerk placed the bundle upon the slate, leaving the knot untouched. "The cord is whole, Monsieur," he said. "The inner sheet has not been unfolded. I cannot state what is written within."

The magistrate inclined his head. In this chamber, to acknowledge that a page remained uninspected was held in higher esteem than the most plausible invention. With chalk, he divided the slate into three columns: beneath the cross he set what was demonstrated; beneath the bar he set what was refuted; beneath the open circle he inscribed the matter of the marsh. A claim that had not met the eye was never suffered to masquerade as truth.

Behind him stood the overseer of the mill-dam, whose sluice had given way after the autumn freshet. The water had drowned two ricks of hay in the meadow below. The man offered no excuse about rains or heavy timber. He stepped to the trestle, bowed, and said: "I failed to gauge the rot in the bottom sill." The magistrate dipped his pen, inscribed the admission without an adjective, and passed to the next matter. A fault owned in a single sentence was complete; an excuse would have prolonged an insolence.

Before noon, a tenant appeared with neighbors who swore that the former bailiff had promised them grazing on the upper common. The magistrate listened in silence, then opened the window looking out upon the orchard. A thin white fog was dissolving over the grass into dry air. "You bring me warm breath," the magistrate remarked. "Breath disappears before the sun clears the wall. If the bailiff gave you the common, show me the skin of calf or rag on which his gall was pressed. What is not fixed in writing does not exist in this estate."

At the close of the session, the surveyor examined the magistrate’s household account and pointed out an error of three sous in the carriage of timber. The magistrate did not plead the dignity of his office. He stepped down from the dais, drew three copper coins from his private purse, placed them in the coffer, and corrected the sum with his own hand. The decree that bound the scribe bound the magistrate with equal sharpness; the law admitted no exemption for the hand that held the seal.

When dusk gathered, a student from the college asked who among the five, or among the clerks who had departed, might be called the master of the house. The magistrate closed the great sheepskin volume upon the reading desk and clasped its brass hasps.

"You look at the furniture," the magistrate replied. "If fire took these chairs tonight, we would buy more from the carpenter tomorrow and nothing would alter. But if an impious hand scratched away three lines from this volume, the estate would vanish into air. We are not the estate; we are only the hands that turn the pages."
