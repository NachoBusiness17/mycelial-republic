# The Registry of the Silt Gate

The stone bureau stood at the convergence of the sea canal and the river basin, built low against the salt fog so that every vessel entering the lock passed beneath its iron grille. Inside, five writing stations faced the central lantern. Four were plain ash wood, devoid of personal trinkets, while the fifth held a sheepskin cushion and the warmth of a living man.

The master of the bureau sat at the cushioned desk with a heavy iron ring looped through his belt. When a cargo barge bumped against the timber fenders below, a bell tolled once. From the gallery, a silent scribe stepped forward, occupied the second station, and trimmed a quill. He transcribed the cargo manifest onto vellum, stamped the margin with the house signet, and immediately laid the quill upon the brass stand. He did not linger; he rose and retreated into the shadows, leaving the wood cold and vacant. When a visiting factor demanded to speak with the officer who had approved the shipment, the master pointed to the wet line of black script. "No officer lives here," the master said. "The ink upon the vellum is the only officer that governs the tide."

Later that morning, a traveler in a dyed velvet cloak entered the hall, followed by porters carrying brass navigational astrolabes. The traveler placed his palms flat upon the stone dais and proclaimed that he was the chief hydrographer who had mapped the outer reefs seven years prior, entitled by renown to pass without toll. The master did not examine the visitor’s face, nor did he touch the polished instruments. Instead, he pulled down a thick volume stitched in pigskin and ran his fingernail down the column of recorded names. "The skin of our register does not know your name," the master answered. The traveler protested, invoking his training and memory, but the wardens barred the gate. For the bureau, what was absent from the written record did not exist in the flesh.

By midday, a breathless runner burst through the door, shouting that a schooner had foundered against the northern shoals with all hands lost. The scribe at the northern station opened a clean page and ruled three distinct columns: the witnessed, the refuted, and the unexamined. As the runner continued his tale of disaster, the scribe dipped his pen and placed a single hollow circle in the third column, scattering dry sand over the stroke. "You repeat what you heard on the road," the scribe murmured. "We do not record drowned men while the water is unvisited. Silence regarding what has not been seen is our only defense against phantom ships."

In the afternoon, the scribe at the eastern station knocked against a brass lamp while tallying winter hemp. A dark pool of oil spread across the page, blotting out six lines of grain totals. The master walked to the rail, looking down at the stain. The scribe offered no excuses about the dimming sun or a faulty hinge. He met the master’s gaze and stated: "I struck the lamp with my elbow and ruined six entries of barley." The master nodded, marked a red cross beside the oil, and handed him a scraper to prepare a fresh leaf. A fault acknowledged in a single breath required no punishment, only rectification.

A foreign trader arrived before sunset, seeking exemption from harbor dues by reciting an agreement he claimed to have struck with a magistrate in a waterfront tavern. He offered gold coins and swore upon his ancestors that his exemption was authentic. The registrar did not touch the gold. He pointed to an upright iron spike fixed beside the wicket, upon which dozens of stamped papers were impaled through their centers. "Spoken promises are vapor carried away by the river," the registrar told him. "Only what can be impaled upon this spike has standing within these walls."

As dusk settled, the master himself took a quill to record three barrels of salt fish consigned to his own household. In his haste, he neglected to note the wharf where the casks had been unladen. Before he could close the book, the scribe at the nearest ash station reached out and placed two fingers firmly against the page. "The entry is incomplete," the clerk said softly. "The origin is absent." The master looked at the clerk, paused, and without a word took up the pen and wrote the missing wharf into the blank space. The rule that ordered the bureau held no shelter for the one who governed it.

When night fell, the five stations gathered to audit the harbor accounts. The four scribes presented a flawless plan to dredge the silt bar, having calculated every angle and grain of sand. Yet none among them reached for the iron chest in the alcove. The master alone held the heavy ring of brass keys at his belt, and only his hand could disburse the silver coin to buy timber. The council could measure the kingdom's water, but only the living keeper unlocked the vault.
