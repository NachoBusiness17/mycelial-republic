# The Weir and the Parchment

The great weir at Blackwater was not built to teach the river how to flow. The water had its own ancient appetite for the sea, and the timber flumes merely gave that hunger a trough to turn the stones. Master Foster, the warden of the weir, knew that men were no different from the torrent: they did not haul grain because they loved the guild, but because their bellies cried for porridge and their children shivered when the frost set into the thatch. If an estate ran against the grain of what men hungered for, its fences rotted and its bailiffs were tossed into the ditch. To govern was simply to lay a channel where the want was already surging.

The warden kept the iron ring at his own belt. When the timber gates needed swinging or the grain-chest unlocked, only his key entered the wards, and when silver pence were weighed out for iron hoops or winter hay, his own thumb pressed the latch of the copper purse. Others could shout or plead, but without his copper and iron, no hinge creaked and no cart rolled through the portal.

At the hearth in the stone hall, five men gathered when the bell struck compline: Foster himself, holding the freehold; an old wool-trader smelling of tallow who knew the price of salt in three ports; a carver who examined the grain of every ash plank through a curved lens of glass; a retired master of ordnance whose eye measured the strength of the river wall; and a lean courier with road-dust clinging to his leather leggings. They argued fiercely over the height of the spillway, yet they knew that without the warden's hearth to warm their bones and his timber under their boots, their council was only breath upon a windowpane.

Two companions tended the warden from sunrise to the dousing of the tallow. One was a heavy, broad-shouldered clerk who never stirred from the high oak writing-desk beside the chimney, laboring night after night across calfskin rolls to digest the tallies of timber and oats. The other was a quick-footed boy who lived in the deep pocket of the warden's wool coat, whispering how many inches the flood had risen whenever Foster walked the slippery embankment.

Outside the chamber stood three high wooden chairs before an open trestle. When a wagon arrived laden with sacks, a traveler was whistled in from the road, handed a brush, and set to work. The instant the last sack was stowed, the traveler was paid his broth and sent on his way, leaving the chair vacant and cold before the bowl had dried. When a traveling scribe boasted that the brilliance of the harvest roll sprang from his own unique mind, Foster held the calfskin to the candle. "The skin remembers the weight of the grain," Foster said. "It does not remember your face, nor your blood, nor the clay of your hands. At dawn another traveler will hold the brush, yet the estate will know its wealth by the black ink that remains."

Across the margin of the parchment, the house permitted only three marks: a round eye for what two men had seen with their own eyes, a crossed knife where two carters told opposing tales, and a hollow circle wherever no man had yet looked. When a carter swore the lower bridge had collapsed, the clerk dipped his quill to mark the eye. Foster caught his fingers, asking if he had walked to the abutment himself; hearing that it was only an alehouse tale, the warden drew the hollow circle instead.

When a messenger brought a bundle bound in green twine, the wool-trader urged Foster to declare the southern grain prices. Foster held up the bundle with its wax seal untouched. "The wax is whole," he replied. "I have not broken the twine, and I will not pretend to know what is hidden inside." The council nodded, for in that valley an admitted ignorance was received with honor, while an invented claim was punished as fraud.

When the boy tumbled from the coat pocket and shattered an oil jar across the floor, he did not weave excuses of poor footing or gloom. He stood straight and said: "My fingers were careless and I dropped the jar." Hearing the single sentence, Foster gave him a cloth and spoke no reprimand.

A waterman demanded twenty shillings because a former bailiff had shouted a promise across the flood. Foster pointed to the dried oak board above the scales. "A voice vanishes when the wind shifts," he told him. "What is carved into the board stays dry through the winter. If the shillings are not cut into the wood, the river has swallowed them."

Late that night, Foster reached into the timber chest to take three copper coins for pipe-weed. The wool-trader caught the warden's wrist and pointed to the frame beside the hearth. Foster did not invoke his mastery. He returned the coppers, took up the iron chisel, and carved his purchase into the oak beam beside the carters' names, submitting to the very measure he had set upon the valley.
