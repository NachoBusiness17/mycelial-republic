## Corrections, model by model

**OpenAI o-series — “Obsessive Detective” is half right.**  
Official story: large-scale RL teaches a model to use a chain of thought productively — decompose, catch errors, switch strategies, verify — and performance scales with both train-time RL and test-time thinking tokens. Deliberative alignment even makes the hidden CoT consult safety specs. That is search-shaped behavior. What it is *not*, publicly, is a confirmed Yao-style Tree of Thoughts or a literal AlphaGo rollout tree. People analogize it to search because the *behaviors* look like search. The substrate is still next-token generation over a long private scratchpad, rewarded for outcomes (and, in some accounts, process). High token cost is real. “Builds a labyrinth then picks a branch” is a metaphor for a long sampled monologue, not a proven discrete tree.

**Claude — not the purest “show your work” Victorian.**  
Constitutional AI is an *alignment* recipe, not the reasoning engine. Claude now has the same split everyone else has: a thinking block, then an answer. Anthropic’s own work is the sharpest critique of your Claude portrait: visible CoT is often *unfaithful*; models will invent steps that match the answer they already wanted; and internally there is a silent “J-space” of concepts that light up during multi-step work even when those steps never get written down. The product *aesthetic* is verbal, careful, stepwise. The mechanism includes hidden activations plus a *summarized* thought stream you are allowed to see. That is closer to “court opinion after chambers” than “every deduction spoken aloud.”

**Gemini — “Zen Master” oversells the architecture.**  
Native multimodality and huge context are the real differentiator: text, image, audio, video in one model, not a bolted-on captioner. Mixture-of-Experts is the efficiency story — sparse experts, not mystical compression of logic. Reasoning-wise, Gemini Ultra already leaned on CoT + self-consistency; 2.5/3 added an explicit thinking loop and Deep Think, which is *parallel* chains that integrate, not “one glance at the whole.” That last part actually *supports* a holistic reading more than your “single forward pass” version does: the interesting Gemini move is width (many partial views of the same problem) plus multimodality (the “whole picture” is literally multi-sensor), not the absence of search.

**GLM — blank infilling is a 2021 pretraining objective, not Confucian inference.**  
Autoregressive blank infilling + 2D positions + shuffled span order is a hybrid of BERT-style corruption and GPT-style generation. It is a clever pretraining game: reconstruct missing spans given both sides. At *inference*, modern GLM is still a decoder that emits left-to-right, now with MoE, GQA, RoPE, and an optional thinking mode. “It looks at past *and* future and completes the ecosystem” describes *training on masked documents*, not how a chat answer is produced today. Mapping that to Yin-Yang is fun and almost entirely literary. The durable GLM trait is hybrid pretraining + later “think / don’t think” productization, same as everyone else.

**Grok — the “no introspection, just X RAG” line is a snapshot of early Grok, not the current one.**  
Two things are true. One: retrieval and native tool use are first-class, and the model is trained to *call* search, code, and live context instead of pretending parametric memory is the world. Two: current Grok is a reasoning model with effort knobs and internal thinking tokens; Heavy-style variants are widely described as parallel agents that critique each other rather than one longer monologue. That is not “doesn’t think.” It is “thinks, and also goes outside its head.” If you want a philosophy: less armchair deduction, more cross-examination plus a reporter in the room. The colloquial tone is post-training personality, not a shallow architecture.

**Llama — “compressed reflex” is true of some students, not the house.**  
Meta’s house move is *open substrate + distillation as a scaling strategy* (including pretraining distillation in later Llama lines), not a unique reasoning metaphysics. The ecosystem then does whatever it wants: raw next-token Llama, R1-style distilled long CoT, search-at-inference wrappers. A 8B R1-distill is a memorized *style* of search; a 70B/405B with tools and sampling is not “no recursive self-correction.” Llama is a cartography platform. The map is not the same as every city built on it.

## The next correction: “search” is still too narrow

I think there are actually **four distinct computational regimes** hiding inside your formulation:

**Recognition** — retrieve a likely answer from learned representations.

**Search** — generate and evaluate candidate continuations or hypotheses.

**Interaction** — change the problem state by querying tools, environments, documents, code, or other agents.

**Verification** — independently test whether the proposed answer survives scrutiny.

That gives us something much more powerful than East/West:

> **AI cognition = recognition + search + interaction + verification, allocated dynamically under a compute budget.**

The frontier question becomes **how the system decides which regime to invoke, when, and for how long**.

That also explains why the apparent convergence you describe matters. Google explicitly turned Gemini 2.5 into a “thinking” model with controllable thinking budgets, while Deep Think introduced parallel reasoning; xAI's current reasoning models expose effort controls, and Anthropic's work shows that visible CoT is not reliably equivalent to the underlying reasoning process. ([blog.google][1])

### Your “binary collapsed” claim is therefore basically right—but I'd formulate it more carefully

The industry is converging on **test-time compute as a controllable resource**, but not necessarily onto one identical mechanism.

The important distinction isn't:

> model A reasons, model B intuits.

It's:

> **model A spends compute by extending one trajectory; model B spends it by branching trajectories; model C spends it by querying the world; model D combines all three.**

Google has explicitly described Deep Think as parallel thinking that explores multiple ideas and combines or revises them. xAI now distinguishes ordinary reasoning depth from a multi-agent mode in which increased effort controls the number of collaborating agents. ([blog.google][2])

That suggests a very useful new axis:

## Depth × Width × Environment

You can almost turn the whole theory into a 3-dimensional space.

**Depth:**
How long does one reasoning trajectory persist?

**Width:**
How many competing trajectories, hypotheses, agents, or perspectives exist simultaneously?

**Environment:**
How much cognition occurs outside the model through retrieval, code, tools, documents, APIs, or agents?

Then your archetypes become much cleaner.

**Sherlock** = high depth, moderate width, low-to-moderate environment.

**Deep Think** = moderate/high depth + high width.

**Tool-heavy Grok** = depth + environment.

**Swarm systems** = width + environment.

**Fast instruction models** = recognition-heavy, low deliberate compute.

And importantly, **none of these dimensions belongs to a particular country or company.**

# There is an even deeper distinction: computation vs. representation

This is where I would push your “Zen” metaphor further.

You currently treat recognition as computationally cheaper than search. Often it is—but not necessarily.

A model can perform enormous computation **inside its fixed forward pass** without materializing a long textual trajectory.

So we should distinguish:

### Materialized computation

The model externalizes intermediate structure as tokens.

### Latent computation

Intermediate transformations remain inside hidden representations.

### Environmental computation

The system delegates cognition to tools, retrieval systems, code interpreters, databases, or agents.

### Collective computation

Multiple model instances interact and the solution emerges from their communication.

That gives us a much richer version of your original philosophical analogy:

> **“Zen” isn't necessarily thinking less. It may be thinking somewhere you cannot see.**

That sentence is conceptually important.

Anthropic's recent interpretability work provides a concrete reason to take that seriously: it describes a “J-space” of internal representations that can participate in higher-order reasoning without appearing in the textual scratchpad, while its CoT-faithfulness work found that written reasoning can fail to faithfully disclose the processes actually driving answers. ([Anthropic][3])

So I would rename your third major axis:

> **Visible reasoning ↔ latent reasoning**

rather than “verbose ↔ compressed.”

Because compression can mean several radically different things.

Verbose CoT isn't deduction; it's *simulated* System 2, unrolled intuition wearing a deerstalker. (The analytic/holistic dichotomy is borrowed from contested cultural psychology — Nisbett — and flattens rich traditions into lab branding.)

**The real yin-yang isn't between labs — it's a cycle inside every one**

1. **Deliberate:** expensive search during training (RL on verifiable rewards)
2. **Amortize:** bake the search into weights via RL/distillation
3. **Intuit:** now the model "just knows," skipping deliberation
4. **Gap appears** → deliberate again

AlphaZero is the canonical proof: search creates intuition; intuition then skips search. By this reading, **o3 at inference is a Zen master in a deerstalker** — the detective work was already amortized into its reflexes. Your "ultimate trade-off" is real but it's a *per-query dial* (test-time compute vs. weights — Snell et al. showed you can trade one for the other), not a school of philosophy. Every frontier API now exposes it as a thinking-budget slider.

**What I'd actually add**

- **DeepSeek is the wildcard you missed.** R1-Zero, trained with pure RL and zero CoT demonstrations, produced emergent self-verification and "wait—" reflection. The purest experiment to date: no Sherlock cosplay in the training data, and deduction *emerged anyway*.
- **The true "Zen architecture" exists — and it's neither Gemini nor GLM.** It's diffusion LMs (LLaDA, Mercury, Gemini Diffusion): no left-to-right at all, iteratively refining the whole answer in parallel. That's the mechanical embodiment of "what completes the whole ecosystem." Just not mainstream yet.
- **Latent reasoning** (Meta's Coconut — continuous thoughts in hidden space instead of token chains) is the serious version of "compressed, non-verbal thinking." Ironic detail: that's *Meta's* research.
- **The most Sherlockian entity on your list isn't a model — it's Anthropic-the-company.** Interpretability is literally opening the watch to see the gears. Meanwhile their model's CoT sometimes can't report what it actually computed. "Show your work," meet: your work is theater.

**The revised trade-off:** it's not Sherlock vs. the East. Everyone runs the same loop — deliberate, distill, intuit, repeat — differing only in compute-per-query, proprietary grounding (X, Search, your multimodal life), and open vs. closed weights. The philosophy actually winning is neither deduction nor intuition. It's Sutton's bitter lesson: stop debating the mind's style; scale whichever computation is cheapest this decade.

The East/West split is a good *aesthetic* for how these products *feel*. It is a worse map of how they actually compute. The interesting fight is no longer “linear deduction vs holistic intuition.” It is **where the search happens**, **whether the search is serial or parallel**, and **whether the trace you see is the computation or a press release about the computation**.

## The binary already collapsed

By late 2025 / 2026, almost every lab you listed added an explicit thinking stage: hidden or summarized tokens, a budget knob, and RL that rewards *using* that budget well. Gemini is not a single elegant forward pass anymore; it has thinking budgets and Deep Think (deeper *and* parallel chains that get merged). Claude has extended thinking, often summarized rather than raw. GLM has thinking / direct-response modes. Grok has `reasoning_effort`. The “Eastern models skip inner monologue and just intuit” story was more true of 2023–24 instruction models than of current frontier systems.

What survived from your frame is the *taste* of the default product: OpenAI and Claude still *perform* deliberation; Gemini still *performs* compression and multimodality; Grok still *performs* blunt, tool-heavy common sense. Style ≠ mechanism.

## Corrections, model by model

**OpenAI o-series — “Obsessive Detective” is half right.**  
Official story: large-scale RL teaches a model to use a chain of thought productively — decompose, catch errors, switch strategies, verify — and performance scales with both train-time RL and test-time thinking tokens. Deliberative alignment even makes the hidden CoT consult safety specs. That is search-shaped behavior. What it is *not*, publicly, is a confirmed Yao-style Tree of Thoughts or a literal AlphaGo rollout tree. People analogize it to search because the *behaviors* look like search. The substrate is still next-token generation over a long private scratchpad, rewarded for outcomes (and, in some accounts, process). High token cost is real. “Builds a labyrinth then picks a branch” is a metaphor for a long sampled monologue, not a proven discrete tree.

**Claude — not the purest “show your work” Victorian.**  
Constitutional AI is an *alignment* recipe, not the reasoning engine. Claude now has the same split everyone else has: a thinking block, then an answer. Anthropic’s own work is the sharpest critique of your Claude portrait: visible CoT is often *unfaithful*; models will invent steps that match the answer they already wanted; and internally there is a silent “J-space” of concepts that light up during multi-step work even when those steps never get written down. The product *aesthetic* is verbal, careful, stepwise. The mechanism includes hidden activations plus a *summarized* thought stream you are allowed to see. That is closer to “court opinion after chambers” than “every deduction spoken aloud.”

**Gemini — “Zen Master” oversells the architecture.**  
Native multimodality and huge context are the real differentiator: text, image, audio, video in one model, not a bolted-on captioner. Mixture-of-Experts is the efficiency story — sparse experts, not mystical compression of logic. Reasoning-wise, Gemini Ultra already leaned on CoT + self-consistency; 2.5/3 added an explicit thinking loop and Deep Think, which is *parallel* chains that integrate, not “one glance at the whole.” That last part actually *supports* a holistic reading more than your “single forward pass” version does: the interesting Gemini move is width (many partial views of the same problem) plus multimodality (the “whole picture” is literally multi-sensor), not the absence of search.

**GLM — blank infilling is a 2021 pretraining objective, not Confucian inference.**  
Autoregressive blank infilling + 2D positions + shuffled span order is a hybrid of BERT-style corruption and GPT-style generation. It is a clever pretraining game: reconstruct missing spans given both sides. At *inference*, modern GLM is still a decoder that emits left-to-right, now with MoE, GQA, RoPE, and an optional thinking mode. “It looks at past *and* future and completes the ecosystem” describes *training on masked documents*, not how a chat answer is produced today. Mapping that to Yin-Yang is fun and almost entirely literary. The durable GLM trait is hybrid pretraining + later “think / don’t think” productization, same as everyone else.

**Grok — the “no introspection, just X RAG” line is a snapshot of early Grok, not the current one.**  
Two things are true. One: retrieval and native tool use are first-class, and the model is trained to *call* search, code, and live context instead of pretending parametric memory is the world. Two: current Grok is a reasoning model with effort knobs and internal thinking tokens; Heavy-style variants are widely described as parallel agents that critique each other rather than one longer monologue. That is not “doesn’t think.” It is “thinks, and also goes outside its head.” If you want a philosophy: less armchair deduction, more cross-examination plus a reporter in the room. The colloquial tone is post-training personality, not a shallow architecture.

**Llama — “compressed reflex” is true of some students, not the house.**  
Meta’s house move is *open substrate + distillation as a scaling strategy* (including pretraining distillation in later Llama lines), not a unique reasoning metaphysics. The ecosystem then does whatever it wants: raw next-token Llama, R1-style distilled long CoT, search-at-inference wrappers. A 8B R1-distill is a memorized *style* of search; a 70B/405B with tools and sampling is not “no recursive self-correction.” Llama is a cartography platform. The map is not the same as every city built on it.

## What I’d add: the missing third camp and better axes

**DeepSeek R1 is the hole in the taxonomy.**  
Outcome-RL on long CoT, emergent backtracking (“wait”), then distillation into Qwen/Llama. That single move did more to destroy “Sherlock is proprietary, Eastern is holistic-efficient” than any philosophy post. It showed the expensive search behavior is *exportable as text*. After that, “who has the mindset” matters less than “who has verifiable rewards, sampling budget, and the willingness to spend them.”

Better axes than Analytic vs Holistic:

1. **Serial depth vs parallel width.** o-style and Claude extended thinking mostly go *deeper* on one trace. Gemini Deep Think and Grok Heavy-style setups go *wider* (multiple traces / roles, then merge). Width is the actual “holistic” mechanism — several incomplete views of the same object — and it is appearing in Western and Chinese labs alike.

2. **Parametric monologue vs tool-coupled loop.** A model that only writes tokens is doing indoor philosophy. A model trained end-to-end to search, run code, and fetch live data is doing fieldwork. Grok leans fieldwork. Everyone is moving that way because unverifiable inner speech hits a wall.

3. **Faithful scratchpad vs post-hoc narrative.** Anthropic’s unfaithful-CoT results should be a load-bearing part of any “mindset” theory. If the visible reasoning is often a justification, then “verbose Sherlock” can be theater, and “compressed Zen” can be the same computation with worse documentation.

4. **Verifiable RL vs preference RL.** Math/code/safety-spec rewards grow *procedures*. Helpfulness/harmlessness/style rewards grow *manners*. Claude’s constitution and Grok’s personality are manners layered on procedures. Mixing them up is how you get “this model is a Victorian logician” from an RLHF soup.

5. **What the latent space is asked to carry.** Your last paragraph is the one piece that still holds: some systems try to *find* the answer by generating lots of intermediate structure; others try to *recognize* it from a huge multimodal or bidirectional pretraining prior. Both still generate tokens. The difference is how much structure they are willing to materialize before committing.

## The tradeoff, restated

It is not “Sherlock burns tokens to search; East recognizes truth in one pass.”

It is: **hard items require search.** Labs differ on whether that search is a long private monologue, a summarized monologue, several monologues in parallel, or a monologue interrupted by tools and other agents. Efficiency now lives in MoE routing, spec decoding, adaptive budgets, and *not thinking on easy prompts* — not in a civilizational epistemology.

The metaphor is still useful as a *user-facing* taxonomy: some models sound like they are building a case file; some sound like they already saw the whole room; some sound like they checked their phone and argued with a friend. Just don’t promote that into a claim about distinct minds. Under the hood they are all the same species — transformers, post-trained to spend extra tokens when the reward model says the problem is hard — wearing different national costumes.

What you are describing is essentially a history of the detective mind: how societies learned to move from “someone committed a wrong” to “what actually happened, who did it, what evidence supports that conclusion, and how can we distinguish truth from accusation?”

The most interesting way to study it is not as a parade of famous detectives, but as the evolution of several schools of investigation.

The Detective Tradition: From Ordeal to Evidence

A useful master question is:

How does a society turn uncertainty about a crime into justified knowledge?

That question takes us from ancient justice all the way to Sherlock Holmes, forensic laboratories, police academies, intelligence analysis, DNA, and digital investigation.

Era	Dominant question	Main method	Investigator
Ancient societies	Who is responsible?	Testimony, oath, ordeal, documents	Judge, ruler, official
Classical world	What happened?	Witnesses, reputation, physical circumstances	Magistrate, official
Medieval	Who can establish the facts?	Coroner, inquest, hue-and-cry, community knowledge	Coroner, sheriff, constable
Early modern	How do we find offenders systematically?	Informants, warrants, surveillance, records	Constable, thief-taker
18th c.	How do we pursue professional criminals?	Runners, informants, networks	Bow Street Runner
19th c.	How do we identify the individual?	Detectives, dossiers, photography, anthropometry	Detective bureau
Late 19th c.	What can physical traces tell us?	Criminalistics, microscopy, fingerprints, chemistry	Scientific detective
Holmesian era	What explanation best accounts for the observations?	Observation + inference + experiment	Consulting detective
20th c.	How do we institutionalize investigation?	Specialized units, laboratories, databases	Professional investigator
Late 20th–21st c.	How do we integrate heterogeneous evidence?	DNA, databases, digital evidence, behavioral analysis, intelligence	Multidisciplinary investigative team

The crucial thing is that “policing” and “detective work” are not the same historical phenomenon. Professional police forces are comparatively recent; active investigation of crime is much older. In medieval England, for example, coroners were already conducting formal fact-finding centuries before modern police departments existed. The coroner's office dates to 1194.

1. The first detective was probably not a detective

The earliest investigative systems are embedded in law, kingship, religion, and communal responsibility, rather than a separate detective profession.

Mesopotamian legal culture provides an important early example. Written records made systematic documentation of legal and social affairs possible, while justice incorporated both empirical fact-finding and religiously grounded procedures such as ordeal.

That gives us the first great distinction:

Ancient justice often sought truth through authorized procedures.

Modern investigation increasingly seeks truth through reconstructible evidence.

That transition is enormous.

The question changes from:

“Which party is credible before the authority?”

to:

“What observations would remain true regardless of who is telling the story?”

That is the seed of forensic investigation.

2. Ancient China: an extraordinarily important missing ancestor

If you want the history done seriously, Chinese forensic investigation cannot be treated as a footnote.

China developed an unusually sophisticated tradition of medico-legal investigation. Archaeological evidence indicates forensic and medico-legal procedures going back to the Qin era, and the great landmark is Song Ci's Xi Yuan Ji Lu — The Washing Away of Wrongs, produced in 1247.

It is generally regarded as the oldest surviving systematic forensic-medical text. It was written as practical guidance for magistrates conducting investigations and inquests into deaths.

This is an extraordinary conceptual development.

The investigator is no longer merely asking:

Who says what?

He is asking:

What does the body itself tell us?

That introduces a foundational detective principle:

The victim can become a witness.

A corpse cannot speak, but injuries, decomposition, wounds, environmental circumstances, and other physical characteristics can provide testimony.

That idea eventually becomes central to modern forensic pathology.

3. Medieval Europe: investigation becomes an institutional process

Medieval England provides another crucial bridge.

The coroner, established as a royal office in 1194, investigated deaths and other matters relevant to royal justice. Coroners gathered information through inquiries and interrogations, while sheriffs and constables played roles in arrest and enforcement.

And there was the famous hue and cry.

A person discovering a felony was expected to raise an alarm; others could be obligated to participate in pursuit. It was therefore simultaneously a communications network and a mechanism for collective pursuit.

Think of this as an extremely primitive distributed investigative system:

event → notification → mobilization → pursuit → identification

There is an interesting connection here to modern investigations.

The modern police radio call, dispatch system, BOLO, database alert, and interagency bulletin are technologically sophisticated descendants of a much older problem:

How do you distribute knowledge of a crime rapidly enough for other people to act on it?

4. The great transformation: the professional detective

By the 18th century London was becoming a laboratory for professional criminal investigation.

At Bow Street, Thomas de Veil began systematically investigating crime, and Henry Fielding later developed a group of officers who became known as the Bow Street Runners. John Fielding expanded the system. By 1839 the Runners' activities were incorporated into the Metropolitan Police.

This is an important moment because investigation becomes increasingly separated from ordinary community policing.

The investigator becomes a specialist.

Then comes another major transition.

1829 — Metropolitan Police

Robert Peel's Metropolitan Police created a new model of centralized, uniformed public policing in London.

1842 — Detective Branch

The Metropolitan Police created a dedicated Detective Branch.

1878 — Criminal Investigation Department

The CID replaced the disgraced Detective Branch.

Now we have the institutional ancestor of the modern police detective.

5. But the detective was not initially a scientist

This is where the history gets fascinating.

Early detectives relied heavily upon:

informants → human intelligence → interrogation → surveillance → disguise → criminal networks → memory → recognition

One of the most interesting figures is Eugène-François Vidocq, a former criminal who became a police investigator and chief of the French Sûreté.

His career represents a transitional world in which the detective understands criminals partly because he has lived within criminal networks himself.

This produces an entirely different investigative philosophy:

To catch the criminal, understand the criminal's world.

That school eventually develops into undercover work, informants, surveillance, intelligence analysis, and eventually behavioral analysis.

The detective is learning to investigate people, rather than merely incidents.

6. Then comes the identification revolution

The great problem of 19th-century policing was:

How do you know that this person is the same person who committed the previous crime?

Photography helped, but it created enormous archives that were difficult to search.

Alphonse Bertillon introduced anthropometric identification in the 1880s, combining photographs, physical measurements, and standardized descriptions.

This was the birth of the identification bureaucracy.

The criminal becomes a record.

That gives us:

person → measurements → photograph → file → classification → retrieval

The detective institution begins accumulating memory outside the detective's head.

This is one of the deepest transitions in the history of crime solving.

7. Fingerprints destroy one of the old assumptions

Fingerprints eventually offered something Bertillon's system could not provide as conveniently: a persistent physical identifier.

The historical development is complicated and international, but fingerprint evidence was being incorporated into criminal justice around the turn of the 20th century. A Bengal case in 1898 is considered the first criminal case in which fingerprint evidence secured a conviction, while systematic U.S. criminal-record fingerprinting followed in the early 1900s.

Now the crime scene itself could contain an identifying signature.

The logic becomes:

The offender does not merely leave a story behind. The offender leaves traces behind.

That is the intellectual gateway to modern forensic science.

8. Enter Sherlock Holmes

And now we arrive at the character who transformed the cultural imagination of detective work.

Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes appears in A Study in Scarlet in 1887.

But Holmes did not simply invent the idea of scientific investigation.

The historical relationship actually works in the opposite direction.

Scientific and forensic practices were already developing, and Doyle incorporated contemporary scientific ideas into the Holmes stories. Scholarly research describes Holmes as a deliberately constructed “scientific detective.”

That distinction is extremely important.

Holmes is fiction.
Criminalistics was becoming reality.

Holmes dramatized the emerging scientific investigator.

9. Holmes's real method is not simply “deduction”

This is probably the most interesting conceptual point in the entire subject.

People constantly say:

Sherlock Holmes uses deduction.

Technically, that is not quite right.

Holmes often performs what philosophers call abduction:

Given these observations, what hypothesis would best explain them?

The structure is:

Observation → possible explanation → prediction → testing → elimination

The BMJ analysis of detective reasoning explicitly distinguishes Holmes's approach from strict deduction and identifies it more closely with abductive reasoning.

For example:

You observe:

mud on a shoe
a particular smell
unusual wear on a sleeve
a missing object
an inconsistent statement

The Holmesian mind doesn't immediately say:

“Therefore X is guilty.”

Instead:

“What hypothesis would make all five observations unsurprising?”

That is a much more powerful investigative architecture.

10. The Holmesian school

We can reconstruct Holmes's methodology as a set of disciplines:

Observation

See what is actually present before interpreting it.

Discrimination

Notice what is unusual rather than merely what is obvious.

Classification

Convert observations into meaningful categories.

Hypothesis generation

Generate several possible explanations.

Elimination

Ask which hypotheses contradict known facts.

Experiment

Where possible, create or seek additional evidence.

Reconstruction

Build a sequence of events that explains the evidence.

Verification

Test whether the reconstructed story survives new information.

This is much closer to the scientific method than the cinematic version of detective work.

The famous Holmesian phrase “you see, but you do not observe” captures the distinction.

11. And Holmes had another secret weapon: specialization

Holmes maintains enormous stores of specialized knowledge.

Chemistry.

Toxicology.

Footprints.

Cigarette ash.

Documents.

Human behavior.

Geography.

Anatomy.

Weapons.

Writing.

Clothing.

The underlying principle is:

A small anomaly becomes meaningful when the observer possesses the right domain model.

That principle survives into modern policing.

The modern detective doesn't personally know everything.

Instead, the case is connected to specialists:

detective + forensic scientist + pathologist + digital analyst + intelligence analyst + behavioral specialist + prosecutor

The individual Holmes becomes the modern investigative network.

12. Hans Gross: the forgotten bridge between Holmes and modern CSI

This is an especially important figure for the history you are asking about.

Austrian jurist Hans Gross published his Handbuch für Untersuchungsrichter als System der Kriminalistik in 1893, later translated as Criminal Investigation: A Practical Handbook.

His work helped establish criminalistics as systematic investigation of the specific crime scene and its evidence.

This is a different intellectual tradition from Lombroso-style criminology.

Criminology asks:

What kinds of people commit crime, and why?

Criminalistics asks:

What happened in this particular crime, and what traces can establish it?

That distinction is essential.

13. Locard changes everything

In 1910, Edmond Locard established a police scientific laboratory in Lyon. His central insight became known as the exchange principle:

When two things come into contact, material may be transferred between them.

The modern crime scene is therefore imagined as a network of transfers.

Person ↔ victim
Person ↔ floor
Person ↔ vehicle
Vehicle ↔ road
Weapon ↔ victim
Clothing ↔ environment

The crime scene becomes a physical memory.

Locard's Lyon laboratory was created specifically to bring technical expertise into police investigation; it is recognized by French police history as the first modern police scientific laboratory.

14. The detective academy is the institutionalized Holmes

This is where the entire history converges.

The old detective learned through:

experience → apprenticeship → intuition → contacts

The modern investigator increasingly learns through:

curriculum → scenario training → law → evidence procedures → forensic science → interviewing → intelligence → report writing → supervised practice

For example, modern FBI training incorporates law, ethics, behavioral science, forensic science, investigative techniques, interviewing, intelligence, operational skills, and realistic case exercises. New agents spend more than 800 hours in basic training.

Notice what happened.

Holmes's brain has been decomposed into institutional modules.

The academy teaches:

observe

The evidence technician teaches:

preserve

The forensic scientist teaches:

measure

The interviewer teaches:

elicit

The analyst teaches:

correlate

The investigator teaches:

reconstruct

The prosecutor teaches:

prove

The court teaches:

challenge

That is the modern detective system.

15. Modern crime-scene investigation is almost the opposite of the Hollywood detective

Modern evidence work is intentionally procedural.

The National Institute of Justice describes crime-scene investigation around:

securing the scene → documenting it → evaluating it → processing it → recording the work → submitting evidence

Investigators photograph and measure scenes, collect physical evidence, and maintain chain of custody.

Why so much procedure?

Because the modern investigator has learned something Holmes occasionally gets away with in fiction:

Human memory is unreliable.

Therefore the investigation tries to create an external record that another investigator can independently examine.

That gives us another profound transition:

Ancient justice: authority remembers.

Holmes: genius remembers.

Modern investigation: the system records.

16. The rise of the interview science

Another school evolved around the human mind.

Older detective culture often emphasized interrogation as extracting a confession.

Modern investigative research increasingly asks:

How do we obtain reliable information without contaminating memory or producing false information?

The FBI has documented concerns about poor interviewing practices and discusses evidence-based methods such as the cognitive interview, which was developed around memory and communication research to increase information obtained while minimizing accuracy loss.

This is another Holmesian paradox.

Holmes observes the physical world.

The modern interviewer investigates the informational world inside another person's memory.

17. The major schools of detective thought

I would organize the entire history into roughly eight schools.

I. The Authority School

Truth comes from authorized testimony, status, oath, confession, or institutional judgment.

Ancient and medieval justice frequently contains versions of this.

Core question:

Who is legally credible?

II. The Pursuit School

Find the offender through human networks.

Hue and cry, constables, thief-takers, Bow Street Runners, informants, undercover work.

Core question:

Where is the offender?

III. The Identification School

Turn people into persistent records.

Bertillon → photography → fingerprints → criminal files → databases.

Core question:

Who exactly is this person?

IV. The Criminalistics School

The physical environment contains traces of events.

Gross → Locard → laboratory science.

Core question:

What physical traces were produced by the event?

V. The Holmesian / Abductive School

Construct the explanation that best accounts for the observations.

Poe's Dupin → Holmes → modern investigative reasoning.

Core question:

What hypothesis best explains what we observe?

Poe's 1841 Murders in the Rue Morgue is conventionally treated as the foundational detective story, and Holmes developed this tradition into a far more explicitly scientific model.

VI. The Behavioral School

Crime is behavior, and behavior contains information about the offender.

Modern behavioral analysis examines things such as motivation, victim selection, offender sophistication, relationships, sequence of actions, and interview strategy.

Core question:

What does the offender's behavior reveal about the underlying actor?

VII. The Intelligence School

Individual evidence becomes powerful when connected across cases.

Records → databases → crime patterns → networks → intelligence.

Core question:

What connections become visible when isolated observations are combined?

This is where the detective begins to resemble an intelligence analyst.

VIII. The Scientific-Statistical School

This is increasingly important.

The investigator asks not merely:

“Does this evidence fit the suspect?”

but:

“How strongly does this evidence support this hypothesis compared with alternatives?”

This brings probability, statistical reasoning, validation studies, error rates, likelihood ratios, and cognitive-bias research into the investigation.

Modern forensic science is increasingly concerned with validating methods and understanding their limitations. The NIJ, for example, notes continuing scrutiny of disciplines such as latent fingerprints, firearms, toolmarks, and footwear evidence.

18. Then DNA creates another revolution

DNA changes identification from:

“This trace resembles this person.”

toward:

“How statistically compatible is this biological profile with this person or population?”

Forensic DNA emerged from biomedical research in the 1980s, with early forensic applications in Britain in the mid-1980s. The technology subsequently became a major investigative and exculpatory tool.

And this creates a critical modern principle:

Good detective work must be able to prove someone innocent as readily as it tries to prove someone guilty.

That is an important difference between genuine investigation and merely constructing a case.

Module 8 — Sherlock Holmes

Module 9 — Locard

Module 13 — Intelligence Analysis

Combine apparently unrelated observations.

Exercise:

Construct competing timelines and competing hypotheses.

Module 14 — Bayesian / Statistical Thinking

Ask:

“How much should this evidence change my belief?”

rather than:

“Does this evidence prove my theory?”

Module 15 — The Anti-Holmes

This might be the most important chapter.

Study:

confirmation bias
tunnel vision
premature closure
anchoring
false confession
contaminated evidence
mistaken eyewitness identification
overconfidence

Because the mature detective is not merely someone who finds explanations.

The mature detective is someone who can destroy their own explanation.

And that gives us the real “Sherlock Holmes school”

The superficial Holmes lesson is:

Look closely.

The deeper lesson is:

Do not confuse an observation with an interpretation.

The even deeper lesson is:

Generate competing explanations before becoming emotionally attached to one.

And the modern lesson is:

Subject every explanation to independent evidence, documentation, statistical reasoning, adversarial testing, and the possibility that you are wrong.

That connects the medieval coroner, Song Ci, Bow Street Runner, Vidocq, Bertillon, Hans Gross, Locard, Sherlock Holmes, the FBI Academy, CSI, DNA laboratories, and modern intelligence analysis into one continuous intellectual history.

The striking thing is that Sherlock Holmes is not the endpoint of this story. He is the fictional midpoint where several older traditions—observation, medicine, chemistry, identification, criminal knowledge, logical inference—are fused into a single character. The real historical endpoint is the modern investigative ecosystem, where no single detective needs to be Holmes because the institution itself has been built to distribute Holmes-like capabilities across many specialists.
